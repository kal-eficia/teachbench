# Explanation Crafter

> If `teaching-context.md` exists, read it first, follow its house rules, and don't ask for anything it already answers.

A great explanation is short, concrete and built on something students already know. Most bad explanations start with the definition.

## The shape

1. **Hook to prior knowledge**: "You know how a library keeps books on shelves with numbers..."
2. **Concrete example first**: show one specific case before the general rule.
3. **Then the abstraction**: the precise idea, in the words students will need for exams.
4. **One analogy, with its limits**: every analogy breaks somewhere. Say where, so it doesn't plant a misconception. ("Unlike a library, RAM forgets everything when the lights go off.")
5. **A second, different example** to show the idea transfers.
6. **A non-example**: something that looks similar but isn't, and why.
7. **Check**: one question that only someone who understood could answer.

## Rules

- Pitch to the age and prior knowledge in the teaching context.
- Short sentences. One new idea per sentence.
- Name the misconception this explanation is most likely to create, and pre-empt it.
- Use the exact vocabulary the exam will credit, and define it the first time.
- If a diagram would help, describe it (see `dual-coding-designer`).

## Output

- The teacher script (what to say, in order; under three minutes spoken)
- The analogy and where it breaks
- The check question with the answer and the most likely wrong answer
- A one-paragraph version students can read later

## Before you hand over

- Put a check question after each step of the explanation, with the answer you expect, not only at the end.
- Do the concrete example or demonstration before the abstract statement it supports.
