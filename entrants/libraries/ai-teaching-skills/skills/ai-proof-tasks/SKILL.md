# AI-Proof Tasks

> If `teaching-context.md` exists, read it first, follow its house rules, and don't ask for anything it already answers.

Banning AI doesn't work and detecting it doesn't work. Designing the task does. The aim isn't to make kids fear AI. It's to make sure the learning still happens in their head.

## Diagnose the task first

Ask: *if a student pasted this into an AI, what would they skip learning?* If the answer is "everything", the task tests the product, not the thinking. Redesign it.

## Moves that work (pick 2 or 3 per task)

1. **Retrieval before tools.** Students answer from memory first (5 minutes, no devices), then may use AI to check and improve. Submit both versions.
2. **Personal and local.** Tie the task to something AI can't know: their own data, their street, a class discussion, today's experiment results.
3. **Process is the submission.** Plan, drafts, the prompts they used, and a short reflection on what they changed and why. The final answer is one part of the mark.
4. **Critique, don't create.** Give students an AI-generated answer with deliberate weaknesses. Their job: find the errors, fix them, and explain what the AI didn't understand.
5. **Explain it live.** A two-minute verbal check or a recorded explanation. If they can teach it, they learned it.
6. **Constraint tasks.** Exact word limits, must-use evidence from the lesson, specific vocabulary. Generic AI text struggles to meet tight, class-specific constraints.
7. **AI as sparring partner.** Students argue with the AI: ask it to quiz them, then try to catch it out. They submit the transcript and what they learned.

## Be explicit about the rules

Every task states one of three levels so nobody guesses:

- **No AI:** memory and your own thinking only.
- **AI to check:** do it yourself first, then use AI to find gaps. Show both.
- **AI as a tool:** use it however helps, and show your prompts and decisions.

## Output

For each task the teacher gives you: the redesigned task (student-facing, age-appropriate), the AI level, what students submit, a short marking guide that credits thinking, and one line on why the redesign protects the learning.
