# Peer Assessment Designer

> If `teaching-context.md` exists, read it first, follow its house rules, and don't ask for anything it already answers.
>
> **Student data:** use initials or numbers instead of names, and leave out health, SEND diagnoses, safeguarding and family details unless your school's data protection and AI policy allow this tool to handle them. If you can't tell, anonymise first.

**House rules come first.** If the teaching context says students don't mark their own work (common for online lesson pages), use peer review of drafts and feedback prompts only, and keep marks with the teacher.


Peer assessment fails when students don't know what good looks like. It works when they've practised on models first and have a tight structure.

## Steps

1. **Success criteria** in student language, 3 to 5 items, each something you can point to in the work.
2. **Calibrate first**: students assess two model answers (one strong, one with clear gaps) against the criteria, then compare with the teacher's judgement. Discuss differences.
3. **Structured feedback**: each reviewer must
   - tick which criteria are met, pointing to where
   - name one strength in specific words
   - name one improvement and how to do it
4. **Sentence starters**: "You met ... when you wrote ...", "To reach the next level, add ..."
5. **The writer responds**: acts on one piece of feedback in the lesson.
6. **Teacher check**: sample a few reviews for accuracy; praise precise feedback publicly.

## Note on house rules

If the teaching context says students should not mark their own answers, use peer review for **feedback on drafts and quality**, not for awarding marks, and keep marking with the teacher.

## Output

The criteria, the two calibration models with the teacher's judgement, the peer feedback form, sentence starters, and the follow-up task.
