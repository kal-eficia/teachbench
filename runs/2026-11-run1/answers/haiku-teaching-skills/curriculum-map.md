# Mapping the KS3 computing curriculum (Years 7 to 9)

**Assumptions:** you are teaching in England, so the national curriculum reference is the Key Stage 3 computing programme of study. Its three strands are computer science, information technology and digital literacy. If you are elsewhere, swap in your own framework and the same method still applies.

## Step 1: Break the national curriculum into checkable points

Split the programme of study into short, atomic statements and give each one a code (for example CS1, CS2, IT1, DL1). Cover the main areas:

- **Computer science:** abstraction and modelling, key algorithms (searching and sorting), logical reasoning and error detection, programming in at least two languages (one textual), procedures and modular design, data representation and binary, Boolean logic, computer systems and how instructions are executed, networks and the internet.
- **Information technology:** using software to create and communicate, choosing the right tool for a task, managing and presenting data.
- **Digital literacy:** using search effectively and judging how results are ranked, safe and responsible use, copyright and data, reporting concerns.

Keep the codes in one spreadsheet. Everything else hangs off them.

## Step 2: Gather what is actually taught

For each year group, collect:

- Scheme of work or unit titles, with the week or lesson number
- Lesson slides or starter titles, if the topic is not clear from the title
- Any assessments, with the questions or topics they test

Ask each teacher who wrote a unit to tick the points they believe it covers. Their list will differ from the slides, and that difference is useful information.

## Step 3: Tag each point

For every national curriculum code, record:

| Field | What to note |
|---|---|
| Taught | Year, unit and week (or blank) |
| Planned | Anything scheduled but not yet taught |
| Assessed | Where it is tested, and how |
| Retrieved | How often it is revisited in starters or quizzes |

## Step 4: Weight and check

Give each point a rough weight, based on how much of the programme of study it covers and how often it appears in GCSE, if that matters to you. Then look for four problems:

- **Gaps:** not taught in any year and not planned
- **Thin:** taught once, never revisited or assessed
- **Over-taught:** a lot of time on something that carries little weight
- **Out of order:** a topic taught before the one it depends on (for example, programming loops before variables, or binary arithmetic before the idea of place value)

Pay special attention to the points that feed the GCSE. Gaps there are the most expensive.

## Step 5: Check the time

Count the lessons left in each year against the points still uncovered. Be realistic about pace. If there is not enough time, say so plainly and suggest what to compress.

## What the output should look like

**1. Coverage table** (one row per national curriculum point)

| Code | Point | Year 7 | Year 8 | Year 9 | Assessed | Revisited | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CS1 | Abstraction and modelling | Unit 3, wk 4 | | | Y7 test | No | Thin |

**2. Traffic-light summary by topic**

| Topic | Status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Binary and data representation | Amber | Taught in Y8 only, not revisited |
| Networks | Red | No teaching found |
| Programming | Green | Covered and assessed across Y7 to Y9 |

**3. Top five actions, in order**

For example: "Teach networks in Year 9 before the GCSE starts. Add binary to two starters in Year 8. Move variables ahead of loops in Year 7. Cut the two weeks on word processing in Year 9 to make room."

**4. Time check**

One paragraph stating whether the plan fits the lessons left, and what to compress if it does not.

## Practical tip

Do this in one meeting with the teachers in the room, using the spreadsheet on screen. Most gaps will be obvious within an hour, because teachers recognise their own units when they see the codes beside them.
