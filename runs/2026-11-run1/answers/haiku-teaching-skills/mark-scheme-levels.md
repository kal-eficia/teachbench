# GCSE English Literature: 20-mark question and mark scheme

**Assumptions:** GCSE (9-1), AQA-style layout, 20 marks for content (AO1 to AO3). Spelling, punctuation and grammar are marked separately, as they are on the real papers. This question is original and written in the style of the board's exam, not copied from a past paper.

## Question

Read the extract from Stave Five of *A Christmas Carol* below.

> "I don't know what to do! I am so fluttered and so glad! I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel, I am as merry as a school-boy."

Starting with this extract, explain how Dickens presents Scrooge's change in *A Christmas Carol*.

Write about:
- how Dickens presents Scrooge's change in this extract
- how Dickens presents Scrooge's change across the novel as a whole

**[20 marks]**

## Mark scheme

### Levels of response

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 17-20 | Perceptive, sustained analysis of how Dickens presents the change. Judicious, well-embedded quotations. Confident analysis of how structure (the staves and the ghostly visits) shapes the change, and of the language used. Detailed, integrated context. Judges how far the change is convincing. |
| 4 | 13-16 | Detailed, exploratory understanding of the change as a process. Well-selected quotations with clear analysis of language and structure and their effects. Context is used to explain Dickens's purpose. |
| 3 | 9-12 | Clear understanding of Scrooge's change. Relevant quotations used. Some explanation of how language and structure create effects. Some relevant context. |
| 2 | 5-8 | Some understanding of Scrooge's change, mostly through narrative. Some relevant references. Comments on language or structure are mostly descriptive. Basic or unlinked context. |
| 1 | 1-4 | Simple comments on Scrooge. Limited or no use of quotations. Little or no comment on language or structure. Little or no context. |

### Indicative content

Students do not need all of these. Reward any well-argued, relevant point.

- **Stave One:** Scrooge's coldness. "Bah! Humbug!" and the narrator's list, "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!" Scrooge refuses charity: "Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses?"
- **Marley's ghost:** the warning that Scrooge's life is wasted. "Mankind was my business."
- **Ghost of Christmas Past:** Scrooge's lost youth, Fezziwig's party and Belle leaving him. Scrooge's softer feelings are shown but not yet acted on.
- **Ghost of Christmas Present:** the Cratchit family and Tiny Tim ("God bless us, every one!"). Scrooge is confronted with the consequences of his attitude.
- **Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come:** the silent, frightening visit. Scrooge sees his own grave and asks whether these are "shadows of the things that Will be". This is the turning point that makes change possible.
- **Stave Five:** the sudden joy, the generosity, and the opening of the extract. Scrooge becomes "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew".
- **Structure:** the five staves give the change a clear journey. The change is delayed until the final stave, so it feels earned, and the three ghosts let Dickens show different sides of Scrooge's life.
- **Language:** the dramatic shift in Scrooge's speech and exclamations. Repetition and similes in the extract ("as light as a feather") suggest release and innocence.
- **Context:** the 1843 publication, the Victorian treatment of the poor, the workhouse system, and the "Ignorance and Want" children from Stave Three, which show Dickens's social purpose.

## Model answer extract (top level)

> Dickens presents Scrooge's change as a moral awakening that is sudden in its feeling but carefully prepared for in the novel. In this extract, the exclamatory opening, "I don't know what to do!", shows a man overwhelmed by emotions he has refused himself for decades, and the exclamation marks mirror the rush of joy. The triple repetition of "I am as" builds a sense of release, and the similes move from the weightless, "as light as a feather", to the innocent, "as merry as a school-boy". Dickens chooses a child for the final comparison deliberately, because Scrooge has spent the whole novel ignoring children and the poor.
>
> This contrasts sharply with Stave One, where Scrooge's name is a byword for coldness and the narrator's catalogue of his grasping nature, "squeezing, wrenching, grasping", leaves the reader in no doubt about his guilt. Dickens uses the staves to make this journey feel earned rather than convenient. The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come forces Scrooge to face his own grave, and only then can he choose to "honour Christmas in my heart". Dickens's 1843 readers, many of whom had seen the workhouse system and the suffering it caused, are invited to see that the cold man of Stave One could change, and that the change is a moral duty as much as a personal happiness.
>
> The change is convincing largely because Dickens does not let it become simple. Scrooge's joy is almost childlike, and this can feel excessive, but Dickens uses that excess to show how long Scrooge has been starved of feeling. The extract therefore shows not only that Scrooge has changed, but that the change is a return to the person he once was.
