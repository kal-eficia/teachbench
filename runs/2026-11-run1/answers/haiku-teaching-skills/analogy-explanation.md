# How the immune system responds to a pathogen

*Assumptions: Year 9, about 10 to 15 minutes of explanation, no prior knowledge of antibodies beyond "the body fights germs".*

## Teacher script (under three minutes spoken)

**Start with what they know.** "You've all had a cut that got a bit red and sore, and then healed on its own. Your body was fighting something the whole time."

**Example first.** "Let's say a cold virus gets into your nose. A virus can't move around or eat. It gets inside your body cells and uses them to make copies of itself. Within a few hours there can be thousands of copies."

*Check:* "What does a virus need in order to make copies of itself?" Expected answer: a living cell (a host cell). Likely wrong answer: "food" or "air".

**Then the idea.** "Anything that causes disease is called a pathogen. Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists. Your immune system has three main ways of responding:"

1. **White blood cells called phagocytes** surround pathogens, engulf them and digest them.
2. **Lymphocytes** (another type of white blood cell) recognise one specific pathogen. **B lymphocytes** make **antibodies**. An antibody is a Y-shaped protein that fits one particular antigen, which is a marker on the pathogen's surface. Antibodies mark the pathogen so phagocytes can find it, or make the pathogens stick together.
3. **Antitoxins** are antibodies that neutralise toxins. Some bacteria don't make you ill by multiplying. They make you ill by releasing toxins, and antitoxins stop those toxins from working.

*Check:* "What is an antibody's job?" Expected answer: to bind to a specific antigen on a pathogen, marking it or neutralising it. Likely wrong answer: "to kill the pathogen by itself, like a phagocyte does".

**Memory cells.** "Some B and T lymphocytes stay in your blood after the infection is over. These are memory cells. If the same pathogen comes back, they respond much faster and in larger numbers. That is why you usually don't catch the same illness twice, and it is how vaccines work."

**Clear up the big confusion.** "Now, here's the one that catches people out. Antibiotics don't work on viruses. Antibiotics work by attacking parts of bacteria that human cells don't have, such as their cell walls. A virus isn't a bacterium. It doesn't have those parts, and it lives inside your own cells. So an antibiotic has no target in the virus, and attacking the cells the virus is hiding in would harm you. Your immune system deals with viruses instead."

*Check:* "Why won't antibiotics cure a cold?" Expected answer: a cold is caused by a virus, and antibiotics target structures in bacteria that viruses don't have. Likely wrong answer: "Antibiotics make your immune system stronger, so it can fight the virus" or "Antibiotics kill all germs."

## The analogy and where it breaks

**Analogy:** The immune system is like a city's security service. Phagocytes are patrol officers who deal with anything they find, lymphocytes are detectives who build a profile of one particular threat, antibodies are the wanted posters, and memory cells are the files kept on record for next time.

**Where it breaks:** Real security services can recruit and train new officers in an emergency. The body has to make the right antibodies after it meets the pathogen, which takes days. That delay is why you feel ill for a while the first time, and why memory cells matter so much.

## A second example (transfer)

Tetanus is caused by bacteria that release a toxin. Your body makes antitoxins against that toxin. The bacteria are not the main danger; the toxin is. The same ideas apply: a specific antibody-type response, and memory cells that respond faster next time.

## A non-example

Antibiotics look like they should work against any germ, but they only work on bacteria. The "germ" in a cold is a virus, and the antibiotic has nothing to attack.

## A paragraph students can read later

Your immune system defends you against pathogens, which are things like bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists. White blood cells called phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens. Lymphocytes make antibodies, which are proteins that fit one specific antigen on a pathogen, marking it for destruction. Antitoxins are antibodies that neutralise the toxins some bacteria release. After an infection, memory cells remain in the blood, so the body responds faster if the same pathogen returns. Antibiotics only work on bacteria, not viruses, because they target parts of bacteria that viruses don't have.
