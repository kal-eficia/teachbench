# Why we have seasons: explanation for Year 7 (age 11 to 12)

**Assumptions:** one lesson of about 50 minutes, UK class, students know that the Earth goes round the Sun once a year and that the Earth spins once a day. You need a torch, a sheet of paper, a ruler and a globe or a ball with a stick through it for the tilt.

## Teacher script (under three minutes spoken, plus the demonstration)

**Step 1: hook, and find out what they think.**
"Quick question. If you stand near a fire, is it hotter than standing a few metres away? Yes. So it seems sensible that summer happens when the Earth is closer to the Sun. Let's test that. Hands up if you think we're closest to the Sun in July."
*Check:* count the hands. Most will go up. Then say: "We're actually closest in early January. Stay with me, because that's the clue."

**Step 2: demonstration with the torch.**
Shine the torch straight down onto the paper. Draw round the bright circle. Now tilt the torch so the beam hits the paper at a slant. Draw round the new shape.
"Same torch, same distance from the paper. Which patch looks brighter?"
*Expected answer:* the straight-down patch is smaller and brighter. The slanted one is bigger and dimmer.
*If students are unsure:* ask them to put their hand flat in the light, then tilt it, and say what changes.

**Step 3: the rule.**
"Sunlight that hits the ground straight on is concentrated into a small patch. Sunlight that hits at a slant is spread over a bigger patch, so each part gets less. The Earth is tilted at about 23.5 degrees. In June, the northern half of the Earth leans towards the Sun. Sunlight hits it more directly, and the days are longer. So that half gets more energy."
*Check:* "In June, is the top half of the Earth tilted towards the Sun or away from it?"
*Expected answer:* towards.

**Step 4: the analogy, and where it breaks.**
"Think of the torch as the Sun and the beam as sunlight. A straight beam makes a bright spot. A slanted beam makes a dim, wide spot. The limit: the Sun is huge and very far away, so its sunlight reaches us almost parallel, and the torch doesn't spin or tilt over a year."

**Step 5: the non-example, which kills the misconception.**
"The Earth is about 147 million kilometres from the Sun in January. In July it is about 152 million kilometres away. That's only about 3% further, which is too small to make the difference between summer and winter. Here's the proof: when it's summer in the UK, it's winter in Australia at the same time. Distance can't explain that. Tilt can."
*Check:* "In January the Earth is closest to the Sun, and the UK is cold. What is the real reason?"
*Expected answer:* the UK is tilted away from the Sun, so the light is spread out and the days are short.
*Most likely wrong answer:* "The Sun is weaker in January, so it's further away." This is circular. It just repeats the distance idea.

## Analogy and where it breaks

- **Analogy:** a torch shone straight down makes a small, bright patch. Tilted, the same torch makes a bigger, dimmer patch.
- **Where it breaks:** the Sun is huge and far away, so its light arrives almost parallel. The torch is small and close. The Earth's tilt stays the same all year, and the Earth moves round the Sun, which the torch can't do.

## Check question, with answer

**Question:** "Why is it summer in the UK in June, when the Earth is further from the Sun than it is in January?"
**Expected answer:** the northern half of the Earth is tilted towards the Sun, so sunlight hits it more directly and the days are longer.
**Most likely wrong answer:** "Because it's closer to the Sun in June." (This is the distance idea again.)

## Read-later version for students

Seasons happen because the Earth is tilted. The Earth leans over by about 23.5 degrees as it goes round the Sun. In June, the UK leans towards the Sun. The Sunlight hits the ground more directly, and the days are longer, so it gets warmer. In January, the UK leans away from the Sun. The light is spread out and the days are short, so it gets colder. The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January, so distance is not the reason for seasons.
