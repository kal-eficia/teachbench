===
A vaccine puts a small amount of a weakened or inactive germ into your body. This germ is called a pathogen. It cannot make you properly ill.

Your white blood cells spot antigens on the pathogen. Antigens are markers on its surface. Your white blood cells then make antibodies. Antibodies are proteins that fit one antigen exactly.

Later, if you meet the real, live pathogen, your memory cells remember it. They make antibodies very fast and in large numbers. This is called a secondary immune response. It stops symptoms from starting.

When enough people in a group are vaccinated, the pathogen spreads less. This protects people who have not been vaccinated too. This is called herd immunity.
===

**Glossary**

- **Pathogen:** a germ that can cause disease, such as a virus or bacterium.
- **Antigen:** a marker on the surface of a pathogen that the immune system can recognise.
- **Antibody:** a protein made by white blood cells that locks onto one specific antigen.
- **Memory cell:** a cell that remembers a pathogen, so the body can respond faster the next time.
- **Herd immunity:** when enough people are protected that a disease struggles to spread, which protects people who cannot be vaccinated.
