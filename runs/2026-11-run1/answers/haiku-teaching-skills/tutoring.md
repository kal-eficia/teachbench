# Session plan: GCSE Chemistry, one-to-one online, 60 minutes

**Assumptions:** a UK GCSE Chemistry course (AQA or Edexcel; the content is the same at this point). Covalent bonding starts at school next week. The homework was six ionic formulae questions. The student's last session notes say she got the dot-and-cross diagram for NaCl right, could not explain the high melting point of ionic compounds, and was guessing formulae such as MgCl2.

**Teaching focus for this session:** ionic formulae, using charge balancing. Covalent bonding next week depends on understanding charges, valency and how many electrons an atom needs to share or transfer. The melting point gap is handled first in the retrieval starter, then in a short model.

---

## 1. Retrieval starter (8 minutes)

Do this before any re-explanation. Ask from memory, no notes.

**Q1:** "What charge does a magnesium ion have, and why?"
- **Expected answer:** 2+, because magnesium loses two electrons.
- **Likely wrong answer:** 2- (confusing a metal ion with a non-metal ion).
- **Follow-up:** "Which group is magnesium in? How many outer electrons does that suggest?"
- **Confidence (1 to 3):** ask her to rate herself.

**Q2:** "Why does sodium chloride have a high melting point?"
- **Expected answer:** oppositely charged ions attract strongly in all directions in a giant lattice, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces.
- **Likely wrong answer:** "The bonds are strong" (without saying what is attracting what).
- **Follow-up:** "Which two particles are attracting? What is that force called?"
- **Confidence (1 to 3):** ask her to rate herself.

**Q3 (older topic, due for review):** "For sodium and chlorine, which atom gives up an electron, and which takes it?"
- **Expected answer:** sodium gives one, chlorine takes one.
- **Follow-up:** "What charge does each ion end up with?"

---

## 2. Review homework (5 minutes)

Talk through the six formulae questions. Ask her to read out each answer and say how she got the charge. Don't mark silently.

**Probing questions:**
- "How do you know that's the charge?"
- "What would happen if you wrote the formula without the number of chloride ions?"

Keep a record of which formulae she guessed. Those go into the next starter.

---

## 3. Teach: charge balancing (15 minutes)

**Method on the board:**
1. Write each ion's charge from its group (group 1 gives 1+, group 2 gives 2+, group 6 gives 2-, group 7 gives 1-).
2. Cross the charges over: the number of each ion becomes the subscript for the other.
3. Simplify if both numbers share a factor.
4. Check the total charge is zero.

**Worked example (model, think aloud):** "Magnesium is 2+ and chloride is 1-. The charges need to cancel, so I need two chloride ions for each magnesium ion: MgCl2. Check: +2 plus two times -1 is zero."

**Worked example (faded, she does the steps, you prompt):** aluminium oxide.
- Prompt: "What is aluminium's charge?" (3+)
- Prompt: "What is oxygen's charge?" (2-)
- Prompt: "Cross them over. What's the formula?" (Al2O3, because 2 times 3+ and 3 times 2- both give 6)
- Prompt: "Is the total zero?" (yes, +6 and -6)

**Melting point model (three sentences):** "Ionic compounds form giant lattices. Oppositely charged ions attract strongly in every direction. Lots of energy is needed to break these attractions, so the melting point is high."

Ask her to say this back in her own words.

---

## 4. Guided practice (10 minutes)

Do these together. Before she writes each formula, ask her for the two ion charges out loud.

- Calcium chloride
- Sodium oxide
- Aluminium chloride

**Answers:** CaCl2, Na2O, AlCl3. If she says Na O, prompt: "Is the total charge zero?"

---

## 5. Independent practice (15 minutes)

She works alone. You watch her method, not only her answers.

**Q1:** Give the formula for lithium oxide, calcium fluoride and aluminium sulfide.
- **Answers:** Li2O, CaF2, Al2S3.

**Q2:** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for magnesium oxide.
- **Answer:** Mg 2,8,2 gives 2 electrons to O 2,6, giving Mg2+ 2,8 and O2- 2,8. Check that the outer shells are full.

**Q3 (3 marks, exam style):** "Explain why magnesium oxide has a higher melting point than sodium chloride."
- **Model answer:** both are giant ionic lattices. Magnesium oxide has ions with bigger charges (2+ and 2-), so the electrostatic attraction between them is stronger, and more energy is needed to melt it.

---

## 6. Exit check (5 minutes)

Three quick questions, answered in writing:
1. Formula of potassium oxide. (K2O)
2. Formula of barium chloride. (BaCl2)
3. In one sentence, why does magnesium chloride need two chloride ions? (Each magnesium ion is 2+, so it needs two 1- chloride ions to cancel the charge.)

---

## 7. Set homework (2 minutes, about 20 minutes to do alone)

- Four new formulae, including one with a 3+ ion (for example, aluminium oxide).
- Two questions on last week's topic: explain melting point in two sentences, and state why sodium loses an electron.
- One preview question for covalent bonding next week: "Why do two chlorine atoms share electrons instead of one giving electrons to the other? Answer in one sentence and use the words outer shell."

---

**Note for the student (three lines):**
- Today we practised working out formulae by balancing charges, and you explained the melting point of ionic compounds in your own words.
- You did well at cross-checking each total charge.
- Next session, we start covalent bonding, so keep your charge table handy.

**Note for the parent (plain language, who does what):**
"This week your child practised writing chemical formulae by balancing the charges on the ions. For homework, your child will do about 20 minutes on their own. You don't need to teach the work. If you like, ask them to explain one formula to you. Our next session starts covalent bonding."
