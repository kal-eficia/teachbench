# Session plan: ionic bonding follow-up and bridge to covalent (60 minutes)

**Assumptions:** exam board is AQA-style GCSE (Combined or Triple; the content below is common to both). She has the standard Year 10 knowledge of ions and the periodic table. Her homework on ionic formulae has been marked or you will mark it at the start. Where I mention "her guessing", the aim is to replace a guess with a method she can run every time.

## Aims for the hour

By the end she should be able to:
1. Work out an ionic formula from the group number or from given ion charges, without guessing, and say why the answer is right.
2. Explain in a full, exam-style answer why ionic compounds have high melting points.
3. See how covalent bonding differs, so she arrives at school next week with a mental hook for it.

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-8 | Retrieval starter (no notes) |

## 0-8: Retrieval starter (low stakes, no notes)

Ask these out loud or on screen, one at a time. She answers from memory.
1. What happens to a sodium atom when it forms an ion? What charge does it get and why?
2. Draw the dot-and-cross diagram for NaCl (a quick sketch is enough, she got this right last time, so this is just a warm-up).
3. What is an ion?
4. How many electrons does a Mg atom lose? What charge is the ion?

Use this to see what has stuck. If question 4 is shaky, that is the root of the MgCl2 guessing, so spend a little longer there.

## 8-28: Formulae, from guessing to a method

**Step 1 (5 min): look at the homework.** Go through her six answers. For every one, ask "How did you decide?" and note whether she was using a rule or a hunch. Do not just mark right or wrong. Look for the pattern in her errors, for example reversing subscripts, forgetting that a group 2 ion is 2+, or writing the formula so the numbers are the charges.

**Step 2 (7 min): teach the one method.** Write it on screen and keep it for the rest of the session.

1. **Find each ion's charge.** Metals in group 1, 2, 3 form 1+, 2+, 3+. Non-metals in group 5, 6, 7 form 3-, 2-, 1-. (Group number tells you the charge: that is the thing she should know cold.)
2. **Make the charges cancel.** Total positive = total negative.
3. **Write the formula** using small subscripts for how many of each ion you need. Check by adding up the charges.

Worked example, said aloud as you write:
- Magnesium chloride. Mg is in group 2, so Mg2+. Cl is in group 7, so Cl-.
- One Mg2+ is +2. One Cl- is -1. I need a second Cl- to make the charges cancel: +2 and -2.
- So the formula is MgCl2. It is not a guess, it is balancing.

Explain the "criss-cross" shortcut only after she can balance charges, and warn her that it can mislead (for example Mg2+ with O2- gives MgO, not Mg2O2). Balancing charges is more reliable.

**Step 3 (8 min): practise, getting harder.** She does these on paper or a shared whiteboard and says the check out loud ("+2 and -2, so they cancel").

| # | Compound | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sodium oxide | Na2O |
| 2 | Calcium chloride | CaCl2 |
| 3 | Magnesium oxide | MgO |
| 4 | Aluminium oxide | Al2O3 |
| 5 | Potassium sulfide | K2S |
| 6 | Aluminium chloride | AlCl3 |

If she is confident, add one stretch question using a compound ion, given on the data sheet or by you: magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)2 (hydroxide is OH-). Explain why the brackets are needed. If she is not confident, stay with the first six and do not add compound ions yet.

## 28-45: Why ionic compounds have high melting points

Her gap is the explanation, not the fact. Build it in stages rather than telling her the whole thing.

**Step 1 (3 min): what is the structure?** Show or sketch a giant ionic lattice (alternating Na+ and Cl- in a regular 3D pattern). Ask: "What is holding it together?" Aim for: strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions. Stress "in all directions" and "throughout the lattice".

**Step 2 (5 min): what happens when it melts?** Ask: "To melt, what has to happen to the ions?" They have to be free to move past each other, so the forces of attraction between the ions have to be overcome. Ask: "Are those forces strong or weak? So how much energy is needed?"

**Step 3 (4 min): build the answer together using this frame.** She fills the gaps:

> Ionic compounds have a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions held together by ______ electrostatic forces of attraction in all directions. A lot of ______ is needed to overcome these forces, so the melting point is ______.

**Step 4 (5 min): she writes it from scratch, no frame.** Then compare it with this model answer:

> Ionic compounds have a giant lattice structure. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions, throughout the lattice. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these strong forces, so ionic compounds have high melting points.

**Common errors to listen for:**
- "The ionic bonds break": prompt with "which forces are overcome, and between what?" (attractions between ions, not covalent bonds inside anything).
- "It's a molecule": NaCl is a lattice, not a molecule. Fix the vocabulary now, it matters next week.
- Saying "strong bonds" with no mention of energy. Marks are usually for "strong forces", "oppositely charged ions" and "lots of energy".

**Quick link (optional, 2 min if time allows):** when molten or dissolved, the ions can move, so ionic compounds conduct. In solid form they cannot. This follows from the same lattice idea.

## 45-55: Bridge to covalent bonding

She starts covalent bonding at school next week, so use ten minutes to give her a scaffold, not to teach it fully.

1. **Contrast question (3 min):** "In ionic bonding, electrons are transferred. What else could two atoms do with electrons if neither wants to give one away?" Lead her to the idea of sharing.
2. **Where it happens (2 min):** between non-metal atoms (for example H and Cl, or two Cl atoms). Say it, then ask her to sort these as likely ionic or covalent from the elements alone: NaCl, HCl, MgO, H2O, CO2, CaF2. She uses metal + non-metal = ionic, non-metal + non-metal = covalent. Answers: ionic, covalent, ionic, covalent, covalent, ionic. (Order is deliberately mixed.)
3. **Preview (3 min):** draw a simple dot-and-cross for H2 (two shared electrons) and tell her this is what she will see at school. Do not go further.
4. **Teaser (2 min):** "Simple covalent substances often have low melting points. Based on what you just explained about ionic ones, why might that be?" Do not expect a full answer, just plant it.

## 55-60: Exit check and homework

**Exit check (3 min, closed book, she answers verbally or in the chat):**
1. Write the formula for aluminium oxide and prove it. (Al2O3, +6 and -6.)
2. Give me the three-part explanation for high melting points.
3. Is NaCl a molecule? Why or why not?

**Homework (about 30 minutes):**
- Six new formulae questions, each with the charge check written out (include at least one group 3 and one group 6 or 7 pairing, for example aluminium fluoride AlF3, lithium oxide Li2O, calcium sulfide CaS, magnesium bromide MgBr2, barium oxide BaO, sodium nitride Na3N).
- One six-mark style question: "Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point." Self-check against the three parts of the model answer.
- Optional, 10 minutes: read the first page of her school's covalent bonding material, or her notes if they have started, and bring one question to next week.

## Notes for you

- **Why the plan is built this way:** she can reproduce a diagram but cannot explain or derive. That usually means she has memorised a picture. Every task above asks her to say why, aloud, and to run a check, which is what turns recall into understanding.
- **Reducing the "guessing" feeling:** praise the method ("you checked the charges"), not just the answer. If she ever says "I think", ask her to find the check.
- **Next week:** start with a short retrieval quiz on this week, then follow whatever school have covered on covalent bonding so the two stay in step.
