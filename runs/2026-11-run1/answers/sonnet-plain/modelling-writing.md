# Modelling an Analytical Paragraph: "The Eagle" by Alfred, Lord Tennyson

**Year 7 English | Approx. 35 to 40 minutes | I do, We do, You do**

*Assumptions: students know basic terms (verb, adjective, simile, personification, alliteration), have the poem printed in front of them, and have met PEE/PEEL-style paragraph structures. I use a point, evidence, explain, effect structure with a link back. The "I do" focuses on the first line of the poem, "We do" on the second stanza, and "You do" on a different line. Adapt the sentence stems to whatever structure your department uses.*

## The poem (on the board and in student hands)

> He clasps the crag with crooked hands;
> Close to the sun in lonely lands,
> Ring'd with the azure world, he stands.
>
> The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
> He watches from his mountain walls,
> And like a thunderbolt he falls.

## Resources

- Poem on the board (so you can annotate it live)
- Paragraph frame on the board and on student sheets
- Mini-whiteboards or paper
- A visualiser or a document camera if you have one

## Success criteria (on the board)

- I make a clear point about what the poet is showing us about the eagle.
- I use a short, exact quotation.
- I zoom in on a word or technique and explain what it makes us picture or feel.
- I link back to the poem's bigger idea.

## The paragraph frame

| Part | Sentence starter |
|---|---|
| **Point** | Tennyson presents the eagle as ... |
| **Evidence** | This is shown when he writes, "..." |
| **Explain** | The word "..." suggests / makes us picture ... |
| **Effect** | This makes the reader feel ... / This creates an image of ... |
| **Link** | Overall, this shows that ... |

---

## Part 1: I do (about 10 minutes)

*Teacher stays at the board, annotating the poem and writing the paragraph live. Speak your thinking aloud. Students only watch, listen and track with their eyes. Do not ask them to write yet.*

**Teacher script:**

"Right, eyes up here. For the next few minutes I'm going to do all the thinking out loud so you can see what is going on in my head when I write about a poem. You don't need to write anything yet. Just watch how I do it, because in a few minutes it's your turn.

My question is: **how does Tennyson want us to see this eagle?**

First I read the poem again, and I'm looking for the words that make the strongest picture. I'm looking at line one: 'He clasps the crag with crooked hands.' Hmm. 'Hands'. Eagles don't have hands. They have talons. So why did Tennyson say hands? [Underline *hands*.] That's interesting. Hands are something people have. So the poet is making the eagle sound a bit human, like a person gripping a rock. That's called **personification**. I'm going to circle it.

And what about 'clasps'? [Underline *clasps*.] To clasp is to grip tightly, firmly. It's not 'touches' or 'rests on'. So he's holding the rock tightly. This is a strong, powerful bird.

And what about 'crooked'? [Underline *crooked*.] Crooked means bent or twisted. That makes me picture sharp, curved talons, like claws bent ready to grab.

So now I've got my idea. My **point** is that Tennyson presents the eagle as powerful and in control. I'll start my paragraph.

[Write on the board and read aloud as you write:]

*Tennyson presents the eagle as a powerful and commanding creature.*

Now my **evidence**. I need a short quotation, just the words I need. I don't need the whole line. I'll use 'clasps the crag'.

[Write:]

*This is shown when he writes, 'He clasps the crag with crooked hands.'*

Hmm, that's the whole line and I only need the key part. A tip: shorter quotations are stronger because they make me focus on the exact words. Let me change it.

[Cross out and rewrite:]

*This is shown when the eagle 'clasps the crag'.*

Better. Now the most important part. The **explain**. This is where I zoom in on one word and say what it makes the reader think. I said 'clasps' is strong. Why does that matter? Let me write that.

[Write:]

*The verb 'clasps' suggests that the eagle grips the rock tightly and firmly, as if it will never let go. The word makes the eagle sound strong and secure on its high cliff.*

Notice I didn't just say 'it's a good verb'. I said what it suggests and what picture it creates.

Now the **effect** on the reader. How does it make us feel?

[Write:]

*This makes the reader feel that the eagle is fierce and mighty, and perhaps a little bit intimidating.*

And finally, a **link**: how does this fit the poem as a whole?

[Write:]

*Overall, this shows that Tennyson wants us to admire the eagle as a proud ruler of the sky and the mountains.*

Let me read my whole paragraph back to check it makes sense.

[Read the finished paragraph aloud.]

*Tennyson presents the eagle as a powerful and commanding creature. This is shown when the eagle 'clasps the crag'. The verb 'clasps' suggests that the eagle grips the rock tightly and firmly, as if it will never let go. The word makes the eagle sound strong and secure on its high cliff. This makes the reader feel that the eagle is fierce and mighty, and perhaps a little bit intimidating. Overall, this shows that Tennyson wants us to admire the eagle as a proud ruler of the sky and the mountains.*

So what did I do? Let's get the steps on the board: **1. Find a word that stands out. 2. Say what it means or suggests. 3. Say what picture or feeling it creates. 4. Link it to the bigger idea.** I'll point to the colours I used to mark each part of the paragraph."

*(Colour-code the paragraph: point, evidence, explain, effect, link.)*

**Quick check (1 minute):** "Turn to your partner. What was the first thing I did before I wrote any sentences?" (Answer: found a word that stood out and thought about what it suggested.)

## Part 2: We do (about 12 minutes)

*Co-construct a paragraph about the second stanza. Use cold calling, mini-whiteboards, and your own live typing or writing. Keep the frame on the board.*

**Teacher script:**

"Now we're going to do one together. I'll still be holding the pen, but I need your brains. We're looking at the second stanza:

*The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;*
*He watches from his mountain walls,*
*And like a thunderbolt he falls.*

Our question: **how does Tennyson show the eagle's power over the world below him?**

**Step 1: Spot the word (2 minutes).** Read the stanza silently and underline one word or phrase you think is interesting. Then turn to your partner and compare. [Pause. Cold call 2 or 3 pairs.]

*Expected responses:* 'wrinkled', 'crawls', 'thunderbolt', 'falls', 'watches'.

*Teacher guidance:* "Lots of good choices. Let's go with **'wrinkled sea'** and **'crawls'**, because I want us to think about how the sea looks from way up high. Look at those words. What does the eagle's view look like? [Take answers: tiny, slow, old, ripples like skin.] So the sea is described like something small and slow, even like an old, wrinkled creature crawling. That's the eagle's viewpoint, from very high up. And 'crawls' is another example of personification, just like the 'hands' in the first stanza."

**Step 2: Write the point together (2 minutes).** "On your whiteboards, write a sentence starting with 'Tennyson presents the eagle as ...' Finish it in your own words." [Boards up. Choose a strong one, praise one thing about it, and write it on the board, tweaking it if needed.]

*Model answer for reference:* *Tennyson presents the eagle as a ruler who looks down on the world from above.*

**Step 3: Evidence (1 minute).** "What is the shortest quotation we need? Tell your partner, then boards up." [Aim for 'wrinkled sea' or 'beneath him crawls'.] "Remember: short and exact."

**Step 4: Explain (4 minutes).** "This is the hard bit, so we'll build it in steps. Question 1: what does 'crawls' suggest about the sea? [Take 2 or 3 answers: slow, small, weak.] Question 2: why would the sea look like that to the eagle? [Because he is so high up.] Question 3: what does that tell us about the eagle compared with the sea? [He is powerful; he is above everything.] Right, one sentence each. Use the stem *The verb 'crawls' suggests...*

[Take 2 student sentences, choose the strongest, and join them into a single 'explain' sentence on the board.]

*Model answer for reference:* *The verb 'crawls' suggests that the sea looks slow and tiny from where the eagle is, as if it is a weak creature far beneath him.*

**Step 5: Effect and link (2 minutes).** "Last two sentences: what does this make the reader feel, and how does it link to the whole poem? Pair talk for 30 seconds, then share." [Write a combined version.]

*Model answer for reference:* *This makes the reader feel that the eagle is far above everything and in complete control. Overall, this shows that Tennyson wants us to see the eagle as the king of nature.*

**Wrap up:** "Read the whole paragraph we've built together out loud. [Choral or one student.] Which sentence do you think is the strongest and why?" (Probably the 'explain' sentence: it zooms in on a word.)

## Part 3: You do (about 12 to 15 minutes)

*Students write independently in silence. Circulate, and use live feedback and the "stop and share" technique.*

**Teacher script:**

"Now it's your turn to write your own analytical paragraph, all on your own. The question is: **how does Tennyson show the eagle's speed and danger in the last line?**

*And like a thunderbolt he falls.*

Take 30 seconds to think first. What stands out? 'Thunderbolt'? 'Falls'? Choose one word or phrase. [Pause.]

Here's what I expect to see in your paragraph. Look at your frame and the board:

1. A **point** about how Tennyson shows the eagle.
2. A **short quotation**.
3. An **explain** sentence that zooms in on one word and says what it suggests. This is the most important part.
4. An **effect** on the reader.
5. A **link** to the whole poem.

You'll have **10 minutes**. Silent, independent. If you get stuck, look at the two paragraphs on the board. I'll walk around and I'll be looking for the word you've chosen and what you say it suggests.

*Hint for those who need it:* a thunderbolt is a flash of lightning with a crash of thunder. What is it like? Sudden, fast, powerful, dangerous. What does 'like' tell you about the technique? It's a **simile**."

**Circulating notes:**

- Point students to the 'explain' sentence if they are only describing the poem (retelling) instead of analysing it. Prompt: "What does that word make you picture? What does it suggest about the eagle?"
- Push stronger writers: "Can you explore two meanings of the word, or explain why he chose 'falls' instead of 'flies' or 'dives'?" (Falls suggests no control or a dramatic, deadly drop. It also contrasts with the eagle being in control. Is he falling, or is he swooping on purpose?)
- Support weaker writers with sentence starters: *The simile 'like a thunderbolt' suggests that the eagle is... This makes the reader imagine...*

**Model answer to hold back (for the teacher):**

*Tennyson presents the eagle as a sudden and deadly hunter. This is shown when he 'falls' 'like a thunderbolt'. The simile compares the eagle to a thunderbolt, which suggests that it strikes with enormous speed and force, with no warning. This makes the reader feel the danger and the excitement of the moment of the attack, as the calm, still eagle of the earlier lines suddenly becomes violent. Overall, this shows that Tennyson sees the eagle as both majestic and terrifying.*

**Feedback and wrap-up (3 minutes):**

"Pens down. Put a tick by your best explain sentence. [Pause.] Swap with a partner and read theirs. Underline one word in their explain sentence that you think is a really good choice and tell them why. Finally, two volunteers to read out their explain sentence."

**Exit check (optional):** "On a sticky note, finish the sentence: *To write a strong analytical paragraph, I need to...*" (Look for: zoom in on a word, say what it suggests, link to the effect.)

## Differentiation notes

- **Support:** give a partly completed paragraph with gaps for the key word and explanation; provide a word bank (suggests, implies, creates the impression, makes us picture, reminds us of).
- **Stretch:** ask students to compare how the eagle is presented in stanza one and stanza two (still and strong, then fast and deadly), or to consider why the poem is only six lines long and the effect of the short, sharp ending.
- **Misconceptions to watch for:** students retelling ("the eagle falls from the mountain") rather than analysing; quoting whole lines; saying a technique is used "to make it more interesting" without saying how.
