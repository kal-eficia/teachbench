# Making peer feedback useful: Year 8 phone stand design sheets

**Assumptions:** A 50 to 60 minute lesson, students work in pairs or threes, design sheets include sketches, annotations and some measurements, and the project is halfway through, so feedback needs to lead to changes before they make anything.

**Why the comments were "good" and "nice":** Students don't know what to look for, don't want to upset friends, and have nothing specific to say it against. The fix is to give them a clear focus, a structure for the comment, and a reason to act on it.

## 1. Give feedback a job: three criteria

Pick three things to look at, tied to the design brief. For a phone stand these could be:
- **Stability:** will it stay upright with a phone in it, and from which angle?
- **Fit and function:** does it fit different phone sizes, allow charging, keep the screen at a useful angle?
- **Making:** can it be made in the workshop with the materials and tools available, in the time they have?

Write them on the board. Tell students they may only comment on these three today.

## 2. Give them a sentence structure

Put these on the board or a card and require all feedback to use them:

- **I can see that...** (describe what is on the sheet)
- **It works well because...** (a specific reason linked to the criteria)
- **One thing I would change or check is... because...**
- **A question I have is...** (to prompt thinking)

"Good" and "nice" are banned. Explain: "If your comment would fit on any design sheet, it isn't specific enough."

## 3. Make it concrete with a quick model

Show two sample comments on a screen:
- "Nice design, good drawing." (Not useful: why?)
- "The angle of the back support looks about 60 degrees, which would keep the phone steady, but I can't see how the base stops it tipping if someone taps the screen. Could you make the base wider or add a lip at the front?" (Useful)

Ask: what makes the second one better? Students name it: it's specific, it explains, and it suggests something.

## 4. Structure the activity (about 30 minutes)

1. **Focus (5 min):** Show the criteria, the sentence frames and the model comments.
2. **Silent read (5 min):** The reviewer swaps sheets with a partner and reads in silence, ticking the criteria they can find evidence for and underlining anything unclear.
3. **Written feedback (8 min):** On a feedback slip or sticky notes stuck directly on the sheet, the reviewer writes: one thing that works (with a reason), one improvement (with a reason and a suggestion), and one question.
4. **Talk it through (6 min):** The pair discuss the comments. The designer explains, and may disagree. The reviewer can ask follow-up questions.
5. **Rotate (optional, 8 min):** Swap again for a second reviewer, so each designer gets two views. The second reviewer reads the first feedback and adds something different.

## 5. Make it count: students must act

- **Response box:** The designer writes a short response on the bottom of the sheet: "I will change X because...", or "I will keep X because..." They must reply to every improvement point, which prevents feedback being ignored.
- **Revise:** Give the last 10 minutes of the lesson to update the sheet in a different colour pen, showing at least one change made because of feedback.
- **Evidence:** Collect the sheets and mark for use of feedback, not only the design quality. This signals that feedback is part of the project.

## 6. Other ways to raise the quality

- **Pair thoughtfully.** Avoid best friends, mix confidence levels, and pair students whose designs are different enough to learn from each other.
- **Use the designer's question.** Before swapping, each designer writes one question on their sheet that they want feedback about ("Is my hinge strong enough?"). The reviewer must answer it.
- **Feedback "stars and a wish" with measurement.** Ask for one number: a measurement, an angle or a dimension. This pushes comments towards specifics.
- **Exemplar gallery.** Briefly show an annotated design that shows the level of detail you expect.
- **Role cards.** Give each reviewer a role: "the user" (does it fit my phone?), "the maker" (can I build it?), and "the client" (does it meet the brief?). Different roles give different comments.
- **Check in as they work.** Circulate and challenge vague comments straight away: "Why? How do you know?"

## What success looks like

By the end, each student should have at least one specific, evidence-based improvement written on their sheet, and be able to say why they made it. Next lesson, ask a few students to share a change they made and the comment that prompted it, to build the habit.
