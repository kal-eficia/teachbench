# Supporting a Year 4 boy with ADHD: low-effort, high-impact strategies

*Assumptions: you're in a UK primary school with a SENCo, and you're looking for things you can do inside your normal teaching, not a separate programme. I've put the biggest wins first. You don't need to do everything: pick two or three, run them for two weeks, then review.*

## The key idea

What you describe (struggling to start, calling out, getting up) are symptoms of the condition, not choices. They come from difficulty with self-regulation and working memory. So the aim is to **change the environment and the routines**, not to try to "fix" him with reminders and consequences. Most of what helps him will also help several other children.

## 1. Starting tasks (the biggest time-saver)

- **Make the first step tiny and visible.** Instead of "write your story", say "write the date and your first sentence". Write the first step on a mini whiteboard or a sticky note on his desk.
- **Give instructions in short chunks, one at a time**, and have him say back what he's doing first. Put it on the board as a numbered list, with a tick-off checklist (3 to 4 steps) on his desk.
- **Do a 60-second "launch check".** Once the class is set going, go to him first, before anyone else. Stay for about 30 seconds until he's written or done the first thing. Most of his difficulty is the first minute, so a short, planned visit does more than ten reminders later.
- **Use a timer.** A visual sand timer or a countdown on the board ("10 minutes, then check in") makes time concrete. Break long tasks into short bursts.
- **Reduce the amount, not the challenge.** Set 4 questions instead of 10 where the learning is the same, or let him show his thinking in a different format for part of a task.
- **Start with something engaging**, where you can: a quick physical or hands-on beginning, or a question he can answer.

## 2. Calling out

- **Agree a private signal beforehand**, like a hand on his desk or a card, that means "you're about to call out, hold on". Do it as a team, not as a telling-off.
- **Give him a legitimate outlet.** A "thought parking" sticky note or mini whiteboard where he writes the idea down so he doesn't lose it. Or give two or three "question tokens" per lesson that he can spend on calling out.
- **Use more ways to answer than hands up.** Whole-class mini-whiteboards, turn-and-talk and choral response let him respond often without interrupting.
- **Catch him getting it right, specifically and quietly.** "Thanks for waiting, I noticed that." Aim for many more positive comments than corrections. Private is better than public with children who often get public corrections.
- **Pre-plan the moments he's most likely to call out** (the end of a story, the start of a discussion) and give him a job at those points, such as being the one who reads the next line.
- **When he does call out**, a calm, neutral response ("hold that thought, I'll come to you") is better than a long reprimand.

## 3. Getting up and moving

- **Build movement into the lesson for everyone.** Hand out books, collect pencils, run an errand, wipe the board. These give him a legitimate reason to move, and it's a positive role.
- **Agree a movement routine**: for instance, he can stand at the back table for part of independent work, or take a "water and walk" break at a set time.
- **Seating**: near the front, close to where you teach, away from the door and the busiest part of the class. Seat him next to a calm, positive role model. Put him on the end of a row so he can get up without disturbing anyone.
- **Small physical outlets help**: a wobble cushion or a resistance band round the chair legs, a quiet fidget object (agree rules: it stays on the desk and isn't a toy). Try one thing at a time.
- **Short movement breaks** (a stretch, "stand up, sit down", a quick jog on the spot) for the whole class every 15 to 20 minutes.

## 4. Whole-day routines that cost you nothing

- A **visual timetable** for the day, so he knows what's next.
- **Warn him before transitions** ("Two minutes, then we tidy up").
- A **consistent start-of-day and start-of-lesson routine.**
- Make sure he has **what he needs on his desk** before you start (pencil, ruler, book) so the setting-up isn't a barrier.

## 5. Quick wins with the adults around him

- **Talk to his parents and his SENCo.** Ask what works at home, what has been tried, and whether there are any recommendations from a paediatrician or an SEN support plan you should be following. A short, positive message home about what went well tends to build the relationship.
- **Ask him.** A calm, private chat ("what helps you start? what makes it hard?") often gets very useful answers, even at age 8 or 9.
- If there is no plan or SEN support in place yet, ask your SENCo about one. It gives you shared strategies and formal support.

## A simple way to track whether it's working

For two weeks, put a tally on a sticky note: how many times did he start the task within 2 minutes? How many calling-out incidents per lesson? Reviewing it takes 5 minutes and tells you which strategy is worth keeping.

## If you only do three things

1. Go to him first after setting the class off, with a tiny first step written down.
2. Agree a private signal and a "thought parking" outlet for calling out.
3. Give him positive, purposeful reasons to move around the classroom.
