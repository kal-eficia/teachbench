# Extension for Four Year 8 Students Who Finish Equations Early

## Assumptions
- The class is working on solving linear equations (one- and two-step, possibly unknowns on both sides and brackets).
- The four students are secure on the standard method, so more of the same questions would be boredom, not challenge.
- You want something that can be set in 30 seconds, needs little marking, and keeps them quietly busy for the rest of the lesson and ideally beyond.

## Why they mess about
They have finished and have nothing worth doing. "Do more questions" tells them the reward for being quick is more of the same. The fix is a standing extension that is more interesting than messing about, and that they know about **before** they finish.

## Set it up as a routine
- Tell the whole class at the start: "If you finish early, there is a challenge on the board or in the folder. You don't need to ask me."
- Keep a **challenge folder** (or a slide titled "Finished? Try this") with three or four tasks, so students always have a next step.
- Give the four a clear expectation: the challenge should be done in their books, and you will ask one of them to present at the end.
- Consider seating them together so they can discuss, which usually improves focus and reasoning.

## Tasks (pick two or three, in roughly this order)

### 1. Reverse the problem: make your own
"Write an equation whose solution is x = 4 that needs at least three steps to solve. Now make one with x = -2. Swap with your partner and solve each other's. Did they get the right answer?"
*Why it works:* designing a question needs a deeper understanding than answering one. It also self-checks (they know the answer).

### 2. Spot the mistake
Give a worked solution with an error and ask what went wrong and what the correct answer is. For example:

> 3(x + 2) = 18
> 3x + 2 = 18
> 3x = 16
> x = 16/3

*Error:* the bracket was not multiplied out correctly (3x + 6, not 3x + 2). Correct answer: x = 4.

Add a few more with different errors, such as dividing only one term, or a sign error when moving a term across the equals sign.

### 3. Form and solve equations from context
- "The perimeter of this rectangle is 46 cm. The length is (2x + 3) cm and the width is (x + 4) cm. Find x, then the area." (Perimeter: 2(2x + 3 + x + 4) = 46, so 3x + 7 = 23, x = 16/3. Check the numbers you choose so they are tidy, for example set the perimeter to 40: 3x + 7 = 20, x = 13/3 is not tidy, so use perimeter 34 instead: 3x + 7 = 17, x = 10/3. Choose values so that x comes out whole before you hand this out. For example, length (2x + 3) and width (x + 4) with perimeter 50 gives 3x + 7 = 25, so x = 6, length 15 cm, width 10 cm, area 150 cm squared.)
- "I think of a number, multiply by 3, subtract 7, and get 20. What was my number?" (9.)
- "Three friends share a bill. Ali pays x pounds, Bea pays 2 pounds more than Ali, and Cal pays twice what Ali pays. The total is 30 pounds. How much did each person pay?" (x + x + 2 + 2x = 30, so 4x = 28, x = 7. Ali 7, Bea 9, Cal 14.)

### 4. Always, sometimes, never
Ask them to decide and prove, with examples:
- "An equation of the form ax + b = c always has exactly one solution."  (Sometimes: true when a is not zero. When a = 0 there is no solution or every number is a solution.)
- "If you add the same number to both sides of an equation, the solution stays the same." (Always.)
- "The solution to an equation with x on both sides is bigger than the solution to a similar equation with x on one side." (Never a rule; encourage testing with examples.)

### 5. Equations with a twist (for the strongest)
- Fractions: x/3 + 2 = 7 (x = 15), then (x + 1)/2 = (x - 3)/4 (x = -5).
- Negative and fractional solutions: 5x + 3 = 2x - 9 (x = -4).
- "How many steps?" Find an equation that takes exactly 4 steps with the standard method. Can they find one that takes only 1 step but looks hard? (For example 2(x + 3) = 2 x 9, since dividing both sides by 2 gives x + 3 = 9.)

### 6. Link to other topics
- Solve to find a missing angle in a triangle or on a straight line, where the angles are given as algebraic expressions (for example x, 2x + 10 and 3x - 40 in a triangle: 6x - 30 = 180, so x = 35).
- Solve the equation that comes from setting two quantities equal, for example "At what point do the two taxi firms cost the same?" (Firm A: 3 pounds plus 2 pounds per mile; Firm B: 1 pound plus 3 pounds per mile; 3 + 2m = 1 + 3m, so m = 2.)

### 7. Puzzle and game options (if you want something different)
- **Balance puzzles:** pan balance diagrams with shapes representing unknowns (a common puzzle format).
- **Equation pyramids or "Find my number" cards:** each card gives a clue as an equation, and students solve a chain to reach a final code.
- **Teach it:** "Write a one-minute explanation for a Year 7 student who has never seen this. Include one worked example and one common mistake." (Teaching the method is a strong test of understanding, and you can use the best one in a future lesson.)

## Practical ideas
- **Make it low-effort for you:** put three or four tasks on a slide or printed sheet, and put the answers on the back so they can self-check. That reduces the demands on you during the lesson.
- **Give them a reason to take it seriously:** a "challenge board" where completed tasks are recorded, or the chance to present a solution to the class.
- **Rotate the tasks:** if the same four students are always finishing early, they will run out of tasks. Keep a larger bank and mix in one new type each week.
- **Don't punish speed:** if they finish and then are given a pile of extra routine work, they will learn to slow down. Make the extension more interesting than the main task, not more of the same.
- **Check they really are secure:** quickly look at their work. If there are small errors, the extension could be a "check and correct" task on their own work instead.
- **Behaviour:** if the messing about continues even with something worthwhile to do, speak to them privately. Tell them you've noticed they finish quickly and that you want to give them something better, and ask what they would find interesting.

## One thing to do tomorrow
Print or display tasks 1 and 2 (make your own, and spot the mistake) with answers, and tell the four at the start of the lesson what they will do when they finish. That alone usually removes most of the idle time.
