# OCR A Level Computer Science NEA: 8-Month Plan and What Help You Can Give

## Assumptions
- The qualification is OCR A Level Computer Science (H446) and the NEA is the **Programming Project** (Component 03/04), worth 70 marks and 20% of the A level.
- Students start now and the work has to be finished, marked and moderated in time for the OCR submission deadline in the spring or early summer of Year 13 (usually mid-May for marks entry, with samples sent shortly after). I have therefore planned eight months, with the **last four to six weeks as a buffer, not working time**. Check the current year's exact dates on the OCR website and on the OCR Interchange timeline, as I cannot verify them from here.
- Everything below about rules is based on my understanding of the OCR specification and the JCQ *Instructions for conducting non-examination assessments*. **Please check the current versions of both documents before you start**, because the details are updated each year and your exams officer will have the current copy. Where I am less than certain I have said so.

## The structure of the project (what the marks reward)
Roughly (check the spec for exact allocations):
| Section | Marks |
|---|---|
| Analysis of the problem | 10 |
| Design of the solution | 15 |
| Developing the solution | 25 |
| Evaluation | 20 |

Students choose their own problem, with a real client or "stakeholder", and must produce a program they have designed and built themselves. The written report is as important as the code, and the **evaluation is 20 marks**, which is the section students most often leave to the final days.

## The core idea: break it into short, checked deadlines
Last-month panic happens when the only deadline is the end. Give students **fortnightly deliverables**, check every one, and make the early checkpoints count as the first stage of the real project, not as a rehearsal.

## Month-by-month plan

### Month 1: Choosing a problem and a client
- **Week 1:** Teach what a suitable project looks like. Share examples of past student projects at different levels (OCR publishes exemplar material). Explain that the problem should need sustained effort, involve some real complexity (for example data structures, algorithms, a database, or an interface that has been thoughtfully designed), and have a real person or group who can give feedback.
- **Weeks 2 to 3:** Students pitch two or three ideas in one paragraph each. Meet each student for ten minutes about feasibility only (see the guidance below on what you can say).
- **Week 4:** Students confirm their stakeholder and have a first meeting with them. **Checkpoint 1:** one-page project proposal agreed, with the name of the stakeholder.

### Month 2: Analysis
- Interview with the stakeholder (record notes or evidence of the meeting).
- Research existing similar solutions.
- Write the problem description, the stakeholder needs, success criteria and the scope (including what is *out* of scope).
- **Checkpoint 2:** Draft analysis section complete. This is where you check the project is neither too simple nor impossible.

### Month 3: Design
- Decide the structure of the program, key algorithms, data structures, the user interface and the test plan.
- Write the test plan **before** the coding, with expected results for each success criterion.
- **Checkpoint 3:** Design section complete, including a test plan.

### Months 4 to 5: Development (the biggest block)
- Work in clearly defined iterations. Each iteration should have a short goal, a version of the code that runs, a test and a short written commentary. Encourage students to keep a development diary with dated entries and screenshots as they go, because it is much harder to reconstruct afterwards.
- Use version control (for example Git or a dated backup system) so there is evidence that students did the work over time. This also helps you confirm authenticity.
- **Checkpoint 4 (end of month 4):** a working core program (the minimum that does something useful), and the development write-up up to date.
- **Checkpoint 5 (end of month 5):** most features built, and the testing evidence matching the test plan.

### Month 6: Testing, stakeholder feedback and polishing
- Complete the testing against the test plan, including failures and fixes.
- Return to the stakeholder with the working program and gather real feedback.
- **Checkpoint 6:** testing evidence and stakeholder feedback recorded.

### Month 7: Evaluation and final write-up
- Evaluate against each success criterion with evidence.
- Discuss the effectiveness of the solution, how it could be improved, and the stakeholder's views.
- Proofread and compile the whole report. Check it is clearly organised, with consistent headings.
- **Checkpoint 7:** full draft complete (this should be **two to three weeks** before your internal final deadline).

### Month 8: Buffer, final submission and marking
- Students finalise the work to your internal deadline, which should be **comfortably before** the OCR deadline.
- You mark the projects, internally standardise with any colleagues, and complete the marks and authentication paperwork.
- This month also protects against illness, lost files and the unexpected.

## Habits that keep them on track
1. **Fortnightly 10-minute progress meeting** with each student in lessons (and written record of what was said, see below).
2. **A public tracker** (a simple spreadsheet or wall chart) of checkpoint completion. Students are more motivated when progress is visible.
3. **Short "stand-up" starts** to lessons: "What did you do last time? What are you doing today? What is blocking you?"
4. **Backward planning for the whole year**, including the dates of school holidays, mock exams, and any other deadlines, to show students where the pressure points are.
5. **Parental or tutor contact early**, not at the end. If a student misses two checkpoints, speak to them and home straight away.
6. **A "late" policy that is realistic**: since the NEA must be finished on time for the board, each missed checkpoint reduces the time available for the later sections. Make this concrete: "If analysis finishes three weeks late, you lose three weeks from development."
7. **Use the mock exam period wisely.** Mocks often cause a stall. Plan lighter NEA targets for those weeks and heavier ones afterwards.

## What help you are allowed to give
The key principle in the JCQ rules and the OCR specification is that **the work must be the candidate's own**, and the teacher's role is to **supervise, guide and authenticate**, not to improve the work. My understanding of the rules is as follows (please check the current wording):

### What you can do
- **Explain the task requirements and the assessment criteria**, including the mark scheme.
- **Teach the skills in advance.** You can teach programming techniques, data structures and algorithms in lessons, as this is part of the course. You can teach students how to structure a report in general terms.
- **Give general advice on the suitability of a project idea**, for example whether it is too small, too large or too similar to a standard textbook problem. You may not choose the problem for them or design the solution.
- **Review work in progress and give general feedback**, for example "your analysis does not yet explain how you will test success" or "you need more evidence of testing". The feedback should be at a level that identifies a general area to improve and **does not tell the student exactly what to write or fix**.
- **Remind students of the rules** on plagiarism, sources, and acknowledging any help or code from other places.
- **Supervise** so that you can confirm that the work is the student's. Students may do some of the work outside lessons, but you must be confident it is theirs. The JCQ rules distinguish between work done under supervision and work done without it, so check what applies to this component.

### What you cannot do
- **Give detailed, specific corrections.** For example, you cannot write sections, fix bugs in their code, rewrite their design, or hand them a model solution to copy.
- **Repeatedly review and correct drafts** so that the final piece reflects your input more than theirs. My understanding is that a draft can be reviewed in general terms, but if you give detailed written or oral guidance on how to improve it, that help has to be taken into account in the marking (and in some circumstances disclosed), and it must not go beyond what the rules allow. Confirm exactly where the line is in the current JCQ document.
- **Provide model answers, templates or essay plans** that give the content of the work.
- **Let students share code or write the report together.** Students can discuss ideas generally, but the final work must be individual.
- **Allow work that has been written, or substantially written, by someone or something else**, including a tutor, a parent, or an AI tool. The JCQ publishes specific guidance on AI use in assessments, which says students must not submit AI-generated work as their own and must acknowledge any AI use that is allowed. Read that document and give students a clear written statement of your department's position.
- **Use the same or very similar project for several students**, or let students copy each other's ideas, without it being clearly each student's own work.

### Records you should keep
- A **log of any help given** (date, what was discussed, whether it was general or specific). If you give more help than the rules allow, it may have to be recorded on the candidate record form and may affect the mark.
- **Evidence of authenticity**: version control history, dated development diary entries and your own observation notes.
- **Candidate authentication statements** (signed by the student) and **your own declaration** when you submit the marks.
- Keep the work **secure** between sessions so it cannot be altered.

### Third parties
- The **stakeholder** can be a real person who gives requirements and feedback. They should not write any part of the solution or report.
- **Online help** such as forums, tutorials and documentation is generally fine for learning, but any code copied or adapted from elsewhere has to be clearly identified and credited, and it should not form the main body of the solution. Students need to show they understand and can explain anything they have used.

### A rule of thumb
Ask: "Could the student have produced this without my help, and is the thinking theirs?" If you pointed to the **area** that needs work, that is guidance. If you supplied the **answer**, that is not allowed.

## Practical things to do this week
1. Download the current OCR specification, the NEA instructions for the Programming Project, and the JCQ NEA and AI-use documents. Read the sections on teacher involvement and authentication.
2. Confirm your internal deadline and the board's deadline with your exams officer.
3. Speak to the whole group about the rules, give them written guidance, and have them sign an acknowledgement.
4. Set up the shared tracker and diary templates before the first lesson.
5. Share exemplar projects (OCR's own, or anonymised work from previous years, if allowed by your school) so students can see the standard.

## If a student falls behind
- At **one missed checkpoint:** a quick catch-up conversation and a revised short deadline.
- At **two missed checkpoints:** meet with the student and tell the head of sixth form or equivalent, and contact home.
- At **three or more:** agree a recovery plan, possibly reducing the scope of the project (which is a perfectly acceptable way to protect the marks), while still keeping within the rules on what guidance you can give.
