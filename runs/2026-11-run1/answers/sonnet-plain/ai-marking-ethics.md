# Guidance: Using AI Tools such as ChatGPT to Support Marking

*Assumptions: this is a school (UK-style) drafting a staff policy. The guidance below should be checked by your Data Protection Officer and Senior Leadership Team before it is issued, and aligned with your existing data protection, safeguarding and assessment policies.*

## 1. Purpose

AI tools can save time, but student work is personal data and marking is a professional judgement. This guidance lets teachers benefit from AI without putting students, the school or the integrity of grades at risk.

## 2. The headline rules

1. **Do not paste identifiable student work into a public AI tool.** Free, consumer versions of ChatGPT and similar tools may store prompts and use them to train models. Once submitted, you cannot get the data back.
2. **The teacher makes every judgement.** AI may assist, but it never decides a mark, grade, level, target or comment that reaches a student or parent.
3. **Only use tools the school has approved.** Approved means a data protection impact assessment has been done, a data processing agreement is in place, and the tool is configured so that student inputs are not used for training.
4. **Be open about it.** Students and parents should know, in plain language, whether and how AI is used in feedback.

## 3. Data protection (UK GDPR)

- Student essays are personal data. They often contain names, details of home life, health, family, beliefs or safeguarding disclosures (including special category data).
- Pasting them into an unapproved tool is likely to be a data breach, even if well intentioned.
- Removing the name is not enough. Essays can identify a student through their content, a class list, a teacher's own context or a distinctive writing style. Treat anonymised work as personal data unless it genuinely cannot be traced.
- If a teacher realises they have pasted identifiable work into an unapproved tool, they should tell the Data Protection Officer straight away. This is a no-blame report; speed matters.
- Do not upload work from students under 13 or work containing any safeguarding content to any AI tool.

## 4. Intellectual property and consent

- Students own the copyright in their work. Check your school's position on whether it may be submitted to third-party systems, and whether parents have been informed.
- Exam board coursework and NEA: follow the exam board's rules (JCQ guidance). AI must not be used to determine or moderate marks on formal assessed work unless the board explicitly permits it.

## 5. Accuracy and fairness: why AI cannot be the marker

- AI tools can be inconsistent. The same essay can receive different marks on different runs.
- They often misapply mark schemes, invent quotations or "find" evidence that is not there, and reward length and polished surface style over genuine insight.
- They can disadvantage students with SEND, EAL students, and writers whose style or dialect differs from the standard patterns the tool was trained on.
- They have no knowledge of what you taught, a student's starting point or the context behind a piece of work.
- Therefore AI-generated marks or grades must never be recorded, reported or used for decisions on sets, predictions, interventions or reports.

## 6. What is acceptable (with an approved tool)

- Generating **generic** feedback banks, comment stems and "what went well / even better if" templates from your own mark scheme, with no student work involved.
- Drafting model answers, success criteria, checklists and common-misconception lists.
- Turning your own rough notes into a clear, polite comment, which you then check and edit.
- Analysing **class-level patterns** from anonymised, aggregated data you have typed in yourself (for example "most students struggled with embedded quotations").
- Making a rubric student-friendly.

## 7. What is not acceptable

- Pasting full essays or extracts of identifiable student work into any unapproved or consumer AI tool.
- Using AI output as the mark, grade or level.
- Sending AI-written feedback to students or parents unchecked.
- Using AI "detectors" as evidence that a student has cheated. They are unreliable and prone to false accusations. Academic integrity concerns must follow normal procedures and be based on professional judgement and conversation with the student.

## 8. If your school approves a specific tool

If SLT approves a tool (for example an education-licensed product with a signed agreement and no training on inputs), add the conditions that apply to it:

- Log in with a school account only, never a personal one.
- Remove names and any other identifying details before use, and check the essay for hidden identifiers.
- Use it to produce a *draft* of feedback only; read and edit every comment.
- Spot-check output against your own marking on a sample to make sure it is reliable and fair.
- Keep a short record of what the tool was used for (department-level is fine).

## 9. Other ways to speed up marking

AI is not the only option. Many teachers save time without it:

- Whole-class feedback sheets instead of individual comments
- Comment codes and marking symbols
- Live marking during lessons
- Mini-assessments targeted at one skill
- Comparative judgement within a department
- Shared comment banks that the department builds together

## 10. Review and training

- Staff should receive short training on this policy and on the risks above before using any AI tool.
- Review this guidance each term in the first year; the technology and regulations are changing quickly.
- Direct questions to the Data Protection Officer or the named AI lead.

## One-line summary for staff

*Think of a public AI tool as a stranger's notebook: never put student work into it, never let it decide a grade, and always check anything it drafts.*
