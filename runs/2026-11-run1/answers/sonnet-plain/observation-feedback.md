# Feedback: Year 9 lesson observation

*Assumptions: this is a developmental, supportive conversation with an early career teacher; about 15 minutes; you will agree next steps together. Written to be spoken, so adapt the wording to your own voice.*

## Opening

Thank you for having me in. I want to start with what I think is really strong, then we'll pick two things to work on, and we'll finish with what you'd like to try next. Before I say anything, how did you feel it went?

*(Listen first. Her own view will show you how self-aware she is, and it will make the points below easier to land.)*

## What went well

**Relationships.** The tone in the room was warm and respectful. Students were relaxed with you, they were happy to speak when you asked, and nobody was afraid of getting something wrong. That is hard to build and it is the foundation for everything else. Keep it.

**Explanation.** Your explanation of the topic was clear and well ordered. You were secure in the subject knowledge and you made the main idea easy to follow. Students could tell you knew what you were talking about.

## Focus 1: Students were passive for 25 minutes

Here is what I saw: for about 25 minutes the only person thinking out loud was you. The explanation was good, but listening is not the same as learning. Most students will nod along, and you can't tell who has understood until the end.

What I'd try:

- **Chunk it.** Break the 25 minutes into three or four short pieces of 5 to 7 minutes. After each piece, give students something to do for a minute or two: a quick question on mini-whiteboards, a one-line summary in their books, or turn to a partner and explain it in their own words.
- **Cut the talk.** Ask yourself which parts of the explanation students could work out or read for themselves. Even trimming 5 minutes gives you more time for practice.
- **Make it visible.** Give them a note-taking frame or a gap-fill to complete while you talk, so there is always something for them to do.

## Focus 2: Questions answered mostly by volunteers

Several hands went up each time and you took the first one, which is natural when you want momentum. The problem is that the same few students did the thinking, and the rest could stay hidden. It also meant you were getting a false picture of how well the whole class understood.

What I'd try:

- **Pose, pause, pounce, bounce.** Ask the question, give everyone 5 to 10 seconds of silent thinking time, then choose a student by name. Then ask a second student what they think of that answer.
- **No hands up** for most questions. Tell students at the start that you'll be choosing, so it feels normal rather than a punishment.
- **Mini-whiteboards or hands-up voting** (A/B/C) for check-for-understanding questions, so every student answers every time and you can see the whole room in a few seconds.
- **Think, pair, share** before you take answers, so quieter students can try an answer out safely first.

## Focus 3: The final task was too hard for about a third of the class

This links to the two points above. Because you had few checks along the way, you didn't find out who was unsure until they were stuck on the final task. About a third of the class couldn't get started, which means a lot of the final part of the lesson was lost for them.

What I'd try:

- **Use the checks to decide.** Use what you see on the whiteboards and in books to decide who is ready for the main task and who needs a bit more modelling first.
- **Scaffold the task rather than lower it.** Keep the same goal for everyone, but give a sentence starter, a worked example, a word bank or a partly completed answer for those who need it. Have a "challenge" extension for those who finish quickly.
- **Model first.** Do the first example together on the board ("I do, we do, you do") so students have seen what a good answer looks like before they try alone.
- **Build in a quick low-stakes start.** The first question of the task should be one almost everybody can answer, so everyone gets started with success.

## Prioritising

That's a lot, so I wouldn't try everything at once. If I had to pick **one thing for next week**: break your talk into chunks with a short student task after each one. This will also fix a lot of the questioning and task-difficulty issues, because you'll see understanding much earlier. For **the week after**, add no-hands-up questioning.

## Next steps

- Plan one lesson this week with your talk chunked into pieces of no more than 7 minutes, each followed by a quick student task.
- Try cold calling after thinking time in at least two parts of that lesson.
- I'll pop in for 15 minutes next week, or you could observe a colleague who uses mini-whiteboards well, and we'll catch up for 10 minutes afterwards.

## Closing

You have the hardest things already: the relationships and a clear, confident explanation. What I've suggested is about moving the thinking from you to the students, and that is very learnable. What questions do you have, and which of these feels most doable to you?
