# Questioning Plan: Finding the nth Term of a Linear Sequence (Year 8)

## The problem
"Does everyone understand?" gets the same three students answering because it is a yes/no question about a feeling, and the students who would say "no" are least likely to say so in front of the class. The fix is to replace it with questions that need a **specific answer from every student**, so you can see who has it and who hasn't.

## Assumptions
- One 50 to 60 minute lesson, with the sequences already met in an earlier lesson (term-to-term rules, continuing patterns).
- Mini whiteboards are available. If not, use scrap paper or the back of exercise books.
- The lesson goal: find the nth term of an increasing and decreasing linear sequence, and use it to find a term or test whether a number is in the sequence.

## Five habits that replace "does everyone understand?"
1. **Everyone answers at once.** Mini whiteboards, "boards up on three". You see every answer in a few seconds.
2. **Think time first.** Ask the question, wait 5 to 10 seconds in silence, *then* choose who answers. Tell students you will do this.
3. **No hands up for most questions.** Choose students at random (names on lolly sticks or a shuffled list). Tell the class at the start, so it feels fair and not like a trap.
4. **Pair talk first.** "Tell your partner your answer and why. 30 seconds." Then cold-call. Students who are unsure now have something to say.
5. **Make wrong answers useful.** "Interesting, who can tell me what that student might have been thinking?" Wrong answers are the thing you most want to see.

## Questions through the lesson

### 1. Starter (5 minutes): activate prior knowledge
Boards up:
- "Here is the sequence 5, 8, 11, 14. Write down the next two terms."
- "What is the rule to get from one term to the next?" (Expect: add 3.)
- "What is the 10th term? Don't write it all out, tell me how you could get there faster." (Pair talk, then cold-call. Aim to hear "multiply by 3 and add something".)

*What you learn:* who is secure on term-to-term rules. Anyone who can't do this will struggle with everything after it.

### 2. Introducing the idea (10 minutes): where the difference comes from
Model with 3, 5, 7, 9. Draw a table of position (n) against term.

- "The difference is 2. Why might the nth term start with 2n?" (Pair talk. Take two answers.)
- "What is 2n for n = 1, 2, 3, 4?" Boards up: 2, 4, 6, 8.
- "How far is each of those from the real term?" Boards up: +1 each time.
- "So what is the nth term?" Boards up: 2n + 1.
- **Hinge question (boards up, multiple choice):** "The nth term of a sequence is 4n - 1. What is the 5th term?" A: 19. B: 20. C: 24. D: 9. Make the wrong options map to real errors: 20 is 4 x 5 and forgetting the -1; 9 is 4 + 5; 24 is 4 x 5 + 4 or similar. Do not put the correct answer first.

*What you learn:* the wrong options tell you exactly what a student misunderstood. Count the boards in each group before you speak.

### 3. Guided practice (10 minutes): "I do, we do, you do"
- "I'll find the nth term of 7, 10, 13, 16. First step?" (Cold-call. Expected: find the difference.)
- "The difference is 3. What goes in front of the n?" (Pair talk, boards.)
- "What is 3n for these terms? What do I add or subtract?" (Boards.)
- Students try 6, 11, 16, 21 on boards alone. Walk the room and look at the whiteboards before anyone speaks. Choose one student with a correct answer and one with a common error to explain.

### 4. Misconception checks (10 minutes)
Pose each as a "who is right?" question, not a recall question. Students vote with boards or by standing on one side of the room.
- **Decreasing sequences:** "20, 17, 14, 11. Sam says the nth term is 3n + 17. Ali says -3n + 23. Who is right? How could you check?" (Expect: test n = 1.)
- **Check the formula:** "You found 4n + 2 for the sequence 6, 10, 14, 18. How can you check without finding the whole sequence again?" (Substitute n = 1 and n = 2.)
- **Is it in the sequence?** "Is 100 a term of 3n + 1? Is 50?" (Pair talk. Ask for method before answer.)
- **Common error:** "A student writes n + 3 for the sequence 3, 6, 9, 12. What did they notice, and what did they miss?" (Pair talk.)

### 5. Independent practice (15 minutes)
- Set a graded set of questions (increasing, then decreasing, then non-integer difference if you want a stretch).
- While they work, ask each of **four or five students** you didn't hear from earlier: "Show me how you found the number in front of n." Aim to speak to the quiet ones.
- Leave a challenge for the finishers: "Make up a sequence whose nth term is 5n - 3, and swap with your partner. Can they get back to the nth term?"

### 6. Plenary (5 minutes): exit ticket
Instead of "does everyone understand?", have every student write on a slip, boards, or in the back of their book:
1. Find the nth term of 9, 13, 17, 21.
2. Find the nth term of 30, 27, 24, 21.
3. One sentence: "The step I find hardest is..."

Collect or photograph the slips. Sort into three piles: secure, nearly, not yet. That tells you who to start with next lesson.

## Quick scripts you can use
- To replace "does everyone understand?": "Show me on your board how you'd find the nth term of 4, 9, 14, 19. Three, two, one, boards up."
- To prompt a quieter student without embarrassing them: "Take 20 seconds with your partner, then I'm going to ask one of you to tell me your first step."
- To handle a wrong answer: "Thank you, that's really useful. Who can tell me why someone might have got that?"
- To stop the same three answering: "I've heard from a few people already. I'd like to hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."

## What to watch for (typical errors)
- Writing the difference as the whole answer (writing "3" or "+3" instead of "3n").
- Putting the term-to-term rule in the wrong place (n + 3 for the sequence 3, 6, 9).
- Getting the sign wrong on decreasing sequences (writing 3n instead of -3n).
- Forgetting to find the constant (writing 2n for 3, 5, 7, 9).
- Substituting n incorrectly when checking (e.g. 4n - 1 for n = 5 giving 4 x 5 - 1 = 19, but some write 45 - 1).

## Making it stick
Pick one of the habits (I recommend "think time, pair talk, then cold-call") and use it for a week before adding another. Teaching the class how you'll be asking questions takes a minute, and it will pay off in every lesson.
