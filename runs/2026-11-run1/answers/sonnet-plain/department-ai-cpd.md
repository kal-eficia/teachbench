# Running a 45-minute maths department meeting on AI-written differentiated worksheets

**Goal of the meeting:** by the end, everyone has used AI to produce one worksheet, checked it using an agreed method, and signed up to a small, time-limited trial with clear rules.

**Assumptions:** 5-8 colleagues, a shared laptop or personal devices, a free or school-approved AI tool, and one topic they will all be teaching soon. The two sceptics are about students being lazy; the third is about mistakes. These are different concerns, so you address them differently.

## Before the meeting (10 minutes of your time)
- Send a short note: "We'll try something practical, not decide anything in the abstract. Please bring a topic you'll teach in the next two weeks and one worksheet you currently use or would like to improve."
- Decide which tool you'll use and check it is approved by your school (data protection and safeguarding). Tell staff clearly: **no student names or personal data go into any AI tool.**
- Prepare one AI-generated worksheet in advance that contains **two deliberate-looking errors you noticed** (or pick a real one from your own testing). This will be your demonstration for the error concern.
- Print a one-page **checking protocol** (see below) and a one-page **prompt template**.

## Agenda
| Time | Item | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Frame and ground rules | Set the tone |
| 5-12 | Name the concerns and agree the question | Listen first |
| 12-17 | Demo: errors in a live worksheet | Make the risk concrete |
| 17-35 | Pairs try it with a real topic and check it | Hands-on |
| 35-42 | Compare and decide the rules | Agree the protocol |
| 42-45 | Commit to a trial | Next steps |

## 0-5: Frame (5 min)
Say something like:
- "We are not deciding whether AI is good or bad. We are deciding whether it saves us enough time to produce better differentiated work, **as long as we can trust the maths**. Today we test that."
- Ground rules: we judge it by what we see, not by assumptions; every concern is valid; nothing goes to students unless a teacher has checked it.
- Define the scope: **teachers use AI to draft resources. Students are not being given AI to do their work.** This sets the lazy-students concern into context straight away.

## 5-12: Name the concerns (7 min)
Ask each of the two sceptical colleagues and the worried colleague to say their concern in one sentence. Write them on the board and then restate them as questions the trial can answer:
1. **"Will it make students lazy?"** becomes: "Will the resources we produce encourage thinking or just copying? And what do we do if students use AI themselves?"
2. **"Will it produce errors?"** becomes: "How often, what kinds, and can we catch them quickly?"
3. Add the upside: **"Does it save us enough time or improve the differentiation?"**

Say that the laziness concern deserves a real answer, and that you'll come back to it in the decision phase. Make it clear that using AI to draft a worksheet does not by itself change what students are asked to do. The quality of the questions and how the worksheet is used does.

## 12-17: Demo of errors (5 min)
Show a pre-prepared AI worksheet on a topic (for example solving linear equations, 3 levels). Ask the department to find any mistakes in 3 minutes, in pairs. Typical issues to look for: a wrong answer in the key, a question with no integer solution when one was intended, difficulty that does not actually ramp, a repeated question, or unrealistic context.

Outcome to draw out: errors **do** happen, and they are findable. This validates the worried colleague and shows that the check takes minutes, not hours. Introduce the checking protocol:

**Checking protocol (draft, to be agreed):**
1. Teacher solves every question independently (or uses a separate trusted answer source) before looking at the AI answer key.
2. Check the difficulty progression: does each level really differ?
3. Check the wording and context for age-appropriateness and curriculum fit.
4. Never give out a worksheet that has not been checked by the teacher who will use it.
5. Log any error found (in a shared document) so we learn which prompts and topics are risky.
6. Where possible, ask the AI to produce the worksheet **and** a worked-solution key, then check the key.

## 17-35: Hands-on in pairs (18 min)
Pair each sceptic with a more positive colleague if you can. Each pair:
1. Picks a topic they will teach soon (2 min).
2. Uses the prompt template to generate a three-tier worksheet (5 min).

**Prompt template (print this):**
> "Create a Year [X] maths worksheet on [topic] for the [exam board/scheme] curriculum. Make three levels: Support (scaffolded, with worked first example), Core, and Challenge (reasoning and problem-solving). 6 questions per level. Include a separate answer key with brief working. Use UK spelling, £ and metric units, and no names of real people."

3. Check it with the protocol: solve the questions, mark up errors, note how long it took (7 min).
4. Improve it: ask the AI to fix one issue or adjust difficulty, or edit by hand (4 min).

During this time circulate. Prompt the sceptics with: "What would you change so students have to think?" Ask the laziness sceptics to look at the **task design**: Are there enough "explain why" and "spot the mistake" tasks? Can they ask the AI to add reasoning questions or error-spotting items? This shifts the focus from AI to the quality of tasks, which is what actually affects whether students think or just copy.

## 35-42: Debrief and decide (7 min)
Ask each pair for one sentence: what did you find (time, errors, quality)?
Then address the concerns directly:
- **Errors:** "How many did we find? How long did it take to check? Is that less time than writing from scratch?" Agree the checking protocol as department policy.
- **Laziness:** distinguish two issues.
  - *Teacher shortcuts:* we stay responsible. The protocol means a teacher always reviews and adapts.
  - *Student reliance on AI:* this is a wider policy question (homework, classwork, assessments) and not what we are trialling today. Note it as a separate agenda item and move on. For the worksheets themselves, we can design tasks that need working shown, explanations, and in-class completion.
- **Value:** "Did it give us something we would not have had time to make?" (for example a third, scaffolded version).

## 42-45: Commit to a small trial (3 min)
Agree, and write on the board:
- **What:** each teacher uses AI to draft **one** differentiated worksheet over the next two weeks, and checks it using the protocol.
- **Who:** all of us, or volunteers plus one sceptic as "critical friend".
- **Log:** a shared document where we record topic, time taken, errors found, and whether students used it well.
- **Review:** 15 minutes at a department meeting in three weeks. Then decide whether to continue, extend or stop.
- **Rules:** no student data in the tool; teacher checks everything; we share good prompts and flag problem ones.

## After the meeting
- Send a one-page summary within 24 hours: the protocol, the prompt template, the log link and the review date.
- Make the log easy (a simple table: topic, tool used, time to produce, time to check, errors found, comments).
- Check in informally with the sceptics after a week. Acknowledge their concerns publicly if they turn out to be right, for example if errors are common on a particular topic.
- Keep the stakes low. A two-week trial that can be stopped is much easier to agree to than a policy.

## Why this approach works
- It turns arguments about AI into evidence from your own department.
- It respects both concerns: the protocol answers the error worry, and focusing on task design answers the laziness worry.
- It is short, practical, and everyone leaves with something useful (a worksheet) so the meeting is not seen as a waste of time.
