# A Christmas Carol: 20-Mark Question, Levels-Based Mark Scheme and Exemplar

*Assumptions: AQA GCSE English Literature style (Paper 1, Section B). The live AQA question is worth 30 marks plus 4 for spelling, punctuation and grammar, so this is a shortened 20-mark version suitable for a mock, timed practice or a mid-unit assessment. It follows the AQA approach of an extract-based task plus whole-text reference, and assesses AO1 and AO2, with AO3 (context) woven in. Suggested time: about 30 minutes.*

---

## The question

**Read the following extract from Stave Five of *A Christmas Carol*.** (Teacher: use the passage beginning "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year" and running to "...Scrooge was better than his word", or your preferred extract showing Scrooge's transformation on Christmas morning.)

**Starting with this extract, explain how far you think Dickens presents Scrooge as a changed man.**

Write about:
- how Dickens presents Scrooge in this extract
- how Dickens presents Scrooge in the novella as a whole.

**[20 marks]**

(Assessment objectives: AO1 read, understand and respond to the text, using textual references; AO2 analyse the language, form and structure used by a writer to create meanings and effects; AO3 show understanding of the relationship between the text and its context. Marks are allocated approximately AO1 8, AO2 8, AO3 4.)

---

## Levels-based mark scheme

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 5: Convincing, critical analysis and exploration** | 17-20 | Critical, exploratory personal response with a well-developed, sustained argument about how far Scrooge changes. Precise, judicious references used throughout, including from both the extract and whole text. Detailed, perceptive analysis of Dickens' methods (language, form and structure), with subject terminology used accurately and with sophistication. Ideas about context are integrated seamlessly and used to deepen the interpretation. |
| **Level 4: Clear, consistent understanding** | 13-16 | Thoughtful, developed response with a clear line of argument. Well-chosen references from the extract and whole text. Clear explanation of Dickens' methods and their effects, with appropriate terminology. Consistent links to context, which are relevant and support the argument. |
| **Level 3: Explained, structured comments** | 9-12 | Sound response with an explained, structured argument. Relevant references from the extract and some from elsewhere. Explanation of some of Dickens' methods, with some terminology. Some relevant links to context. |
| **Level 2: Some demonstration of understanding** | 5-8 | Supported response with some relevant comments on character and ideas. References are present but may be descriptive. Some awareness of Dickens' methods, with simple or occasionally inaccurate terminology. Limited or implicit awareness of context. |
| **Level 1: Simple, limited comments** | 1-4 | Simple comments on Scrooge, mostly retelling. Few or generalised references. Little or no awareness of methods or context. |
| **0** | 0 | No rewardable material. |

**Notes for markers**
- "How far" invites evaluation: reward a clear judgement (for example Scrooge changes fully; changes but is shaped by fear first; or remains partly bound to his past in the way he expresses joy). Do not penalise any reasoned view.
- Reward whole-text coverage. A response confined to the extract cannot reach the top of Level 4.
- Reward quotation that is embedded and analysed over quotation that is merely listed.
- Do not credit a list of contextual facts that is not linked to the question.

## Indicative content

Examiners should be aware that students may respond in many valid ways. The following points are possibilities, not a checklist.

### The extract (Stave Five)
- The transformation is shown through energy and exuberance: exclamatory sentences, "I don't know what day of the month it is! I don't know how long I've been among the Spirits."
- Simile and comparison show childlike joy: "I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel, I am as merry as a schoolboy." The triple structure and reversal of his earlier cold, aged image suggest rebirth.
- Contrast with the opening description (a "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner"): sibilance and harsh verbs replaced by light, playful sounds.
- Laughter replaces the earlier dry, mean silence: "a splendid laugh, a most illustrious laugh."
- Action shows change: he sends the turkey to the Cratchits, gives to charity, attends Fred's party.
- Narrative voice becomes warm and approving: "Scrooge was better than his word."

### The whole text
- **Stave One:** Scrooge as isolated, miserly and dismissive of the poor ("Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses?"); the cold imagery ("the cold within him froze his old features").
- **Staves Two to Four:** the Ghosts prompt his gradual change: sympathy for young Scrooge, regret over Belle, concern for Tiny Tim, terror at his own grave.
- **Structure:** the five-stave pattern of redemption narrative; the Ghosts of past, present and future as a moral journey; the cyclical return to Christmas.
- **Form:** a novella with the conventions of a Christmas or ghost story and a moral fable; the happy ending reinforces the message.
- **Whether the change is believable:** it happens in one night, supernaturally, which may make it symbolic rather than realistic. Some students may argue it is complete (he becomes "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man"), others that Dickens needs it to be dramatic to persuade readers.

### Context (AO3)
- Victorian attitudes to poverty, the Poor Law and workhouses; Dickens' concern for children and the poor (Ignorance and Want).
- Christian ideas of repentance, redemption and charity; the revival of Christmas as a festival of generosity.
- Dickens' purpose: to move wealthy readers to take responsibility for the poor.

## Model answer extract (top level)

*This is an extract, not a complete response, written to show the quality of analysis and argument expected at Level 5.*

> Dickens presents Scrooge's transformation not as a quiet change of heart but as an explosive release of everything his earlier life had frozen. In the novella's opening, Scrooge is a "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner", a list of harsh verbs whose relentless accumulation and sibilance mimic the hoarding that defines him. By Stave Five, the syntax itself has changed. Scrooge's sentences tumble out as exclamations, and his triple simile, "as light as a feather", "as happy as an angel", "as merry as a schoolboy", replaces the weight of money with weightlessness, and the hardened old man with a child. The repetition of "I am" suggests a man newly discovering that he can be, rather than merely have. Dickens even reverses the cold imagery that dominated Stave One, in which "the cold within him froze his old features", so that the frost of Scrooge's character gives way to warmth and light.
>
> However, Dickens is careful to make the change more than emotional. Scrooge's joy is proved through action: the turkey sent to the Cratchits, the donation to the charity collector he had earlier dismissed, and above all his appearance at Fred's table. This matters because Dickens' Victorian readers, many of them comfortable and complacent about poverty, are shown that redemption is not a feeling but a practice of generosity. The narrator's closing verdict, that Scrooge "became as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew", uses the repeated "good" to turn Scrooge's personal journey into a model of social responsibility. It is therefore difficult to argue that Dickens leaves any ambiguity about whether Scrooge has changed; rather, the more interesting question is why the change is so complete. The structure of the novella, in which three Spirits lead Scrooge through his own past, present and likely future, makes the transformation feel earned, while the suddenness of its final celebration suggests that Dickens is writing a moral fable in which readers are meant to be convinced less by realism than by the urgency of the message.

*Why this reaches Level 5:* a clear, sustained argument that directly addresses "how far"; precise, embedded quotations from the extract and the whole text; analysis of specific methods (verb lists, sibilance, triple structure, repetition, reversal of cold imagery, narrative voice, structure, form); and context (Victorian readers, attitudes to poverty) integrated into the argument rather than added on.
