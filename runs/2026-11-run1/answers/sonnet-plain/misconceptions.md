# Year 10 electricity quiz: what to do about it

## What the data is telling you
Most of these errors are misconceptions, not gaps, and the confidence levels matter.

| Q | Wrong idea | Confidence | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | Current gets used up in a bulb | 14/26 wrong, 9 very confident | The most important problem. A firmly held, well-known misconception: students picture current as a "stuff" that is consumed. |
| 8 | Battery stores electricity or charge | 12 wrong, 8 confident | Another firm misconception, linked to Q3: electricity is a substance that is stored and used up. |
| 5 | Parallel bulbs dimmer | 11 wrong, mostly not confident | Weakly held. Students are guessing, probably by analogy with series. Likely to fix with a demonstration. |
| 6 | Voltmeter measures current | 4 wrong, 3 blank | A small group with a vocabulary or recall gap. |
| 9 | Resistance unit: amps / volts | 3 wrong | Recall of units. Minor. |

The confident wrong answers (Q3, Q8) need the most deliberate teaching. Confidently held misconceptions are not fixed by repeating the correct answer. They usually need the student to see their model fail.

## Plan

### 1. Q3 and Q8: current and energy, a short sequence (1 to 2 lessons)
- **Predict, observe, explain.** Show a simple series circuit with ammeters before and after a bulb (a simulation such as PhET Circuit Construction Kit works well if you lack equipment). Ask students to predict the second reading and vote, then reveal. Equal readings contradict "used up".
- **Give them a better model.** A rope loop or "bike chain" model or a coal-trucks-on-a-train model: charge goes round and round, but energy is carried by it and transferred at the bulb. Name what changes: energy is transferred, current is not used up.
- **Fix the Q8 wording.** A battery stores chemical energy, and the circuit transfers this to the components. Use a sentence frame: "The battery transfers ___ energy to the ___ by ___" and practise with different components.
- Check by re-asking Q3 in a new setting (current at three points in a series circuit with two bulbs) rather than the same wording.

### 2. Q5: parallel brightness (half a lesson)
- Demonstrate with real bulbs or a simulation: one bulb on a cell, then two in parallel across the same cell. Brightness is unchanged. Then contrast with two in series, which are dimmer.
- Reason from the idea that each parallel branch has the full battery potential difference, so each bulb behaves as if it were alone (the battery supplies more total current).
- Because the students were unsure, a clear demonstration plus a "why" explanation should be enough.

### 3. Q6 and Q9: quick recall (starters)
- Two or three retrieval starters on a spaced schedule: "Voltmeter measures ___ (potential difference), connected in ___ (parallel)", "Ammeter measures ___ in ___". Include the unit table: current (A), potential difference (V), resistance (ohm).
- Use a matching task with quantity, symbol, unit and instrument.
- Identify the 3 blank students and check them individually.

### 4. Check it has worked
- Run a short diagnostic multiple-choice quiz a week later, where each wrong option represents one of these misconceptions (for example, "current is smaller after the bulb", "brightness halves", "the battery stores current"), with a confidence rating on each answer.
- Re-test a few weeks later as well, since students often revert.

## Wider points
- Q3 and Q8 probably share a root: the idea of electricity as a thing that gets used up. Treat them together instead of as two separate corrections.
- Share the findings with students: "Many of you thought... here is why that is a sensible idea, and why it does not work." That normalises the mistake and helps motivation.
- Look at whether other electricity questions in the unit (series versus parallel current and potential difference) depend on the same misconception.
- Record which students were confidently wrong, since they are the ones to check first in the retest.
