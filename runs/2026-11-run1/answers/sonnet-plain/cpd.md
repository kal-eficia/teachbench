# Staff session plan: Using AI to plan lessons (60 minutes, 15 teachers)

**Assumptions:** a normal classroom or hall with a screen and wifi; teachers have their own laptops or can use their phones; your school allows access to at least one general-purpose AI assistant on staff devices; you will ask staff to bring one real lesson they have to plan in the next fortnight. If your school has a policy on AI, check it and quote it on slide 1.

## Aims
By the end, every teacher should have:
1. Used an AI tool themselves, on a real lesson.
2. A simple prompt structure they can reuse.
3. A clear sense of what AI is good and bad at, and what is still their professional judgement.
4. One thing to try this week.

The session is deliberately hands-on. Sceptics are convinced by results on their own work, not by a pitch.

## Before the session (15 minutes of prep)
- Pre-test your demo prompt so there are no surprises. Have a screenshot backup in case wifi fails.
- Ask staff in advance (a two-line email): "Bring a lesson or topic you have to plan in the next two weeks."
- Make a one-page handout (see end) and print it.
- Create pairs in advance, mixing subjects and confidence levels. Put sceptics with a curious partner, not with each other.
- Check your school's data and AI policy. Be ready to say what staff may and may not enter.

## Running order

| Time | Section | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:05 | **Frame it** | State the aim: "Save time on the planning you already do, while you stay in charge." Say openly that scepticism is reasonable. Give three ground rules (below). |
| 0:05-0:15 | **Live demo** | Plan a lesson live from a topic a non-specialist can follow. Show a weak prompt first, then a strong one, and let the difference speak for itself. |
| 0:15-0:20 | **The prompt recipe** | Teach one reusable structure. |
| 0:20-0:40 | **Pairs: try it on your own lesson** | Each pair works on one teacher's lesson, then swaps. |
| 0:40-0:50 | **Check it: what did the AI get wrong?** | Share failures and fixes. Discuss limits, accuracy and bias. |

### 0:00-0:05 Frame it
Three ground rules to say aloud:
1. **Never enter pupil names, personal data or anything confidential.** Describe "a Year 9 class of 28, three with reading difficulties" rather than naming anyone.
2. **You are the expert.** AI produces a first draft. You check it, correct it and own it.
3. **You do not have to like it.** Try it once on something real, then decide.

### 0:05-0:15 Live demo
Use a topic outside your own subject so no one thinks "that only works for you".
- **Weak prompt:** "Plan a lesson on the water cycle." Show the generic output.
- **Strong prompt:** use the recipe below with real context. Show how much better the result is.
- Then ask a follow-up in front of the group ("make the starter more active", "add a differentiated task for pupils who finish early", "give me three common misconceptions"). Conversation is the point: the first answer is rarely the best.
- Deliberately point out one mistake in the output. This models checking and reassures sceptics that you are not claiming it is perfect.

### 0:15-0:20 The prompt recipe
Put it on the board:

> **Context** (who, what year, how long, what they already know) +
> **Task** (what you want) +
> **Constraints** (resources, class needs, style) +
> **Format** (table, steps, timings) +
> **Then ask it to improve** (follow-ups).

Example: *"I teach Year 8 geography, 50 minutes, a mixed class of 28 with five pupils who read well below age expectations. They know what a river is but not erosion. Plan a lesson on river erosion with a short starter, one practical or modelling task, and a mini quiz. No equipment beyond a whiteboard and printouts. Give me a table with timings."*

### 0:20-0:40 Pairs on a real lesson
- Teacher A drives the AI on their own lesson for about 8 minutes, with Teacher B reading over their shoulder and asking "what would you want it to change?".
- Swap for the next 8 minutes.
- Final 4 minutes: each marks the output green (use as is), amber (edit) or red (bin).
- Circulate. Help anyone stuck (usually because the prompt is too vague) and make sure nobody is left alone with a blank screen. For the very nervous, offer a ready-made prompt they can paste in and modify.
- Optional prompts for those who finish: "turn this into a worksheet", "write three exam-style questions with a mark scheme", "adapt this for pupils with EAL", "write a parent email about this topic".

### 0:40-0:50 What did it get wrong?
Ask: "Who found a mistake?" Gather examples (wrong facts, generic activities, unrealistic timings, American spelling, made-up references). Then cover:
- **Strengths:** first drafts, ideas, differentiation options, quiz questions, rewording, tidying.
- **Weaknesses:** factual errors, invented sources, curriculum or exam-board detail, tone, knowing your pupils.
- **Rule of thumb:** the more specific and checkable the task, the more you can trust it; always verify facts and exam-board content yourself.
- Cover any school policy: pupil data, using pupil work in AI tools, and what pupils may do.

Let sceptics speak. Valid points (accuracy, workload creep, pupils over-relying on it, fairness) deserve a straight answer, not a defence.

### 0:50-0:57 Commit
Each teacher completes: **"This week I will try AI for ______, and I will check ______."** Collect them verbally or on sticky notes. This turns a pleasant session into a changed habit.

### 0:57-1:00 Close
- Share the handout and any approved tool details.
- Offer a 20-minute drop-in or a shared channel for tips.
- Say when you will follow up.

## Handling a mixed room
- **Sceptics:** acknowledge the concerns, give them a job (the "fact-checker" in the pair), and let them judge the output on their own lesson. Do not try to convert them.
- **Nervous beginners:** ready-made prompt on paper, partner who has used it, no pressure to share.
- **Keen users:** challenge them to adapt a lesson for a specific pupil need, or to test where the tool fails.
- **Subject mix:** keep examples subject-neutral, and have one or two prompts ready in different subjects (maths, English, PE, languages).

## Handout (one page)
1. The five-part prompt recipe, with an example.
2. Five prompts to try: starters, differentiation, misconception check, quiz with mark scheme, rewording for accessibility.
3. A checking list: facts, curriculum fit, tone, timing, bias, suitability for my class.
4. What not to enter: pupil names, personal data, confidential information.
5. Where to get help and who to ask.

## Follow-up (two weeks later)
A short email: "What did you try? What worked? What didn't?" Share two or three real examples from colleagues, and run a 15-minute slot in a later meeting to share what people have learned.
