# Mid-lesson check: adding fractions with different denominators (Year 8)

**Assumptions:** students answer on mini whiteboards (or hands-up voting or a quick poll) so you see every answer at once, and the check takes about 2 minutes. Pick one option. All three are designed so that wrong answers tell you what the misconception is, not just that the student is wrong.

---

## Option 1: Calculate and show (fastest, most direct)

**Question:** Work out 2/3 + 1/4. Write your answer as a single fraction.

**Correct answer:** 11/12

**What wrong answers tell you:**

| Answer | What went wrong | Your response |
|---|---|---|
| 3/7 | Added tops and added bottoms | Go back. Re-teach why the denominators must match, with a drawing of unequal pieces |
| 3/12 | Found the common denominator but did not change the numerators | Brief fix: whatever you do to the bottom you do to the top. Then retry |
| 11/24 | Changed both fractions correctly but then added the denominators | Short fix: the denominator names the size of the pieces, so it stays the same when you add |
| 8/12 + 3/12 left unsimplified or unfinished | Method fine, not finished | Move on |
| 11/12 | Correct | Move on |

**Decision rule:** if at least about 80 percent show 11/12 (or the correct method with a small slip), move on. If a clear group wrote 3/7, go back to the whole class. If it is mostly small slips, pair the confident students with the others and move on.

---

## Option 2: Multiple choice with diagnostic wrong answers

**Question:** What is 2/3 + 1/4?

- A) 3/7
- B) 11/12
- C) 3/12
- D) 11/24

Students hold up fingers or show A to D on a whiteboard, or you use a poll.

**Correct answer:** B

**What each wrong answer means:**
- **A, 3/7:** adds numerators and denominators. The main misconception.
- **C, 3/12:** found a common denominator but forgot to scale the numerators.
- **D, 11/24:** scaled correctly, then added the denominators.

**Decision rule:** if most choose B, move on. If A is popular, go back to the idea that fractions must be the same size pieces before you can add. If C or D are the main wrong answers, the idea is mostly there, so do a quick recap on the last step and give another example.

**Tip:** ask one student who chose B and one who chose a wrong answer to explain their thinking. Do not say which is right until you have heard both.

---

## Option 3: Spot the mistake (tests understanding, not just procedure)

**Question:** Sam works out 1/2 + 1/3 and gets 2/5. **Without doing the full calculation, how can you tell Sam must be wrong?** Then work out the correct answer.

**Strong answers:**
- "1/2 is already half, and you're adding something to it, so the answer must be more than 1/2. 2/5 is less than 1/2."
- Or: "You can't add the bottoms because they're different size pieces."

**Correct calculation:** 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6

**What responses tell you:**
- **A student who sees the answer is too small** understands what adding fractions means, not just the steps. Move on.
- **A student who can redo it correctly but cannot say why Sam is wrong** has the procedure but not the meaning. Move on, but come back to the reasoning later in the lesson.
- **A student who thinks Sam is right** has the main misconception. Go back, using a drawing (a bar divided into halves and thirds).

**Decision rule:** if most can explain the error or give 5/6, move on. If many agree with Sam, go back.

---

## Which one to choose

- **Option 1** if you want speed and a visible, whole-class picture.
- **Option 2** if you want the quickest way to read the room and the wrong answers are easy to group.
- **Option 3** if you want to check for understanding, not just procedure, and you have an extra minute for discussion.

If you use one now, keep another for the end of the lesson as an exit check, using different numbers (for example 3/4 + 1/6 = 11/12, or 1/5 + 3/10 = 1/2 for a question where simplifying is needed).
