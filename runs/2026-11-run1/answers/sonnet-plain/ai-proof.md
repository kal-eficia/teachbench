# Replacing "Write an essay explaining the causes of WW1" for Year 9

You are right to be wary. That title is the most generic essay prompt there is, and an AI will produce a competent answer in seconds. Rather than trying to ban AI, set homework where the thinking has to be visible and personal to your class, and where AI output is easy to spot or simply not much use.

**Assumptions:** You see the class at least weekly, you can spend 5 to 10 minutes of lesson time on follow-up, and you want homework that takes about 30 to 45 minutes. Pick one of the options below, or combine two.

## Principle

Keep the content (causes of WW1) but change the **task** so that it needs:
- a decision or ranking the student must defend,
- something done in class that the student must refer to,
- a spoken or short follow-up in the next lesson, which shows who did the thinking.

## Option 1: Rank and defend (my top pick)

**Homework:** On a card, rank these six causes from most to least important in starting the war in 1914: alliances, militarism, imperial rivalry, nationalism, the assassination of Franz Ferdinand, the Schlieffen Plan / mobilisation timetables. Then write **120 words only** defending why your number 1 is more important than your number 2.

**Why it works:** The short word count forces precision. A ranking is a personal judgement. It's easy to compare with their lesson notes. In class next lesson, students stand on a line from "most important" to "least" and you ask two or three to defend their position out loud, with no notes. Students who let AI do it can't.

## Option 2: Fix the flawed answer

**Homework:** Give them a deliberately flawed paragraph (you write it, or generate one and plant errors). It might say the assassination was "the only cause", mix up the Triple Alliance and Triple Entente, and give no evidence. Students must:
1. Highlight three things that are wrong or weak.
2. Rewrite it so it's accurate, with at least two pieces of specific evidence from your lessons.
3. Add a sentence on what you would still want to find out.

**Why it works:** Spotting errors needs real knowledge. AI can do it too, but the task only makes sense with your specific text, and the evidence must come from your lessons.

## Option 3: Connect to what we did in class

**Homework:** "Use the timeline we built on Tuesday. Choose the one day or event between 1905 and 1914 that you think made war hardest to avoid. Explain your choice in a short paragraph, and say what could have happened differently."

Use a class-specific resource (a timeline, a map you drew, a class debate) that AI doesn't have access to. Ask them to refer to something specific from it.

## Option 4: Persuade a specific audience

**Homework:** "Write a 150-word letter from a diplomat in July 1914 to their foreign minister, arguing that their country should (or should not) support their ally. You must use at least three of the causes we have studied and show the diplomat's worries about what might happen."

Role-play tasks still use AI well, so add a spoken follow-up: next lesson they read the letter aloud and a classmate asks one question that the writer must answer on the spot.

## Option 5: Annotated source plus question

**Homework:** Give a primary source (e.g. the Kaiser's "blank cheque" telegram, a 1914 cartoon, or the Schlieffen Plan map). Students answer three questions in writing: What does this show? What cause does it link to? How far does it explain why war broke out?

Keep the source in your lesson so the question is tied to it.

## How to make any option AI-resistant

1. **Make a bit of the work happen in the room.** Start the next lesson with a 5-minute, closed-book low-stakes quiz or "explain it out loud" on the homework topic. If the homework was theirs, they can do it.
2. **Ask for process, not just product.** Have them hand in their plan, their draft notes or a photo of their rough work. A line: "Tell me one thing that surprised you or one thing you changed your mind about."
3. **Ask for opinion and justification.** "How far do you agree?" and "Which is most important?" beat "Explain the causes".
4. **Use local, personal or class-based hooks.** Refer to your lesson activities, your examples, your wording.
5. **Be transparent.** Tell them it's fine to use AI to check their understanding or quiz themselves, but the work you collect must be theirs and they must be able to talk about it. If you allow AI at some stage, set it as a task: "Ask an AI to explain the causes of WW1, then tell me two things it got wrong, vague or missing compared with what we learned."
6. **Mark for thinking.** Give credit for a clear line of argument, specific evidence, and honesty about uncertainty. A bland, list-like essay should score modestly.

## If you must set the essay

Make it a two-stage task: plan in class (15 minutes, handwritten, a mind map and a thesis sentence), then write the essay for homework, then spend 5 minutes in class on a quick "explain your argument to a partner". Compare the written work with the plan. Ask for the essay to answer a narrower question, for example: "Was the alliance system or nationalism more responsible for turning a Balkan crisis into a world war? Use at least three facts from our lessons."
