# First mentoring session: 20 minutes

**Student:** Year 11, predicted grade 4s, capable of 6s. Says she is "just not motivated" and revises by reading her notes.

**Assumptions:** this is your first meeting, so rapport matters more than fixing everything. The session ends with one small thing she chooses to try this week, and you will check in on it next time. Where I give wording, adapt it to how you normally talk.

## What this session is for

1. She feels listened to, not lectured.
2. You understand what "not motivated" means for her.
3. She experiences, rather than is told, that re-reading is a weak revision method.
4. She leaves with one small, specific action she picked herself.

Do not try to fix everything. One win in the first meeting beats a long plan she forgets.

## Timings at a glance

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-3 | Set the tone |
| 3-9 | Understand "not motivated" |
| 9-16 | Try a revision experiment |
| 16-19 | Choose one action |
| 19-20 | Close |

## 0-3 minutes: set the tone

Say what this is and what it is not. Something like:

> "This isn't about telling you off or going through grades. I'm here to help you work out what would make the next few months easier. We've got 20 minutes, and by the end I'd like you to leave with one small thing you want to try."

Then an easy opener, not about school results: "What's one subject you actually don't mind, and why?" The answer is useful data later (it tells you what conditions make her engage).

## 3-9 minutes: understand "not motivated"

"Not motivated" is a label, not a cause. Your job is to find what sits underneath it. Ask, and then genuinely listen:

1. **"When you say not motivated, what does that look like on a normal evening?"** (Does she not start? Start and drift? Avoid certain subjects?)
2. **"Was there a time this year you felt more switched on? What was different?"** (Looks for exceptions.)
3. **"On a scale of 1 to 10, how much do you want to do well in your GCSEs? Why not a lower number?"** (Her answer to "why not lower" is her own reason, which is more powerful than any you give.)
4. **"What's the hardest bit: starting, keeping going, or not knowing what to do?"**

What to listen for:
- *Doesn't know how to start or what to do:* a methods problem, most likely. This fits her revising by reading notes.
- *Feels she isn't improving, so why bother:* a belief problem. Reading notes feels like work but gives little result, which is demoralising.
- *Overwhelmed or anxious:* a confidence or workload problem.
- *Real low mood, or things going on at home:* follow your school's pastoral or safeguarding route, and do not try to solve it in this session.

Reflect back what you heard in one sentence: "So it sounds like it's less about not caring and more about not being sure it's working. Is that fair?" Let her correct you.

## 9-16 minutes: a revision experiment (do, don't tell)

She says she revises by reading notes. Don't criticise it. Run a quick test so she sees the result herself.

1. **Pick a topic she has studied, from any subject** (maths, English, whichever she chooses). Choose a half-page of her notes. (1 min)
2. **She reads it for 2 minutes.** (2 min)
3. **Cover it. She writes down, or says out loud, everything she can remember.** (2 min)
4. **She checks against the notes** and marks what she missed. (1 min)
5. **Ask:** "How did that feel compared to reading? How much did you get?" (1 min)

Then, lightly:

> "Reading feels easy because the notes are right there, so your brain thinks it knows it. Pulling it out of your head, like you just did, is the hard bit, and the hard bit is what makes it stick. You've just done more useful revision in 5 minutes than 20 minutes of rereading, and you found the gaps yourself."

Keep it short and non-judgemental. The aim is "oh, that's different", not "you've been doing it wrong".

## 16-19 minutes: choose one action

Offer two or three options, so she chooses:

- **Cover and write:** after reading a page of notes, cover it and write what you remember. Do it for 15 minutes on one subject.
- **Past questions:** do 3 exam questions on a topic, then mark with the mark scheme.
- **Flashcards or quick quiz:** 10 cards on one topic, three days in a row.

Make it small and concrete using this frame, in her words:

> "This week I'll [action], for [time], on [subject], on [which days]."

If she says "I'll try", ask: "How likely is it, out of 10, that you'll do that? If it's under 7, what would make it a bit easier?" Shrink it until she is at 8 or more. A tiny action done is more motivating than a big one skipped.

## 19-20 minutes: close

- Repeat her action back to her.
- Say when you will check in: "Next time, I'll ask how it went. No pressure if it didn't, we'll just work out why."
- End on something genuine you noticed: how she handled the experiment, how she described her reason for wanting to do well. Specific, not generic.

## After the session (2 minutes)

Note down three things: her reason for wanting to do well, which method she chose, and anything that suggested a non-academic issue. Start next session by asking about the action, before anything else.

## Notes

- **Say less than you think.** If you are talking for more than a third of the time, ask another question instead.
- **Avoid:** "You just need to try harder", comparing her to others, and mentioning her predicted grade as a threat.
- **If she gives short answers,** use the scale questions and the choice-based tasks, which are easier than open questions for a quiet student.
- **Later sessions:** build on what worked (a weekly plan, topic prioritisation using her mock results, practice with timed questions).
