# How the immune system fights a pathogen (Year 9)

**Assumptions:** one 50 to 60 minute lesson, mixed-ability class, no practical equipment needed. Adjust the pace to suit your group.

## Learning objectives
By the end, students can:
- Describe how the body stops pathogens getting in.
- Explain what white blood cells, antibodies, antitoxins and memory cells do.
- Explain why a second infection is usually milder than the first.
- Explain why antibiotics do not work on viruses.

## Starter (5 min): "Fortress body"
Ask: "You are surrounded by bacteria and viruses all day. Why aren't you ill all the time?" Take answers on mini whiteboards. Collect ideas: skin, snot, stomach acid, tears. Name these as the **first line of defence** (barriers). Anything that gets past them meets the **immune system**.

## Key idea to hook on to
Use a **security team** analogy, then point out where it breaks down.

| Part of the body | Security team analogy |
|---|---|
| Skin, mucus, stomach acid | Locked doors and gates |
| Phagocytes | Guards who grab and swallow intruders |
| Lymphocytes | Detectives who identify the intruder and make a custom weapon |
| Antibodies | Custom-made handcuffs that fit one intruder only |
| Antitoxins | Neutralising the poison the intruder releases |
| Memory cells | A "wanted poster" file kept for next time |

## Teaching sequence (about 25 min)

### 1. Pathogens (2 min)
Pathogens are microorganisms that cause disease: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists. Each pathogen has **antigens**, which are molecules on its surface that act like a name tag.

### 2. White blood cells (8 min)
Two jobs to teach, with a sketch of each:
- **Phagocytosis:** a phagocyte engulfs the pathogen and digests it. This is non-specific, meaning it works on almost anything.
- **Lymphocytes:** these recognise a specific antigen and make **antibodies**.

### 3. Antibodies (5 min)
- Each antibody is shaped to fit one antigen, like a key in a lock.
- They stick to the pathogen, clump pathogens together and mark them so phagocytes can destroy them.
- Common misconception: antibodies do not "kill" directly. They label and disable, and phagocytes finish the job.

### 4. Antitoxins (3 min)
Some bacteria cause harm by releasing **toxins**. White blood cells can also produce **antitoxins**, which bind to and neutralise these toxins. Quick contrast: antibodies target the pathogen, antitoxins target the poison.

### 5. Memory cells (5 min)
- After the first infection, some lymphocytes stay in the blood as **memory cells**.
- On a second exposure to the same antigen, they produce the right antibodies much faster and in greater quantity.
- You usually don't get ill, which is **immunity**.
- This is the principle behind vaccination, which you can set up as a follow-on lesson.

**Draw the graph:** antibody concentration against time, with a small, slow peak for the first infection and a larger, faster peak for the second. Students label "primary response" and "secondary response".

## The misconception: "antibiotics work on viruses" (8 min)
Tackle it directly rather than hoping it goes away.

**Elicit it first.** Show this statement and ask students to vote with thumbs up or down: *"If you have a cold, antibiotics will help you get better."* Expect plenty of thumbs up. Do not correct yet.

**Then explain:**
- **Antibiotics** (for example penicillin) work by damaging bacterial structures, such as cell walls or the machinery bacteria use to make proteins.
- **Viruses are not cells.** They have no cell wall to burst. They also live and reproduce *inside* your own cells, so a drug that attacked them would risk harming your cells too.
- So antibiotics have no effect on colds, flu, COVID-19 or chickenpox.
- Viruses are managed by the immune system, **vaccines**, rest, and sometimes specific **antiviral** drugs.

**Why it matters:** overusing antibiotics helps bacteria evolve **resistance**. Link this to MRSA and say that taking antibiotics for viral illness is one of the causes of the problem.

**Check:** thumbs vote again on the same statement, then ask one student to explain why in a single sentence.

## Check for understanding (5 min): quick quiz
Use mini whiteboards.
1. Which cells engulf pathogens? (phagocytes)
2. What is the name tag on a pathogen's surface? (antigen)
3. Which cells remember a pathogen? (memory cells)
4. What do antitoxins neutralise? (toxins)
5. True or false: antibiotics cure flu. (false)
6. Explain in one sentence why you rarely get chickenpox twice. (memory cells make antibodies quickly)

## Exit task / homework
Write a 4 to 5 sentence "story of an infection" following one bacterium from entering a cut to being cleared, using the words *antigen, phagocyte, lymphocyte, antibody, memory cell*. Stretch: add a sentence on why an antibiotic would be used here but not for a virus.

## Common errors to listen for
- "Antibodies eat bacteria." (phagocytes do the engulfing)
- "Antibiotics and antibodies are the same thing." Write both words on the board and underline the differences: antiBIOtic is a drug, antiBODY is made by your body.
- "Memory cells mean I can never be ill again." (only that same pathogen, and some viruses such as flu change their antigens)

## Differentiation
- **Support:** give a labelled diagram and sentence starters ("A phagocyte ... because ...").
- **Challenge:** ask why a new flu vaccine is needed most years (antigen variation), or why memory cells don't protect against a new strain.
