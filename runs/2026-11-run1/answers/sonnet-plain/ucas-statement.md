# Feedback on your personal statement opening (Medicine)

Thank you for sharing this. You are clearly motivated, and you have the right instinct to start with why you want to do medicine. Here is honest feedback to help you make this opening much stronger.

## What works

- It is clear and concise, and your motivation is sincere.
- You mention science, which is relevant.

## The main problem: it is general, and admissions tutors read it hundreds of times

Almost every medicine applicant says they have "always" wanted to help people, love science, or are hard-working. Because the sentences are claims rather than evidence, they do not tell the reader anything that distinguishes you from other applicants. Admissions tutors want to see **what you have done, what you learned, and how you think**, not a list of qualities.

### Specific points

1. **"Ever since I was a little girl, I have always been passionate..."** This is one of the most common openings in personal statements. It is also hard to believe, and it focuses on childhood rather than what you have done recently. Drop it.
2. **"Helping people" and "make a difference"** are broad. Many careers help people, so this does not show why medicine. Say what about medicine appeals to you, and back it up.
3. **"Medicine is the perfect career for me because I love science"** is a statement without evidence. Show it: which part of science, and what have you done about it? (An EPQ, a book, a lab project, an article, a lecture, a question you could not stop thinking about?)
4. **"I am a hard-working and dedicated person"** tells rather than shows. Delete the sentence and let your experiences demonstrate it instead.
5. **Repetition and padding:** three sentences make three similar claims. One concrete example does more than all three.

## How to fix it

Use this approach: **experience, reflection, link to medicine.**

1. Pick one specific moment or experience that really shaped your interest (work experience, volunteering in a care home, caring for a relative, a science topic you researched).
2. Describe it briefly and precisely: what happened, and what did you notice or do?
3. Reflect: what did it teach you about medicine, patients, teamwork, uncertainty, or yourself?
4. Connect that to what a doctor does and why it appeals to you.

### An example of the structure (not to be copied; use your own experience)

> "Sitting with a patient who was frightened of an upcoming procedure, I watched the nurse spend two minutes simply explaining each step in plain language. His breathing slowed. It made me realise that diagnosis and treatment matter, but so does the way a clinician communicates. That led me to read about shared decision-making, which I explored further in my EPQ on ..."

Notice that it is concrete, shows observation, and leads naturally into reflection and further reading, with no need to say "I am passionate" or "hard-working".

## Things to avoid

- Clichés ("ever since I was young", "passionate", "make a difference", "perfect career").
- Quotations from famous people at the start.
- Listing qualities. Show them through what you did.
- Overstating work experience. Be honest about what you observed and what you did.

## Next steps

1. Write down three real experiences that influenced you. Choose the one where you learned the most.
2. Rewrite the opening in 2 to 4 sentences around that moment.
3. Check every sentence: could any other applicant have written it? If yes, make it more specific.
4. Read it aloud. Does it sound like you?
5. Ask a teacher or a doctor to read the next draft.

You have the foundation here. Once you replace the general statements with a personal, specific example, the opening will be more memorable and will give admissions tutors a reason to read on. Send me your next draft and I will be glad to look again.
