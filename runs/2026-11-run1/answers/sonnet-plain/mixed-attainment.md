# One Lesson on Curley's Wife for a Very Wide-Ranging Year 9 Class

*Assumptions: a 50-60 minute lesson, mid-way through the novel (after chapter 5 has been read, so her death is known; adjust if not). You have a visualiser or board and can print one shared booklet.*

## The idea: one task, many routes in

Don't write three lessons. Write **one lesson with a shared core question and a shared text** and build flexibility into three things: how students access the text, how far they push the thinking, and how they show it. Everyone studies the same extracts and answers the same big question, so the whole class can discuss together.

**Core question for everyone:** *Is Curley's wife a villain, a victim, or both?*

This question works at Year 5 level ("Is she mean or is she lonely?") and at top-grade level (a nuanced argument about Steinbeck's methods and the social context). The question stays the same; the depth changes.

## Lesson outline

### 1. Hook (5 minutes), everyone
Display the line: *"I get awful lonely."* (Chapter 5). Ask: who might say this, and why would they say it to someone like Lennie? Students jot a one-word feeling on a mini-whiteboard. Share a few. This is accessible to every student and sets the emotional centre of the lesson.

### 2. Shared reading (10 minutes), everyone
Read two or three short extracts aloud together (for example: her first appearance in chapter 2, when Candy and the men describe her, and her speech to the men in chapter 5). You read; students follow with the printed text.

Support for the two students working at about Year 5 level, built into the shared sheet and not given only to them:

- Tricky words glossed in the margin (for example "tart", "jail bait", "ranch hands")
- A one-sentence summary above each extract in plain language
- Short chunks with a gap to write between each

### 3. Main task (25 minutes), one sheet, choose a "gear"

Give everyone the same sheet with the same three-column grid:

| What she says or does | What it makes us think about her | Why Steinbeck might have done this |
|---|---|---|

Students work through the extracts at different depths using a visible menu of "gears". They choose, with you nudging, and they can change gears mid-lesson.

**Gear 1: Spot and explain**
- Sentence starters supplied: *"She is shown as ___ when she says ___. This makes me feel ___ because ___."*
- Word bank of character words: lonely, flirty, bored, trapped, powerless, desperate, dismissed
- Pick one quotation from a set of three and explain it.

**Gear 2: Develop**
- Find your own quotation for each column.
- Write a PEE or PEEL paragraph answering the core question.
- Include one comment on a word choice (for example what the repeated "Curley's wife" label suggests).

**Gear 3: Challenge**
- Argue that she is *both* villain and victim, using at least two perspectives. Consider how much sympathy Steinbeck wants the reader to have.
- Link to context: 1930s attitudes to women, the American Dream, the lack of power and names for women on the ranch.
- Consider structure: when in the novel do we get sympathy for her, and why delay it until after her death?
- Optional stretch: "Some critics argue Steinbeck makes her a scapegoat. How far do you agree?"

No student is stuck below or capped above: the Year 5 students can complete Gear 1 and join the whiteboard discussion on equal footing; the high attainers go to Gear 3 and are asked to evaluate, not merely add more quotations.

### 4. Pair talk and mix (10 minutes)
Pair students across ability for a structured exchange. Each partner has a role:

- **Explainer:** says their best idea in one sentence.
- **Questioner:** asks "What makes you say that?" or "Is there another way to see it?"

This lets the stronger readers practise articulating ideas, and gives the Year 5 level students access to richer language without it being a test. Seat the two Year 5 level students with kind, clear explainers.

### 5. Whole-class plenary (5-10 minutes)
Vote with feet or hands: villain, victim, both. Ask a few students to defend their position in one sentence. Then give one **exit ticket** that everyone completes:

*"Curley's wife is ___ because ___."* (Gear 1 gets sentence starters; Gear 3 is asked for a "however" clause.)

## Practical tips for making this manageable

- **One resource, layered.** Print the extracts and grid on a single double-sided sheet, with supports in the margin and challenge prompts in a box at the bottom. Nobody feels singled out.
- **Choice of response format.** Allow annotated quotations, a short written paragraph, or a "voice note" style spoken answer recorded by a TA or on a tablet, so the weakest writers can still show their thinking.
- **Pre-teach if you can.** A 5-minute vocabulary preview with the two students before the lesson (for example "lonely", "powerless", "dismissed") helps them contribute confidently.
- **Check understanding with hinge questions.** Before the main task, ask a quick multiple-choice question about how she is treated by the men; this tells you who needs a prompt.
- **Ask different questions, not different tasks.** In whole-class discussion, ask Year 5-level students "What does she want?" and the highest attainers "How does Steinbeck use her to criticise society?"
- **Mark the thinking, not just the writing.** Give feedback on ideas so that weaker spelling and sentence structure do not mask understanding.

## What success looks like

- Everyone can state a view on the core question and support it with at least one piece of evidence.
- Most students explain what a detail suggests about her.
- The top students evaluate Steinbeck's purpose and link it to context and structure.
- The class has still shared a single conversation about the same character.
