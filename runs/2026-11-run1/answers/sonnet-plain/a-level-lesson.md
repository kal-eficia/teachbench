# A level Psychology (AQA), Year 12: Milgram's obedience study (70 minutes)

**Group:** 14 students | **Prior learning:** Asch (conformity), taught last week | **Key difficulty:** evaluation (AO3)

**Assumptions:** this is the first of the obedience lessons (Milgram's baseline study, findings and evaluation). Situational variables and explanations of obedience come in later lessons. You have mini whiteboards, a short handout of the procedure, and printed evaluation cards.

## Learning objectives
- **Describe (AO1):** the procedure, findings and conclusions of Milgram's (1963) study of obedience.
- **Evaluate (AO3):** the study using developed points (point, evidence, explain, link).
- **Apply (AO2/AO3):** link strengths and weaknesses to the question "How far can we trust the findings?"

## Timings at a glance
| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-10 | Retrieval starter: Asch and the "what makes people comply?" hook |
| 10-25 | Milgram: procedure and findings (knowledge) |

## 1. Retrieval starter (10 min)
- On whiteboards, quick retrieval from last week: "Name the three types of conformity", "What was the percentage of conformity on critical trials in Asch's study?" (about 32%), "Give one variable that affected conformity."
- Hook question for discussion in pairs (2 min): "If someone in authority told you to do something you thought was wrong, would you do it? What would make you more likely to?" Take two or three answers. Introduce the word **obedience** and ask how it differs from conformity (obedience involves a direct order from someone with authority; conformity is influenced by peers and may be unspoken).

## 2. Milgram's baseline study: input (15 min)
Show the study in sections. Students take notes on a structured handout (headings: Aim, Procedure, Findings, Conclusion). Pause often for recall questions.

**Aim:** to investigate whether ordinary people would obey an authority figure's orders to harm another person.

**Procedure (1963, Yale University):**
- 40 male participants, recruited through newspaper ads, aged 20-50, various jobs. Told it was a study of memory and learning.
- Participant was always the "teacher" (rigged draw). A confederate ("Mr Wallace") was the "learner" in another room, strapped to a chair with electrodes.
- Teacher gave a shock each time the learner got a word-pair wrong, increasing by 15 volts from 15V to 450V. Shock generator labelled from "Slight Shock" to "Danger: Severe Shock" and "XXX".
- Learner gave pre-recorded responses (protests, then screaming, then silence at higher voltages).
- If the teacher hesitated, the experimenter (grey lab coat) gave standard verbal prods: "Please continue", "The experiment requires that you continue", "It is absolutely essential that you continue", "You have no other choice, you must go on."
- Participants were debriefed afterwards and reunited with the learner.

**Findings:**
- **100%** went to at least 300V; **65%** went all the way to 450V.
- Many showed signs of extreme stress: sweating, trembling, nervous laughter, and some had seizures.
- Qualitative data from observation and later interviews.

**Conclusion:** ordinary people are willing to obey an authority figure even when this means harming an innocent person. Obedience seems to depend more on the situation than on the person's character.

Quick check (cold-call, whiteboards): "What percentage went to 450V?" "What was the role of the confederate?" "Name one prod."

## 3. Predict and compare (5 min)
Tell students that before the study Milgram asked psychiatrists to predict the results. They thought only about 1 in 1000 (the "most sadistic" fringe) would go to 450V. Students discuss in pairs: "Why might people have been so wrong?" This leads into the key idea that situation matters, and that findings were surprising.

## 4. Evaluation workshop (25 min)
Because evaluation is the weak spot, do this in three structured stages instead of simply asking students to evaluate.

### Stage A: Model the structure (7 min)
Teach a simple structure for each point. Use **"Point, Evidence, Explain, Link"** (or "PEEL"). Add a final "so what?" step: link back to the question (e.g. how far the findings can be trusted, generalised or justified).

**Sentence stems on the board:**
- "One strength/limitation of Milgram's study is ______."
- "For example, ______ (evidence)."
- "This means that ______ (explain the effect on the findings)."
- "Therefore, ______ (link to whether we can trust or generalise the findings)."

**Worked model (teacher writes live):**
> One limitation of Milgram's study is that it lacked population validity because all 40 participants were male and volunteers from one area. This means the findings may not apply to women or other cultures. Therefore, we cannot assume that 65% obedience would be found in other groups.

Ask students to label P, E, E, L in the model with colours.

### Stage B: Matching and sorting (8 min)
Give pairs a set of cards: points (strength or limitation), evidence, and explanation. Students match them into complete evaluation points and sort into strength/limitation. Include items such as:
- **Lab study, high control, standardised procedure** (strength: replicable, so reliability can be checked; Milgram's procedure had standardised prods and a fixed script).
- **Lack of population validity** (all male, from one area, volunteers).
- **Low ecological validity / artificial task** (shocks and memory test not like real life). But counter: participants were genuinely distressed, suggesting they believed it, and this supports experimental realism.
- **Demand characteristics (Orne and Holland, 1968):** participants may have guessed shocks were fake. Counter: Milgram reported that most (about 70% in his follow-up questionnaire) believed they were real, but Perry (2012), who looked at archive recordings, argued many participants doubted the setup.
- **Supporting evidence for validity:** Hofling et al. (1966), real nurses obeyed unauthorised drug orders (21 out of 22), and Burger (2009) found a similar rate of continuing past 150V in a partial replication.
- **Ethical issues:** deception (told it was about memory), lack of full informed consent, psychological harm (stress, seizures), right to withdraw undermined by the prods. Counter: debrief given, and in a follow-up most participants (84%) said they were glad to have taken part.

Include some cards as "not a developed point" so students can spot weak ones (for example "It was unethical" with no explanation).

### Stage C: Build your own point (10 min)
Each student (individually, then swap with a partner) writes **one** fully developed evaluation point using the stems on a mini whiteboard or a piece of paper. Circulate and give feedback. Select two examples to show on the board and have the class improve them together ("What's missing? What does it mean for the findings?").

Strengthen weak answers with two prompt questions: **"So what does that mean for the findings?"** and **"Why does that matter?"**

## 5. Written task and peer review (12 min)
Set an exam-style question (AQA style):
> **Evaluate Milgram's study of obedience (6 marks).**
(You can also set an "Outline and evaluate" 8 or 12 mark question for stronger groups later.)

- Students write **two** developed evaluation points (about 8 minutes) using PEEL. Allow them to use their notes and stems for this first attempt.
- Peer review (4 minutes): swap and underline P, E, E, L in different colours. If one is missing, the writer adds it. Use a short checklist: Did they name the point? Did they give evidence? Did they explain the effect on the findings? Did they link back?

## 6. Exit ticket (3 min)
On a slip of paper:
1. One thing Milgram found (with the percentage).
2. One evaluation point (one sentence with a "so what").
3. One thing you are still unsure about.

Use the slips to plan the retrieval starter and any reteaching next lesson.

## Differentiation
- **Support:** provide partially completed PEEL paragraphs with a word bank; allow pair writing for the first point.
- **Challenge:** ask for a counterpoint ("However, ...") and a judgement ("Overall, the study is ... because ...") to move towards top-band evaluation. Ask them to consider how Asch and Milgram can be compared (both lab studies, both lacked population validity, but Milgram had more ethical issues).

## Homework
- Write the full 6-mark answer (or a 12-mark answer for stronger students) on Milgram using PEEL, to be marked with a simple 3-column grid (point, evidence, explain/link).
- Retrieval: make 10 flashcards on Milgram's procedure and findings, and 5 on evaluation points (with a counterpoint for each).

## Looking ahead
