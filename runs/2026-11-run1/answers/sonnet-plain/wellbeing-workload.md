# Cutting marking time this term without hurting learning

**Assumptions:** secondary school, a typical written marking policy (comments, next steps, regular book checks), and you can change policy mid-term but cannot add staff or time. I have kept the suggestions to things that are realistic to start within two to three weeks.

## The principle

Marking is worth the time only if it changes what a student does next. Much of the marking that takes the longest (detailed written comments on every piece, evidencing every act of feedback, marking work that is already low-stakes practice) is the least likely to change what students do. The aim is to keep the feedback and remove the paperwork around it.

## What to do

### 1. Change what must be marked, and how often

- **Stop requiring a written comment on every piece.** Agree that a deep, written mark happens at set points (for example one major piece per half-term per class), and the rest is checked in other ways.
- **Distinguish three kinds of work in the policy:**
  - *Practice and retrieval work*: not marked by the teacher. Checked in class (self-marked against answers, or whole-class feedback).
  - *Regular classwork or homework*: skimmed for patterns, then whole-class feedback.
  - *Key assessed pieces*: marked in detail, with a specific next step.
- **Remove any rule about colour of pen, number of comments, or stamps.** These add time and show nothing about learning.

### 2. Replace individual comments with whole-class feedback

- The teacher reads a set of books or papers, not marking each one, and fills in a one-page sheet: what most students did well, common mistakes, a few examples to show (anonymised), misconceptions to reteach, and a few students to see individually.
- The next lesson starts with this feedback, then students improve their work. This usually takes 10 to 15 minutes per class set, against 1 to 2 hours of written marking.
- Provide a simple template so nobody has to design it.

### 3. Move feedback into the lesson

- **Live marking and circulating** while students work, with a quick verbal correction. Students act on it straight away, which is more effective than a comment they read later (or do not).
- **Mini whiteboards and hinge questions** so you see understanding in real time, with no marking needed.
- **Self- and peer-marking** against a mark scheme or model answer for quizzes and practice questions, with the teacher checking a sample. Students learn from seeing the answer, and it saves teacher time.

### 4. Use low-stakes quizzes for retrieval

- Short weekly quizzes (5 to 10 questions) that students mark themselves in a different colour, with the teacher collecting scores in a few minutes. Quizzes support learning and need almost no marking time.

### 5. Standardise the tools, not the effort

- **Shared comment banks or codes** for common errors, so a teacher writes "SP2" or ticks a box instead of rewriting the same comment.
- **Shared mark schemes and model answers** per department, so each teacher is not creating their own.
- **Common assessments, marked together**: departments agree a few key assessments per year and moderate in a meeting rather than each teacher marking alone and then checking later.

### 6. Stop the checking-up

- **Replace book scrutiny that looks for evidence of marking** with scrutiny that looks at student progress and responses to feedback. If leaders are checking for ticks and dated comments, teachers will keep doing them.
- **Agree what leaders will and will not ask for**, and put it in writing. This is often the biggest morale win, because it removes fear as well as workload.

## A plan for this term

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Ask staff for the two or three marking tasks that take the most time and seem to help students least (short anonymous form or a quick department discussion). Use the answers to choose which changes to make first. |
| Week 2 | Agree a short, revised marking statement (one page), with the three kinds of work above. Share it with a clear message: "this replaces the old policy; nothing else is expected." |
| Weeks 3 to 4 | Departments choose their approach (for example whole-class feedback in English and humanities, self-marked quizzes in maths and science), with templates provided. Pilot with a few volunteers first. |
| Half-term | Short check with the pilot staff and a sample of students. What saved time? Did students still improve? |
| Second half-term | Roll out to all, adjust, and tell staff what has changed as a result of what they said. |

## How to check you have not hurt learning

- Compare assessment results on a topic before and after for pilot classes, looking at patterns rather than judging individual teachers.
- Ask students whether they know what to do to improve (a quick survey or a few student conversations).
- Look at whether students are acting on feedback (improved work after a feedback lesson) rather than whether marking is visible in books.

## How to check you have saved time

- Ask pilot teachers to estimate marking hours per week before and after (a rough figure is fine, and do not turn this into a monitoring exercise).
- As a rough illustration, if a teacher with six classes replaces two hours of written marking per class set with fifteen minutes of whole-class feedback, that is several hours per week saved. The real figure will depend on your policy and subjects.

## Things to avoid

- **Adding new requirements on top of the old policy** (for example whole-class feedback sheets as well as written comments). If anything is added, something must be removed.
- **Evidence-of-feedback requirements.** If staff must prove they gave feedback, the time saved is lost.
- **Imposing one approach on every subject.** A maths teacher and an art teacher need different things.
- **Announcing it as a "new initiative".** Frame it as "we are removing things", which fits better with low morale.

## A short message you could send to staff

> "From next week, we are changing what we ask of you on marking. You do not need to write a comment on every piece of work, or evidence every piece of feedback. Detailed marking is for key assessed pieces. For everything else, whole-class feedback, live feedback and quizzes are fine. Nothing new is being added. Leaders will no longer check books for marking. We will check back in at half-term to see what is working."

This gives staff something clear and specific, and shows leaders are taking things away, not adding to the load.
