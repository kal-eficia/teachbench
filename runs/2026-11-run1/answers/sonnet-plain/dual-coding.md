# How a coastal spit forms: board diagram and teaching script (Year 8)

**Assumptions:** one 10 to 15 minute explanation, whiteboard with at least two colours of pen, class already knows what erosion, transportation and deposition mean. Your example is Spurn Head (Holderness coast, Yorkshire) or Hurst Castle Spit; swap in whichever you prefer.

## The big picture to draw first

A spit is a long, narrow finger of sand or shingle that is joined to the land at one end and sticks out into the sea (or across a river mouth) at the other. It forms where the coastline suddenly changes direction.

## Board layout

Draw it in **four stages, left to right or top to bottom**, so students can see the spit grow. Use one colour for land (green), one for sea (blue), one for sand (yellow or orange), and one for arrows (red for wind, blue for waves and drift).

A simple plan view (as if looking down from above):

```
STAGE 1: Straight coast, then a bend        STAGE 2: Sediment moves along the coast

 LAND ____________________                   LAND ____________________
      \                  \_______                \                   \_______
       \  river / bay            ~~~              \  river / bay   ====>  ~~~
        \________              SEA                 \________   sand      SEA
                                                   (red arrow: wind -> )
 (draw a red arrow for prevailing wind;      (blue arrow: longshore drift along the shore)
  label "prevailing wind")

STAGE 3: Deposition past the bend            STAGE 4: Spit grows, hook forms, marsh behind

 LAND ____________________                   LAND ____________________
      \                  \_______                \                   \_______
       \   bay      ======>>>>>                   \   SALT MARSH  _.-"""-.   SEA
        \________   (sand builds out)              \________   .-'  hook  )
                                                              (spit curves at the end)
```

Label these on the final diagram: **prevailing wind, longshore drift, deposition, spit, recurved end (hook), salt marsh, river mouth or bay, change in direction of coastline, secondary wind or waves.**

## Stage by stage: what to draw and what to say

### Stage 1: The coastline and the bend (1 to 2 minutes)

**Draw:** a straight coastline running across the board, then a sharp bend where the land turns inland (a river mouth or bay). Add a red arrow for the prevailing wind blowing at an angle to the shore.

**Say:** "Here is a stretch of coast. It runs straight for a while, then the land suddenly turns away, maybe because there's a river mouth or a bay. The wind here usually blows from the same direction. We call that the prevailing wind. Remember this bend, because the whole story depends on it."

**Check:** "What do we call a wind that usually blows from the same direction?" (prevailing)

### Stage 2: Longshore drift (3 minutes)

**Draw:** a zigzag blue arrow along the shoreline. Waves arrive at an angle (swash), then drain straight back down the slope (backwash). Mark swash with an arrow going up the beach at an angle and backwash with an arrow going straight back down.

**Say:** "The waves come in at an angle because of the wind. The swash carries sand and pebbles up the beach at that angle. Then gravity pulls the water straight back down the slope. That's the backwash. Each wave moves the sediment a little way along the beach. Wave after wave, the material zigzags along the coast. That movement is called longshore drift."

**Check:** Ask students to trace the zigzag with a finger in the air. "Which direction is the sand travelling overall?"

### Stage 3: Deposition at the bend (3 minutes)

**Draw:** the zigzag arrow reaches the bend. Then show the arrow continuing straight out into the sea beyond the bend. Add yellow sand building up where the arrow loses energy.

**Say:** "Longshore drift carries the sand along the coast. But when it reaches the bend, the coastline turns away and the sediment keeps going in a straight line out into open water. There, the water is deeper and the waves lose energy, and where a river mouth meets the sea the flow slows down too. With less energy the waves can't carry the sand any more, so they drop it. This is deposition. Over weeks and years the sand builds up into a ridge sticking out from the land."

**Link to prior learning:** "Remember, waves drop heavier material first when they lose energy."

### Stage 4: Growth, the hook and the salt marsh (3 to 4 minutes)

**Draw:** extend the yellow ridge as a long finger. At the tip, draw a hooked curve back towards the land. Draw a second, smaller arrow for occasional winds from a different direction. Shade the sheltered water behind the spit and label it "salt marsh".

**Say:** "The spit keeps growing as long as longshore drift keeps delivering sediment. The end of the spit often curves round into a hook, called a recurved end. This happens when the wind or waves occasionally come from a different direction, or when waves bend (refract) round the end of the spit. Behind the spit the water is calm and sheltered. Mud and silt settle there, and plants take root. That's a salt marsh, an important habitat for birds."

**If the river keeps flowing:** "A river flowing into the sea stops the spit joining across to the other side, because the current keeps the gap open."

## Quick summary to say at the end (1 minute)

"So: a spit needs a change in the coastline's direction, longshore drift to deliver sediment, and deposition where the energy drops. A hook forms when the wind changes direction. A salt marsh forms in the sheltered water behind."

## Plenary questions (2 to 3 minutes)

1. Why does the sand stop moving at the bend? (The coast changes direction and the water is deeper, so energy is lost and deposition happens.)
2. What is the difference between swash and backwash?
3. Why does a spit often have a curved end?
4. Why can a spit never grow all the way across a river mouth?
5. Name a spit in the UK. (Spurn Head, Hurst Castle Spit, Orford Ness, Blakeney Point.)

## Common misconceptions to watch for

- "The waves push sand straight up the beach." Remind them swash arrives at an angle but backwash returns straight down.
- "The sea builds spits by erosion." The spit is made by **deposition**; the sand was **eroded** and **transported** elsewhere first.
- "The salt marsh forms in front of the spit." It forms in the calm, sheltered area **behind** it.

## Optional extension

Ask students to predict what happens if a storm breaches the spit, or if people build groynes on the beach upstream (the sediment supply is cut off, so the spit is starved and shrinks).

## Tip for the board

Keep Stage 1 on the board and add each later stage in a different colour on the same diagram, so students see the spit grow rather than four separate pictures. Photograph the final board for the class to copy or label in their books.
