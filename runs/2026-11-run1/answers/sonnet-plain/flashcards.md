Q: What is the rate of a chemical reaction? | A: How quickly reactants are used up or products are formed, i.e. the change in amount of a substance per unit time.
Q: What is collision theory? | A: Reactions happen only when particles collide with enough energy and in the right orientation.
Q: What is activation energy? | A: The minimum energy particles need when they collide for a reaction to happen.
Q: What is a successful collision (effective collision)? | A: A collision where particles have at least the activation energy, so they react.
Q: Do all collisions between reactant particles cause a reaction? | A: No. Only collisions with enough energy (at least the activation energy) cause a reaction.
Q: What two things decide the rate of a reaction, according to collision theory? | A: The frequency of collisions between particles and the energy of those collisions.
Q: Name the five main factors that affect rate of reaction. | A: Temperature, concentration (of solutions), pressure (of gases), surface area (of solids) and catalysts.
Q: What happens to the rate when temperature increases? | A: The rate increases.
Q: Why does increasing temperature increase rate? (2 reasons) | A: Particles move faster so they collide more often, and more of the collisions have energy of at least the activation energy.
Q: Which reason is more important for a rise in rate with temperature: more collisions or more energetic collisions? | A: More energetic collisions. A larger proportion of collisions are successful.
Q: What happens to the rate when concentration of a solution increases? | A: The rate increases.
Q: Why does increasing concentration increase rate? | A: There are more particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent.
Q: What happens to the rate when the pressure of a gas increases? | A: The rate increases.
Q: Why does increasing pressure increase the rate of a gas reaction? | A: The gas particles are squashed closer together, so collisions are more frequent.
Q: Does increasing pressure affect the rate of a reaction between solids or liquids? | A: Not noticeably. Pressure only matters for reactions involving gases.
Q: What happens to the rate when the surface area of a solid increases? | A: The rate increases.
Q: Why does increasing surface area increase rate? | A: More particles of the solid are exposed, so collisions with the other reactant are more frequent.
Q: How can you increase the surface area of a solid? | A: Break it into smaller pieces or grind it into a powder.
Q: Which has the greater surface area: 1 g of marble chips or 1 g of powdered marble? | A: Powdered marble (smaller pieces mean more surface area for the same mass).
Q: What is a catalyst? | A: A substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up in the reaction.
Q: Is a catalyst used up in a reaction? | A: No. It is unchanged chemically at the end and can be reused.
Q: How does a catalyst work? | A: It provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
Q: Why does lowering the activation energy increase the rate? | A: More collisions have enough energy to react, so more collisions are successful.
Q: Does a catalyst change the amount of product made? | A: No. It only changes how fast the product is made.
Q: Give the catalyst used to speed up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. | A: Manganese(IV) oxide (manganese dioxide), MnO2.
Q: Name an industrial process that uses a catalyst and give the catalyst. | A: Haber process (making ammonia) uses iron. (Contact process uses vanadium(V) oxide.)
Q: Why are catalysts useful in industry? | A: They speed up reactions and allow lower temperatures, which saves energy and money, and they can be reused.
Q: What are enzymes? | A: Biological catalysts.
Q: What does a steeper line on a rate graph (volume or mass against time) show? | A: A faster rate of reaction.
Q: What does a flat (horizontal) line on a rate graph show? | A: The reaction has stopped because at least one reactant has been used up.
Q: Two curves on the same axes end at the same final volume of gas. What does that tell you? | A: The same amount of product was made, so the amount of limiting reactant was the same. Only the rate differed.
Q: On a graph, how does increasing a factor like temperature change the curve? | A: The curve starts steeper and levels off sooner, but ends at the same height if the amount of reactant is unchanged.
Q: Why does the rate of a reaction decrease over time? | A: Reactants are used up, so their concentration falls and collisions become less frequent.
Q: When is the rate of reaction highest? | A: At the start, when reactant concentrations are highest.
Q: Name three ways to measure the rate of a reaction. | A: Measure the volume of gas produced, measure the loss in mass, or time how long a precipitate takes to hide a mark (disappearing cross).
Q: What apparatus can you use to measure the volume of gas produced? | A: A gas syringe, or an upturned measuring cylinder or burette in water.
Q: When is measuring mass loss a suitable method? | A: When a gas is given off that escapes into the air, e.g. carbon dioxide from marble chips and acid, using a balance.
Q: What can you do to stop the loss of mass method losing liquid spray? | A: Put a loose plug of cotton wool in the neck of the flask.
Q: Describe the disappearing cross experiment. | A: Sodium thiosulfate reacts with hydrochloric acid to form a cloudy sulfur precipitate. Time how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear.
Q: In the disappearing cross experiment, what does a shorter time mean? | A: A faster rate of reaction.
Q: How do you calculate rate from a time taken (disappearing cross)? | A: Rate is proportional to 1 divided by time (1/t).
Q: What is the formula for mean rate of reaction? | A: Mean rate = quantity of reactant used or product formed / time taken.
Q: What are the units of rate if you measure volume of gas in cm3 and time in seconds? | A: cm3/s.
Q: What are the units of rate if you measure mass in grams and time in seconds? | A: g/s.
Q: Calculate the mean rate: 48 cm3 of gas made in 40 s. | A: 48 / 40 = 1.2 cm3/s.
Q: How do you find the rate at a particular time from a graph? | A: Draw a tangent to the curve at that time and calculate its gradient (change in y / change in x).
Q: Why is a tangent used to find rate at a point on a curve? | A: The gradient of the curve changes, so the tangent gives the gradient (rate) at that exact point.
Q: How do you find the mean rate over a time period from a graph? | A: Read the change in quantity between the two times and divide by the time difference.
Q: In a rate investigation, what is the independent variable if you change the temperature? | A: Temperature.
Q: What are control variables, and give two for a magnesium and acid rate experiment. | A: Variables kept the same to make it a fair test, e.g. volume and concentration of acid, mass of magnesium, same size of magnesium ribbon.
Q: Magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid. What are the products? | A: Magnesium chloride and hydrogen gas (Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2).
Q: Marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with hydrochloric acid. What are the products? | A: Calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide.
Q: What is the test for hydrogen gas? | A: A lit splint gives a squeaky pop.
Q: Why is a higher temperature a safety concern in rate experiments? | A: Hot liquids can scald and glassware can crack, so use a water bath, wear eye protection and take care.
Q: Why do dust explosions (e.g. flour or coal dust) happen so fast? | A: The very large surface area of the fine powder means extremely fast reaction with oxygen, releasing a lot of energy at once.
Q: Why does food keep longer in a fridge? | A: The lower temperature slows the reactions that make food go off.
Q: Exam tip: how should you explain an effect on rate in 'explain' questions? | A: Link the factor to collision frequency or energy of collisions, e.g. 'more particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions, so a faster rate'.
Q: Exam tip: what is the common mistake to avoid about collisions? | A: Do not say 'more collisions' alone. Say 'more frequent collisions' or 'more successful collisions' as appropriate.
Q: Exam tip: common mistake about temperature and particles? | A: Do not say particles 'get bigger' or 'the activation energy goes down'. They move faster and have more energy.
Q: Does a higher concentration mean more energetic particles? | A: No. Concentration changes how often particles collide, not their energy.
