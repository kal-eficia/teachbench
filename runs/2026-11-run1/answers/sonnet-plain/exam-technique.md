# Year 11 lesson: scoring on AQA GCSE Geography 9-mark "assess" questions

**Assumptions:** A 60 minute lesson, Year 11, students already know the content. Use a case study or topic they know well, so that the technique is the focus, not the content. Example used here: "Assess the effectiveness of the management of a flood risk in the UK" or a similar "assess" question from Paper 1 or 2. Swap in whichever topic you have just taught. Mark scheme level descriptors are used as the AQA 9-mark scheme (Level 3: 7 to 9, Level 2: 4 to 6, Level 1: 1 to 3), with an additional up to 3 SPaG marks on some questions (check which papers in your specification).

## Why marks are lost on "assess"

Typical problems:
- **Describing instead of weighing up.** They list facts about the case study but never judge them.
- **One-sided answers.** Only positives or only negatives.
- **No judgement or conclusion.** Nothing says "overall, the most significant factor is...".
- **Unlinked facts.** Case study detail isn't tied back to the question.
- **Poor time use.** Too long on one point, too little time at the end.

## Learning objectives

By the end, students can:
1. Explain what "assess" requires (weigh up, consider different factors, reach a judgement).
2. Plan a 9-mark answer in 2 minutes.
3. Write a balanced, evidence-based answer with a justified conclusion.
4. Use level descriptors to judge and improve an answer.

## Lesson plan

### 1. Starter: what does "assess" mean? (8 min)
- Show three command words on the board: *describe*, *explain*, *assess*. Ask students to define each in their own words and give a one-line example of what each answer starts like.
- Establish: **assess = weigh up the importance, success or impact, using evidence, and reach a judgement.**
- Quick activity: "How good a pizza is X?" Students "assess" a simple, non-geographical thing in 60 seconds (pros, cons, overall verdict) to show the structure.

### 2. Teach the structure: PEEL + judgement (10 min)
Model a simple scaffold on the board:

- **Intro (1 sentence):** State your overall view or what you are going to weigh up.
- **Point 1 (strength / one factor):** Point, Evidence (case study: names, figures, places), Explain, Link back to the question.
- **Point 2 (a contrasting factor or limitation):** same structure, ideally starting with a connective like "However", "On the other hand".
- **Point 3 (optional, a third factor):** If time allows.
- **Conclusion (2 sentences):** "Overall, ... was most/least effective because... but this depends on...". Make a clear judgement, with a reason.

Tell students: for 9 marks, aim for **two or three developed points plus a conclusion**, about 15 minutes of writing at most. Time guide: roughly one minute per mark (9 minutes), plus planning and checking.

### 3. Model a strong answer, think aloud (8 min)
- Show a **planning grid** (Factor | Evidence | Strength/limitation | Judgement) and fill in the first row with the class.
- Then write the first paragraph live on the board while you narrate the thinking: "Here I'm adding my evidence... here I'm judging how effective it is... here I'm linking back to the question".
- Highlight connectives and judgement phrases: *this was effective because... however its limitation is... the most significant factor was... overall... to a large extent... to a limited extent.*

### 4. Mark an anonymous answer (10 min)
- Give pairs two anonymised student answers to the same question: one Level 1/2 (descriptive, one-sided), one Level 3. (Write these yourself, or use two answers from previous years with names removed.)
- Pairs use the level descriptors (simplified on a card) and highlight: green = evidence, yellow = explanation, pink = judgement/conclusion.
- Discuss: what's missing in the weaker one? What did the stronger one do that earned the marks? Give each pair a mark and a one-sentence reason, then reveal the "real" marks.

### 5. Plan and write (15 min)
- Give a new "assess" question on a different case study or a different aspect of the same topic.
- **Plan (3 min):** students use the planning grid with 2 to 3 factors, evidence for each, a judgement, and their conclusion line.
- **Write (12 min):** timed, silent. Offer sentence starters for those who need them (on a card). Challenge: more able students must include a counter-argument and say what the judgement depends on.

### 6. Peer feedback and improve (7 min)
- Swap with a partner. Using the checklist below, tick and add one "even better if..." comment.
- Students redraft one paragraph, or add or strengthen their conclusion, in a different colour.

### 7. Plenary (2 min)
- Cold-call two or three students to share their conclusion sentence.
- Exit ticket: "Write one thing you will do differently next time you see the word 'assess'."

## Checklist (give each student a card)

- [ ] Did I make a judgement (not just describe)?
- [ ] Do I have at least two developed points?
- [ ] Have I included place-specific evidence (names, figures, dates)?
- [ ] Have I shown both sides or limitations?
- [ ] Is my conclusion clear and justified?
- [ ] Did I link back to the question?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a partly completed planning grid and sentence starters. Pre-select the evidence. Offer a "what's the best / least effective" prompt for the conclusion.
- **Stretch:** Ask them to compare short-term and long-term effects, or different stakeholders, and to evaluate which factor matters most, and why.

## Homework / follow-up

- Set one more 9-mark "assess" question under timed conditions, which you mark with the level descriptors and use a "what went well / even better if / next time" comment.
- Repeat the technique in the next two or three lessons with a 5-minute "mini assess" planning starter to build fluency.
- Keep a class bank of strong judgement phrases and connectives on the wall.

## Teacher notes

- Check the question wording in the spec exemplars and past papers, and match the lesson to the command words that appear (assess, evaluate, to what extent).
- Mark a few answers from the last mock first and use the real weaknesses to shape the examples. If many students describe without judging, spend longer on step 4.
- Reinforce that examiners reward developed, evidence-based points with a clear final judgement more than lots of short facts.
