# Year 13 Economics: 15-minute retrieval task (macroeconomics, with micro interleaved)

**Assumptions:** closed book, pen and paper, no devices. Students are in the macro part of the course (AD/AS, inflation, unemployment, fiscal and monetary policy) with the exam about five months away. The task mixes recent topics with older micro content (spaced and interleaved retrieval), then a short exam-style application. If your board is AQA, Edexcel or OCR, the content fits all three.

## Timings

| Time | Part |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Section A: quick recall (10 questions, 30 seconds each) |
| 5 to 10 | Section B: explain and link (3 short questions) |
| 10 to 15 | Section C: exam-style paragraph, plus self-check using answers |

## Student sheet

### Section A: Quick recall (10 marks, 5 minutes)

Write short answers only.

1. Define aggregate demand and give its four components.
2. State the formula for price elasticity of demand.
3. Give two possible causes of cost-push inflation.
4. What is the difference between frictional and structural unemployment?
5. What is a negative externality? Give one example.
6. Name two tools of fiscal policy.
7. What happens to the exchange rate if interest rates rise, other things equal?
8. What is the multiplier effect?
9. Define a "maximum price" and name one possible consequence.
10. What is the difference between a budget deficit and the national debt?

### Section B: Explain and link (9 marks, 5 minutes)

11. Explain how a rise in interest rates might reduce inflation. (3 marks)
12. Explain why a sales tax on a good with inelastic demand raises more revenue than on one with elastic demand. (3 marks)
13. Explain one reason why the long-run aggregate supply curve might be vertical in the classical view. (3 marks)

### Section C: Exam-style paragraph (6 marks, 5 minutes)

14. "Cutting income tax is the best way for a government to reduce unemployment." Write one developed analytical paragraph that explains how this could work, and one short evaluative point on a limitation or alternative policy. Use a chain of reasoning (A leads to B leads to C).

Then spend the last minute checking your answers against the mark scheme your teacher reads out.

## Teacher answers and mark scheme

### Section A (1 mark each)

1. Total planned spending on an economy's goods and services at a given price level. Consumption, investment, government spending, net exports (X minus M).
2. PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price.
3. Any two: rising wages (not matched by productivity), higher raw material or oil prices, higher import prices due to a weaker currency, higher indirect taxes.
4. Frictional: temporary, between jobs. Structural: mismatch of skills or location due to long-term change in the economy, for example deindustrialisation.
5. A cost to a third party that is not reflected in the market price. Example: pollution from a factory; second-hand smoke.
6. Any two: changes in taxation, government spending, borrowing.
7. It tends to rise (higher returns attract hot money inflows, increasing demand for the currency).
8. An initial injection of spending leads to a larger final increase in national income, because spending becomes someone else's income.
9. A legal ceiling on the price, set below equilibrium. Consequence: excess demand, shortages, black markets, non-price rationing.
10. A deficit is the annual gap when government spending exceeds revenue; the national debt is the accumulated total of past borrowing.

### Section B

11. (3 marks) Higher interest rates raise the cost of borrowing and reward saving, so consumption and investment fall (1). AD shifts left (1). Less demand-pull pressure means lower inflation (1). Credit also: stronger currency lowers import prices.
12. (3 marks) With inelastic demand, quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises (1). So most of the tax is passed on to consumers and sales stay high (1). The government collects the tax on more units, so revenue is higher (1).
13. (3 marks) In the classical view, output in the long run is determined by factors of production and technology, not the price level (1). Markets clear, with flexible wages and prices (1). The economy returns to full employment, so AD changes affect only the price level (1).

### Section C (6 marks)

Level 1 (1 to 2): states that tax cuts increase demand; limited chain.
Level 2 (3 to 4): clear chain: tax cut raises disposable income, consumption and AD rise (shift right), firms produce more and need more workers, so demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment falls. Use of a diagram or multiplier adds credit.
Level 3 (5 to 6): as above, plus accurate evaluation, for example:

- Depends on the marginal propensity to consume: if households save the extra, the effect is small.
- Time lags and the size of the multiplier.
- Does not help structural unemployment, where supply-side policies (education, retraining) are better.
- Risk of a larger budget deficit and inflation if near full capacity.
- Government spending may have a bigger multiplier than tax cuts.

## Why this task works (teacher notes)

- Retrieval over recognition: students must produce answers from memory, which strengthens recall.
- Interleaving: questions 4, 5, 9, 12 revisit micro content from Year 12 alongside macro, so older material does not decay before the exam.
- Low stakes: collect nothing; students mark and note gaps. A quick tally of which questions most students missed tells you what to reteach next lesson.
- Reuse: rotate the questions every week or two, increasing the share of older content, and add a mixed paper at the end of term.
