# Preparing for tomorrow's meeting with the parent

**Assumptions:** this is a UK secondary school with a behaviour policy, you have recorded the detentions, and the meeting is formal enough that your head of year (HoY) will lead on process. If your school has a different procedure for complaints, follow that.

## First, the right frame
Mum's real message is that **her daughter is unhappy and she thinks the school is treating her unfairly.** Whether or not "picking on" is accurate, the distress is real and needs to be heard. Your aim is not to win. It is for the mum to leave feeling listened to, clear on the facts, and confident that you want her daughter to do well.

## Before the meeting (tonight)

### 1. Gather your facts
Bring a one-page factual summary:
- Date, lesson, reason and sanction for each of the three detentions.
- What the school's behaviour policy says about equipment and talking over the teacher, and that the sanctions followed it.
- How many times the same behaviours happened *without* a sanction (warnings, reminders), if you have this. This shows proportionality.
- Whether other students received the same sanction for the same behaviour.
- The pattern of her behaviour and attendance in other lessons. The two detentions from other teachers matter, but go carefully: they show a pattern, but should not be used as a weapon.
- Anything positive: a good piece of work, a moment she did well. Bring at least one.

### 2. Check your own practice honestly
Ask yourself, without defensiveness:
- Are my sanctions consistent with how I treat others in the class?
- Do I use the same tone and warnings for her as for everyone else?
- Is there any chance that equipment-related detentions penalise a pupil who has a real barrier (cost, home circumstances, SEND, organisation)?
- Have I ever corrected her in a way that was public or sharp?
- Has she been a target for repeated "small" comments I might not notice?

If you spot something you could do better, it is far stronger to say so than to be found out later.

### 3. Brief your head of year
Agree beforehand:
- Who opens and who keeps time.
- That the HoY will keep the conversation fair and not let it become a debate about whether you personally like her.
- The outcome you both want (see below).
- Whether there is any wider context you should know about: pastoral concerns, SEND, safeguarding flags, friendship problems, anything at home. Ask the HoY or SENCo before the meeting, as this may change how you approach it.

### 4. Think about what she might be experiencing
Three detentions and two more from others in a half-term is a lot for a Year 9. She may feel everyone is against her, may be struggling socially, or may be hiding something else. Crying at home suggests something is genuinely wrong, even if you are not the cause.

## What to say: an outline

### Opening (HoY chairs)
Thank mum for coming and raising this directly. Say: "I'm glad you got in touch. It's upsetting to hear [daughter] has been coming home in tears, and I want to understand what's going on."

### Listen first (5+ minutes)
- Let her say everything without interrupting or defending.
- Take notes. Repeat back: "So what I'm hearing is that she feels singled out, and she's worried it's unfair. Have I got that right?"
- Ask open questions: "What has [daughter] said about my lessons?", "Is there a particular moment that upset her?"

### Share the facts calmly
- "Let me explain what's been happening from my side."
- Walk through the three detentions: date, reason, what policy says. Say, for example: "Each time, she had been reminded first, and I gave the same sanction I'd give any student."
- Keep it neutral. Avoid "she always" or "she never". Describe behaviour, not character.

### Acknowledge what is fair
Say something real, such as:
- "I can see why three detentions in a half-term would feel like a lot, especially on top of others."
- "I don't want her to feel picked on, and if she does, I want to put that right."
- "I do like having her in my class. She's [genuine strength]."

### Offer a plan
Propose small, concrete steps:
- A fresh start: a short, private conversation between you and her, with the HoY or form tutor present if she prefers.
- Equipment: ask whether a lack of equipment is about cost or organisation. Offer a spare kit or a pencil case in the classroom so it stops being a flashpoint.
- Seating or a signal: a quiet non-verbal reminder instead of a public telling-off.
- A check-in in two weeks with mum and HoY.
- A positive-contact phone call or note home when things go well.

### Close
Summarise what has been agreed and who will do what. Confirm a follow-up date. Thank her again.

## Phrases to use and avoid

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "I can hear how worried you are." | "That's not true." |
| "Here's what happened, and here's the policy." | "She's a difficult child." |
| "I want her to do well in my lessons." | "I treat everyone the same." (it can sound dismissive) |
| "Let's find a way to start again." | "She needs to change her attitude." |
| "I'd like to understand more about what she's told you." | "She's exaggerating." |

## If it gets difficult
- **If mum raises her voice:** stay calm and slow. Let the HoY step in. Say: "I want to make sure we can talk about this properly. Can we take a minute?"
- **If she accuses you of not liking her daughter:** do not argue. Say: "I understand why it feels like that. I do want her to succeed, and I'd like to show you that."
- **If she raises something serious** (bullying by others, a safeguarding worry, a comment attributed to you that you did not make): note it, say you will look into it properly, and pass it to the HoY or DSL as policy requires. Do not respond on the spot.
- **If she asks for you to stop giving detentions:** you can agree to review how you handle equipment and talking, but do not promise that the school's behaviour policy won't be applied.

## After the meeting
- Write up a short note of what was said and agreed the same day. Share it with the HoY.
- Follow through on every action you promised.
- In the next lesson, greet her warmly, give early positive recognition, and keep the first few exchanges low-key.
- Look for a small chance to notice something she does well in the next week.

## Final thought
You are unlikely to convince mum in one meeting that you do not dislike her daughter. You can show her, by what you say and by what happens in the following weeks. Calm, fair, consistent and warm is the goal.
