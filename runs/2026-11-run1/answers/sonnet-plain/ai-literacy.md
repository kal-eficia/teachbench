# Year 8 Lesson: How AI Chatbots Work and How to Use Them Well for Learning

**Duration:** 60 minutes
**Class:** Year 8 (ages 12 to 13), mixed ability
**Subject:** Computing / PSHE / form time / cross-curricular (adaptable)

*Assumptions: students have access to a device (one per student or per pair) and your school permits a suitable, age-appropriate AI chatbot under supervision. If not, use the "no devices" alternative below, with printed prompts and sample responses. No prior knowledge of AI is assumed.*

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson students will be able to:

1. Explain in simple terms how a chatbot produces its answers (it predicts likely next words from patterns in lots of text, it does not "know" or "think").
2. Explain why chatbots can be wrong and how to check them.
3. Write a clear prompt and use a chatbot to help them learn, not to do their work for them.

## Success criteria

- I can describe how a chatbot works in two or three sentences.
- I can spot at least one thing a chatbot got wrong and say how I checked.
- I can write a prompt that helps me learn something.

## Key vocabulary

chatbot, AI, prompt, training data, prediction, hallucination, bias, verify

## Resources

- Slides or whiteboard
- Student devices (or printed copies of the prompts and sample responses)
- Prepared "chatbot gets it wrong" examples (see Activity 2)
- Mini-whiteboards (optional)
- Exit ticket slips

## Lesson outline

| Time | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Starter | Human or chatbot? |
| 5-17 | How it works | Predict the next word + explanation |
| 17-32 | Chatbots get things wrong | Fact-check task |
| 32-50 | Using chatbots well | Good vs poor prompts; learning prompts |
| 50-57 | Plenary | Rules for using AI for learning |
| 57-60 | Exit ticket | Three questions |

## 1. Starter: Human or chatbot? (0 to 5 minutes)

Show three short pieces of text on the board. Each is either written by a student or generated by a chatbot (prepare in advance: one clearly human and slightly messy, one chatbot, one chatbot edited to look human). Students vote on mini-whiteboards or by hand: human or chatbot?

Reveal answers. Ask: "How sure were you? What clues did you use?" The point is that it's often hard to tell, which is why we need to understand how these tools work.

Say: "By the end of the hour you'll know what's really happening inside a chatbot and how to use one without it making you a worse learner."

## 2. How does a chatbot work? (5 to 17 minutes)

**Activity: Be the chatbot (5 minutes).** Give a sentence beginning on the board: "The cat sat on the ..." Students write the word they think comes next on a mini-whiteboard. Compare answers: most say "mat". Try another: "I'm so tired, I'm going to ..." Then a third that is less predictable: "Yesterday the teacher turned into a ..."

Ask: "How did you decide? You used what you've read and heard before."

**Teacher explanation (5 minutes).** Use a simple diagram on the board:

1. A chatbot is trained on a huge amount of text (books, websites, conversations).
2. It learns patterns: which words and ideas tend to follow others.
3. When you type a prompt, it predicts one word at a time, choosing words that are likely to fit, until it has built an answer.
4. It does not look things up like a search engine, it does not understand like a person, and it has no feelings or experiences. It is very good at sounding right.

Key line to write up: **"A chatbot predicts what sounds right. It does not check whether it is right."**

**Check for understanding (2 minutes).** Pairs: explain to your partner in one sentence how a chatbot makes an answer. Cold call two pairs.

## 3. Chatbots get things wrong (17 to 32 minutes)

**Introduce hallucination and bias (3 minutes).** Explain: sometimes a chatbot makes up facts, names, quotes or sources that sound real (this is called a hallucination). It can also repeat unfair ideas that were in its training data (bias). It can be out of date.

**Task: Fact-check detective (12 minutes).** In pairs, students get a short chatbot-generated answer on a topic from their current learning (for example a history, science or geography topic) that you've prepared with 3 deliberate errors planted, or use a live chatbot to ask "Tell me three facts about [topic], with a source for each" and check them.

Students:
1. Highlight any claim they are not sure about.
2. Check each one against a reliable source (textbook, school-approved website, teacher).
3. Record on a sheet: Claim / Right or wrong? / How I checked.

Feedback: ask pairs to share one error they found and how they found it. Note if any "sources" were invented.

**Key line:** "Trust, but verify. A chatbot is a starting point, never the final answer."

## 4. Using chatbots well for learning (32 to 50 minutes)

**Good prompts (6 minutes).** Show two prompts:

- Poor: "Do my homework on photosynthesis."
- Better: "I'm in Year 8. Explain photosynthesis in simple words, then ask me three questions to test whether I understood."

Discuss: Which one helps you learn? Why? Point out features of a good prompt: say who you are and what you need, give the task, ask for a style or format, and ask it to check your thinking.

**Ways to use AI that help learning (3 minutes).** Display a short list:

- Ask it to quiz you.
- Ask it to explain something a different way if you're stuck.
- Ask it to give feedback on your own answer (after you've tried it).
- Ask it for a revision plan.

**Ways that don't help learning:** copying answers, getting it to write your work, skipping the thinking. If it does the thinking, you do not get better, and it's against school rules to hand in work that isn't yours.

**Task: Write and test a learning prompt (9 minutes).** Students choose a topic they are studying. They write a prompt that uses the chatbot to help them learn (not to do the work), try it, and then rate the response on a 1 to 5 scale for helpfulness and accuracy. They note one improvement to their prompt and try again.

*No devices alternative:* give out three printed prompts and responses and have students rank which prompt would help learning most and why, then rewrite the weakest prompt.

**Share (2 minutes).** Volunteers read their best prompt.

## 5. Plenary: our class rules for AI and learning (50 to 57 minutes)

In groups of four, students agree three "rules for using AI well", then share. Aim to land on points such as:

1. Try it myself first.
2. Check anything important.
3. Use it to understand, not to copy.
4. Never share personal information (name, school, address, passwords, photos) with a chatbot.
5. Say when I've used it, and follow the school and teacher's rules.

Record the final class list on the board.

## 6. Exit ticket (57 to 60 minutes)

Students answer on a slip:

1. In one sentence, how does a chatbot make its answers?
2. Name one way a chatbot can be wrong.
3. Write one prompt you could use to help you learn something this week.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** sentence starters for explaining how a chatbot works ("A chatbot is trained on... It predicts..."); a prompt-writing frame (I am... Please... Then ask me...); pre-selected errors that are easier to spot.
- **Stretch:** ask students to compare two chatbots' answers or two prompts for the same task; ask why bias might appear in training data; ask them to write a "how to check an AI answer" checklist for Year 7s.
- **SEND/EAL:** keep key vocabulary on a visible word bank with images; allow talk in pairs before writing.

## Assessment

- Mini-whiteboard responses and cold calling in the starter and explanation.
- The fact-check sheets and the written learning prompt (checks objectives 2 and 3).
- Exit ticket to check objective 1 and to plan the next lesson.

## Safeguarding and teacher notes

- Use a school-approved tool with age-appropriate settings, or demonstrate on the board only if one is not available.
- Remind students not to enter personal information.
- Preview any responses you use. Chatbots can produce unexpected content, so supervise device use closely.
- Many chatbots have age restrictions in their terms of use, so check these against your school's policy before students use them directly.

## Follow-up ideas

- Homework: ask a chatbot to quiz you on a topic and bring one question it got wrong or one correction you made.
- Next lesson: bias in AI, or using a chatbot to give feedback on a draft paragraph.
