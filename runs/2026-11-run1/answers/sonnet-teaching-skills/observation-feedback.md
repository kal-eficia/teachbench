# Feedback for the Year 9 lesson observation

**Assumptions:** You are her mentor or line manager. The observation was developmental, so no grade is given. Her name and the topic weren't given, so I've left placeholders. I've chosen one priority and parked the rest, because one practised change will do more than three at once.

---

## 1. The written feedback

**Lesson observed:** Year 9, [subject/topic], [date]

### What worked, and why it matters

**1. Relationships.** Students were relaxed, responded when you spoke, and were willing to answer your questions. That matters because students will only take a risk, such as saying a half-formed idea out loud, with a teacher they trust. You've already built the thing that makes the next step possible.

**2. A clear explanation.** The explanation of [topic] was well structured and easy to follow, and students who answered showed they had followed it. A clear explanation is the hardest part to get right, and you've got it.

### The one priority: make every student think during the explanation

For about 25 minutes, students mostly listened. During that time, questions were answered by volunteers, so you could see what some students understood but not the whole room. That showed up at the end: the final task was too hard for about a third of the class, and you only found out once they were already stuck.

These three things are the same issue seen at different moments. The explanation was good, but there was no point where every student had to show what they'd taken from it, so there was no signal that a third of the class needed more before the task.

### Your action step

**Break the explanation into chunks of about five minutes. At the end of each chunk, ask one question that every student answers at the same moment, on a mini whiteboard (or a show of fingers, A/B/C/D).**

- Write the question before the lesson, one per chunk.
- Say "everyone write your answer" and wait before you call on anyone.
- Scan the boards. If most are right, move on. If a clear group is wrong, say "let me say that another way" and reteach that chunk before moving on.
- Use what the boards show you to decide how hard to make the final task, and which students to sit near or work with first.

You don't have to change the content or the clear explanation. You're just adding a pause with a question after each piece.

### Follow-up

- I'll come back for 20 minutes within **two to three weeks**. I'll look at the middle of the lesson, not the whole thing.
- Success looks like: students answering together at least three times in the explanation, you changing something you were about to do based on what the boards showed, and fewer students stuck at the start of the final task.
- I'll bring a copy of the notes I took so we can compare.

### Parked for later

Two other things came up that we won't work on yet:
- How the final task is pitched (we can plan a "start easier, then add challenge" version once you can see where students are during the lesson)
- Who gets asked (once everyone is responding, we can look at cold-calling)

## 2. Conversation plan (20 to 25 minutes, in private)

### Opening (3 minutes)
Start with her view, not yours:
- "How do you think it went?"
- "What would you change if you taught it again tomorrow?"
- "How did you know the class had understood?"

If she says something like "I wasn't sure about the end", build on that: it makes the priority hers.

### Strengths (3 minutes)
Share the two strengths with specific evidence: a moment where a student took a risk, a part of the explanation that was particularly clear. Give a specific example, not "nice lesson".

### The priority (5 minutes)
Share what you saw, in neutral numbers: "For 25 minutes, the questions were answered by students who put their hands up. I noted [number] different students speaking. A third of the class then couldn't start the task. What do you make of that?"

Let her connect the dots. If she doesn't, say: "I think it's the same issue: we can only see understanding in the students who volunteer."

### Rehearse the action step (8 minutes)
Take a chunk from a topic she's teaching next week.
1. Ask her to pick the first five minutes of the explanation.
2. Together, write one question that only someone who understood could answer, with a plausible wrong answer in mind.
3. She teaches the chunk to you, asks the question, and says "everyone write" aloud.
4. You hold up an answer, right or wrong, and she practises what she says next: "Most of you have got this" or "Let me try that another way".

Rehearsal is the most important part of the meeting. Don't skip it.

### Agree the next step (2 minutes)
- "What will you try in your next Year 9 lesson?"
- "What might get in the way?" (typical answers: time, boards not to hand, worry about losing the flow. Answer: start with only two chunks if she wants.)
- Agree the follow-up date.
