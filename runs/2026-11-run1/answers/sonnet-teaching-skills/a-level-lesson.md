# Year 12 AQA Psychology: Milgram's obedience study (70 minutes, 14 students)

**Assumptions:** this is the first lesson on obedience, so it covers Milgram's (1963) baseline study: procedure, findings and evaluation. Variations and explanations of obedience come later. Last week's Asch lesson is the retrieval base. The AQA 16-mark "outline and evaluate" question is the end goal (about 6 marks AO1 and 10 marks AO3, so evaluation carries most of the marks). Check the split against your own mark scheme.

**The one big idea of the lesson:** evaluation is a chain, not a label. Students don't lose marks because they don't know the criticisms. They lose them because they name a criticism ("it lacks ecological validity") and stop. So the lesson teaches a four-step chain and practises it until it is a habit.

**The chain (put it on the board all lesson):**
1. **Name it:** the strength or limitation, in a technical term.
2. **Prove it:** the evidence from this study or another one.
3. **Explain it:** why that evidence makes it a strength or limitation.
4. **So what?** what this does to the study's conclusion (is the 65% still trustworthy?).

**Objectives (students can):**
- Describe Milgram's procedure and findings accurately.
- Write one complete evaluation paragraph using all four links of the chain.
- Judge which criticism matters most and say why.

## Content for the teacher (check figures against your textbook before sharing)

**Procedure (Milgram, 1963, Yale):** 40 male volunteers recruited by newspaper advert, aged roughly 20-50, told it was a study of memory and learning. Fixed rigged draw: participant always "teacher", confederate always "learner" (Mr Wallace). Experimenter in a grey lab coat. Shock generator from 15 V to 450 V in 30 steps of 15 V. Learner gave wrong answers by design and shock increased each time. Standard prods, in order: "Please continue", "The experiment requires that you continue", "It is absolutely essential that you continue", "You have no other choice, you must go on".

**Findings:** 100% went to 300 V, 65% went to 450 V. Many showed extreme tension (sweating, trembling, nervous laughter), and three had seizures. Debriefed afterwards. Later follow-up: most said they were glad to have taken part.

**Retrieval checks during AO1 (hinge questions on whiteboards):**
1. At 300 V, how many participants had stopped? (None. All reached 300 V.) Wrong answers show confusion between 300 V and the 65% at 450 V.
2. What did the experimenter say when a participant wanted to stop? (The four prods in order.) Common error: students think the experimenter threatened or forced them.
3. Why did Milgram use a confederate learner? (To control the learner's responses and keep the procedure standardised.)

## Modelling script (I do)

**Paragraph A (full chain, good):**
"One limitation of Milgram's study is low internal validity **[name it]**. Orne and Holland argued that participants may have guessed the shocks were fake, because the experimenter stayed calm and the learner's protests followed a script **[prove it]**. If participants were just going along with what they thought the researcher wanted, they were responding to demand characteristics rather than showing genuine obedience **[explain it]**. This means the 65% figure may overstate how obedient people really are, so Milgram's conclusion that ordinary people obey harmful orders is less trustworthy **[so what?]**. However, Milgram reported that most participants said afterwards that they believed the shocks were real, and their visible distress supports that."

**Paragraph B (broken, for students to repair):**
"The study was unethical. Participants were deceived and could not withdraw. This is a problem."
Ask: Which step is missing? (Prove it: which participants, what did they see? Explain: why does deception matter? So what: does it change how we judge the findings or the study?)

## Differentiation
- **Support:** sentence starters for each chain step on a half-sheet ("This is a limitation because...", "This means...", "As a result, the conclusion that ... is..."). Seat as a pair with a stronger writer for We do.
- **Stretch:** after the paragraph, add a counter-point beginning "However," and judge which side is stronger. Or evaluate the "so what" itself: is the 65% still useful if it overstates obedience?

## Mark-scheme guidance for the You do paragraph
A top-level evaluation point includes: a clear, accurate criticism or strength; specific evidence (named study, figure or feature); an explanation of why it matters; and a link back to the conclusion. A point that names the issue only ("lacks ecological validity") gets very little credit.

**Model for generalisability:** "Milgram's sample was 40 American men volunteering through a newspaper advert **[prove it]**, which limits generalisability **[name it]**. Women, other cultures and people who don't volunteer may behave differently **[explain it]**, so we cannot be sure the 65% applies to the general population, which weakens the claim that obedience is a universal human tendency **[so what?]**. Support for the finding comes from Sheridan and King's (1972) study with real shocks to a puppy, where most participants, including all of the women, gave the maximum shock."

**Model for support from another study:** "Hofling et al. (1966) found 21 of 22 real nurses were ready to give an unauthorised drug dose on a phone instruction from an unknown doctor. This is a real hospital setting, so it supports the idea that Milgram's findings were not just a lab effect, which strengthens the external validity of the conclusion."

## Common errors to listen for
- Mixing up **internal validity** (did it measure obedience?) and **external validity / ecological validity** (does it apply in real life?).
- Calling anything "unethical" without naming which guideline (deception, protection from harm, right to withdraw) and what happened in the study.
- Writing "so it is not valid" with no reason. Prompt: "Valid how? Valid for what?"
- Stopping after the "prove it" step. The exit ticket checks this.

## Homework (30 minutes)
1. 5 retrieval questions on Asch and Milgram (Milgram: the 3 numbers, the 4 prods, 2 criticisms).
2. Write one complete evaluation paragraph on a different criticism from the one chosen in class, using all four chain steps and a counter-point. Hand in next lesson.
3. Stretch: one sentence on which of the three criticisms (generalisability, internal validity, ethics) is most serious, and why.

Use the exit tickets to pick the two most common gaps (usually missing "so what?" and vague evidence) and build starter questions on them. Pair a student who wrote a full chain with one who didn't.
