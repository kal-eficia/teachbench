# Year 8 solving equations: challenge for your four fast finishers

**Assumption:** they have mastered one- and two-step equations and some brackets or unknowns on both sides. The tasks below stay on the topic (solving equations) but push thinking deeper rather than adding more of the same. I'd offer them to the whole class as a "challenge desk" rather than something just for these four, so it isn't a reward that others resent.

**How it's checked (so it doesn't turn into more mess-about time):** each student keeps a "challenge log" page. They write the answer and one line of working or reasoning. You glance at the log for 30 seconds at the end of the lesson, or the first minute of the next. Ask them to bring any answer they're stuck on to the plenary.

## Tier 1: Deeper (same topic, harder thinking)

**1. Find the flaw.** Sam solves 3(x + 4) = 2x + 17 like this:
"3x + 4 = 2x + 17, so x + 4 = 17, so x = 13."
Find Sam's mistake, correct it and check your answer by substituting.
*Strong response:* Sam didn't multiply the 4 by 3 (should be 3x + 12). Correct answer x = 5. Check: 3(9) = 27 and 2(5) + 17 = 27.

**2. Reverse it.** Write three different equations, each with x on both sides, whose solution is x = −2. Make one with brackets and one with a negative coefficient. Swap with a partner to check.
*Strong response:* e.g. 4x + 14 = x + 8 (both sides equal 6 when x = −2). Each checked by substitution.

**3. Always, sometimes, never.** "The solution to ax + b = cx + d is always positive." Try to prove or disprove with examples.
*Strong response:* sometimes. They give a positive and a negative solution and notice the signs of the numbers change the answer.

## Tier 2: Wider (use the skill in new contexts)

**4. Think of a number.** "I think of a number, multiply it by 3 and subtract 4. The answer is the same as double my number plus 5." Form an equation and solve it. Then write your own puzzle that has the answer 12.
*Strong response:* 3n − 4 = 2n + 5, n = 9. Their own puzzle has a consistent equation and a checked solution (e.g. 5n − 6 = 3n + 18).

**5. Shapes.** An isosceles triangle has two equal sides of 3x − 1 and x + 7. The third side is 2x. Find x and the perimeter.
*Strong response:* 3x − 1 = x + 7 gives x = 4. Sides are 11, 11 and 8, perimeter 30.

**6. Fractions.** Solve (x + 3)/2 = (2x − 1)/3.
*Strong response:* multiply both sides by 6: 3(x + 3) = 2(2x − 1), 3x + 9 = 4x − 2, x = 11. Check: 14/2 = 7 and 21/3 = 7.

## Tier 3: Beyond the course (a taste of Year 9 and 10)

**7. No solution, or every solution?** Try to solve 2x + 3 = 2x + 5 and then 2x + 3 = 2x + 3. What happens? Why? Write a sentence explaining each in your own words, then create one equation of each type.
*Strong response:* the first gives 3 = 5, which is never true, so no solution. The second gives 3 = 3, which is always true, so every number works. They explain it using the balance idea.

**8. Two things at once.** x + y = 10 and x − y = 4. Find x and y by reasoning (no method taught). Then invent your own pair.
*Strong response:* adding gives 2x = 14 so x = 7 and y = 3. A strong student explains why adding the equations removes y.

**9. Teach it.** Write a 5-line worked example with a "common mistake" warning that you could give a Year 7 class to teach solving equations with unknowns on both sides.
*Strong response:* correct, clear steps; the common mistake named; a check by substitution.

## Practical set-up
- Put the tasks on a single printed sheet or a slide titled "Challenge desk". Keep it available every lesson.
- They choose a tier, but must complete at least one from Tier 1 before moving on.
- Pick one task each week for a 3-minute whole-class discussion, led by one of the four. That gives them a real audience and makes the work matter.
