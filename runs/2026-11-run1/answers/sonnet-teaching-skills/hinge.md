# Mid-lesson hinge questions: adding fractions with different denominators

**Assumptions:** Year 8, you have just modelled adding two fractions with unlike denominators (for example 1/2 + 1/3 using equivalent fractions) and the next step is practice or harder examples. Pupils have mini whiteboards, or ABCD cards, or you use fingers (1 to 4). Every pupil answers at the same moment. Each question takes under a minute.

**How to run any of them:** Show the question. Give 20 to 30 seconds of silent thinking time, and say "show me" when everyone is ready. Scan the room before you say anything, then use the decision rule. Never tell them whether they're right until you've seen the spread.

The three options differ in what they test, so choose the one that fits where you are:

| Option | Best if... | Style |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | You want a quick check on the main idea | One correct answer |
| **2** | Pupils have been practising and you want to catch carry-over errors | One correct answer, harder |
| **3** | You want to check the *method*, not just the answer | Two correct answers (stated) |

---

## Option 1: The main idea

**1/2 + 1/3 = ?**

- **A** 2/5
- **B** 5/6
- **C** 2/6
- **D** 5/12

**Correct answer: B**

| Choice | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **A** 2/5 | Adding the tops (1 + 1) and the bottoms (2 + 3). Treating fractions like two separate whole numbers. |
| **B** 5/6 | Correct: 3/6 + 2/6 |
| **C** 2/6 | Added the tops (1 + 1) and used the common denominator 6, without converting the numerators. |
| **D** 5/12 | Converted correctly to 3/6 + 2/6, then added the denominators as well (6 + 6) as well as the numerators. |

**Can you get it right for the wrong reason?** Only by guessing 5/6. If a pupil is unsure, ask three of them to explain their choice in one sentence ("Why that one?") after the vote.

**Decision rule**
- **80% or more choose B:** move on to harder pairs, such as 3/4 + 1/6.
- **Many choose A:** reteach from the start. Show 1/2 + 1/3 with a fraction wall or bar model: "Is 2/5 bigger or smaller than one half? Then it can't be right." The main idea is that you can only add pieces of the same size.
- **Many choose C:** they know the denominator but not the numerators. Reteach "what happens to the top when the bottom changes?" using 1/2 = 3/6.
- **Many choose D:** they understand the conversion but think "adding" means adding everything. Use a pizza: "6 slices plus 6 slices of the same size is still 6 slices, but you have 5 of them."
- **Split between several:** pair a B pupil with an A pupil to explain, then re-vote.

## Option 2: Where the practice goes wrong

**3/4 + 1/6 = ?**

- **A** 4/10
- **B** 4/12
- **C** 10/12
- **D** 11/12

**Correct answer: D**

| Choice | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **A** 4/10 | Adding tops and adding bottoms (3 + 1 and 4 + 6). |
| **B** 4/12 | Changed both denominators to 12 but forgot to change the numerators (3 + 1 over 12). |
| **C** 10/12 | Converted 3/4 correctly to 9/12 but left 1/6 as "1" on top (9 + 1 over 12). Half-converted. |
| **D** 11/12 | Correct: 9/12 + 2/12 |

**Can you get it right for the wrong reason?** Very unlikely. The numbers are not friendly enough to guess, and the three wrong answers all come from real mistakes.

**Decision rule**
- **80% or more choose D:** set the practice set, with unlike denominators where neither is a factor of the other.
- **Many choose A:** the core idea hasn't landed. Go back to Option 1's reteach.
- **Many choose B:** they know to find a common denominator but think the top stays the same. Ask: "3/4 is three quarters. If I cut it into twelfths, how many twelfths is it?" Draw it.
- **Many choose C:** they only converted one fraction. Reteach "do the same to *both* fractions, top and bottom". Model with a visual.
- **Mixed:** group the pupils by the mistake they made, give each group a worked example for their error, then re-vote.

## Option 3: The method (two correct answers)

**Which TWO of these are correct ways to start 2/5 + 1/3?**

- **A** Rewrite as 6/15 + 5/15
- **B** Add 2 + 1 and 5 + 3 to make 3/8
- **C** Rewrite as 12/30 + 10/30
- **D** Rewrite as 2/15 + 1/15

**Correct answers: A and C**

| Choice | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **A** 6/15 + 5/15 | Correct: 15 is the lowest common denominator |
| **B** Add tops and bottoms | The "add across" misconception |
| **C** 12/30 + 10/30 | Also correct: 30 is a common denominator (not the lowest). Someone who picks only A may believe it has to be the *smallest*. |
| **D** 2/15 + 1/15 | Changed the denominators but did not change the numerators |

**Can you get it right for the wrong reason?** A pupil who picks only A or only C has half the idea. Ask pupils to show **two** answers. Anyone showing one answer has a partial understanding; this is useful information too.

**Decision rule**
- **80% or more show A and C:** move on. Mention that you can use any common denominator, and that the lowest one gives smaller numbers to simplify.
- **Many pick A only:** pupils think the denominator must be the lowest. Show that 12/30 + 10/30 = 22/30, which simplifies to 11/15, the same as 6/15 + 5/15. Then stress why the lowest is *easier*, not *required*.
- **Many pick B:** go back to Option 1's reteach.
- **Many pick D:** same as Option 2, choice B.

---

## Which one to use?

- **Not sure the main idea has landed?** Use **Option 1**. It's the fastest check.
- **Pupils have been working for a while?** Use **Option 2**. It catches the errors that appear once the method starts to feel familiar.
- **Want to check understanding rather than a procedure?** Use **Option 3**.

Whichever you choose, ask a pupil who got it right to explain *why* before you move on, then ask one who got it wrong what they were thinking. Their explanation will tell you exactly what to reteach.
