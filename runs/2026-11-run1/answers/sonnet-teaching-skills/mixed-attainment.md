# Curley's wife: one lesson, every student

**The approach:** one text, one big question, one task for everyone. Students differ in how much support they take and how far they push, not in what they are asked to think about. No one gets a "different lesson", and no one is visibly labelled.

**Assumptions:** about 60 minutes, you have read to at least the end of Chapter 5 (or are teaching this after Chapter 5), and the class has a copy of the text. Your two Year 5-level readers read slowly or with effort but can think and speak well about character.

## The big question

> **Is Curley's wife a villain, a victim, or something more complicated?**

It is open, it has a low entry point ("What did you notice about her?") and a very high ceiling (methods, context, interpretation). Everyone argues it from the same three pieces of evidence.

## The three evidence cards (same for all)

Print them on one A4 sheet, with line numbers or a gap beside each for annotation.

| Card | Where | What to quote |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Her entrance | Chapter 2 | "the rectangle of sunshine in the doorway was cut off"; "full, rouged lips and wide-spaced eyes, heavily made up"; "Her fingernails were red"; "Her hair hung in little rolled clusters, like sausages" |
| 2. Her loneliness | Chapter 5 | "I get awful lonely"; "I coulda made somethin' of myself" |
| 3. After her death | Chapter 5 | "She was very pretty and simple, and her face was sweet and young." |

Check each quotation against your edition before printing, as editions vary slightly in punctuation. Keep each card to a few lines so a weak reader isn't buried in text.

## Timeline

| Time | What happens | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | **Starter, from memory.** Three questions: Who is Curley? Where does Curley's wife first appear? Give one word the men use to describe her. Then ask: "Do we ever learn her name? Why might that matter?" | All. Oral answers first, so non-writers can shine. |
| 5-10 | **Set the question.** Show the big question. Students put a first vote on a scale (villain 1 ... victim 5) on a mini-whiteboard or a hand line. This is the "before" vote. | All |
| 10-20 | **Read the cards aloud, once, with the class.** You read Card 1, a confident reader reads Card 2, you read Card 3. Pause to gloss "rouged" (coloured red with make-up) and "brittle" (hard and easily broken). | All. Removes the reading barrier for the two Year 5-level students without singling them out. |
| 20-40 | **The core task** (below). Students work in mixed-attainment pairs for the first 5 minutes, then alone. | All |
| 40-50 | **Talk it out.** Stand-on-a-line debate: villain at one end, victim at the other. Cold-call using the question ladder below. Students can move. | All |
| 50-58 | **Exit ticket**: the same big question, one paragraph. Same success criteria for all. | All |
| 58-60 | Re-vote and note who moved. | All |

## The core task: Notice, Explain, Judge

Every student does the same three steps on the evidence sheet.

1. **Notice.** Underline one word or phrase on each card that makes you think something about her.
2. **Explain.** For each, finish the sentence: *Steinbeck makes her seem ___ when he writes "___". This makes me feel ___.*
3. **Judge.** Write 4 to 8 sentences answering the big question, using at least two of your three pieces of evidence.

### Support is on the table, not in your head

Put these on a side table, available to anyone, any time, with no questions asked. Tell the class: "Everyone can take a help card. Good writers use tools."

- **Sentence starters:** "Steinbeck shows she is lonely when...", "At first we think... but later...", "This word suggests... because..."
- **Word bank with meanings and a small picture or gesture** for rouged, brittle, lonely, dream, ambition, trapped, victim, villain, ambitious.
- **A "say it first" prompt:** "Tell your partner your answer in one sentence before you write."
- **A paragraph skeleton** with gaps to fill.

The two Year 5-level students can use all of these. Because the whole class sees support as normal, no one has to be seen taking it. Success for them is a spoken or written judgement with one quotation and one idea about why, which is a genuine, valid answer to the question.

### Challenge is also on the table, for everyone

Pin or place these "go further" prompts on the same sheet. Anyone who finishes the Judge step can pick one, and early finishers who think they have nothing left to say are pointed here.

1. **Method.** Pick one word or image on Card 1 and say what *else* it could mean. (Why does the light get "cut off"? What does "like sausages" do to our picture of her?)
2. **Shift.** Compare how we are asked to see her on Card 1 and Card 3. Why does Steinbeck show us both, and which is "truer"?
3. **Context.** The novel is set in 1930s California during the Depression. How might that shape what is possible for a young woman with ambitions? How might a 1930s reader respond differently from you?
4. **Interpretation.** Write your opening sentence so it holds two readings at once: "On one hand... yet..."
5. **Writer's choice.** She has no name. What would change if she did?

Stronger students should reach the "two readings, one judgement" level with these. For GCSE, this builds the three habits examiners reward: a clear personal response with well-chosen evidence, analysis of the writer's methods, and a sense of context. ## Same success criteria for everyone

Show these three lines and use them for the exit ticket:

- **Say it:** I answer the question and give a reason.
- **Prove it:** I use a quotation (even a short one) to back my reason.
- **Question it:** I say why it might also be read another way.

Most students will get the first two. Your strongest will reach the third on their own. The two Year 5-level readers are aiming for "Say it" with a firm "Prove it", and that counts as success. Say so out loud.

## Questioning ladder for the debate

Ask the same question of different students at different depths. All can answer; some answers go further.

1. "What word on the card makes you think that?" (anyone, quick, concrete)
2. "What does that word make you feel about her?"
3. "Can someone give me a different reading of the same word?"
4. "Does that change if we think about 1930s America?"
5. "Which of Steinbeck's choices makes us sympathise with her only after she's dead, and why might he do that?"

Use cold-calling (no hands up) so quieter and weaker readers aren't always passed over, but give them a "pass" rule: "I'll come back to you in a minute" after a partner chat.

## Marking and checking

Don't mark 30 paragraphs for three levels. Sort the exit tickets into three piles by eye using the three success lines. Use the piles to plan the next lesson's starter (pile 1: reteach "prove it" with a model; pile 2: push "question it"; pile 3: extension reading). Pick one anonymous strong paragraph to share next lesson.

## Handle with care

The death scene in Chapter 5 is violent. Look at your class before Card 3, and tell students it's a moment for thoughtfulness, not shock. The men's words for her ("tart", "tramp", "jail bait") are part of the evidence for how she is treated. Name this explicitly: the point is how the *men* label her, and whether the text invites us to agree.
