# Session plan: GCSE Chemistry, Year 10 (1:1, online, 60 minutes)

**Focus this week:** explaining *why* ionic compounds have high melting points, with the formulae method rebuilt through retrieval so she stops guessing. Covalent bonding is parked until the school starts it, but this session sets it up.

**Assumptions:** exam board not stated, so the content is pitched at the common GCSE level (AQA/Edexcel/OCR all expect the same ideas). Dot-and-cross for NaCl is secure. "S" is used for the student below.

**Why this focus:** she can draw the diagram but cannot explain the property. That is the gap that matters most for covalent bonding next week, because the covalent topic is taught by contrast ("simple molecular substances have low melting points because..."). The formulae guessing is real, but it is a method problem that short, repeated retrieval will fix, so it runs through the starter, practice and homework rather than taking the main teaching slot.

## 1. Retrieval starter (10 minutes, no notes)

Ask S to type or say each answer, then rate confidence: **guessing / think so / certain**.

| # | Question | Answer | Why asked |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is formed when sodium (Group 1) reacts with chlorine (Group 7)? Describe what happens to the electrons. | Sodium chloride; sodium loses one electron to form Na+, chlorine gains one to form Cl- | Last week's content, anchors ions |
| 2 | What charge does an ion from Group 2 have? Group 6? Group 7? | 2+; 2-; 1- | Missed pattern: the charge rule is what makes formulae stop being guesses |
| 3 | Write the formula of magnesium chloride and say how you got it. | MgCl2. Mg2+ needs two Cl- to balance 2+ and 2- | She guessed this last week |
| 4 | Write the formula of calcium oxide. | CaO (2+ and 2- balance, one of each) | Contrast with Q3: tests method, not memory |
| 5 | Write the formula of sodium oxide. | Na2O (two 1+ to balance one 2-) | Tests the other way round |
| 6 | State what is meant by an ionic bond. | The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions | Needed for today's explanation |

**What to listen for:** if she gets Q3 right but says "think so" or "guessing", the method has not yet become automatic. Do not move on until she can say "balance the charges".

## 2. Homework review (5 minutes)

Pick the two questions she found hardest. Ask her to talk you through each one: "What charges did you start with? What did you do next?" Mark the *method*. If she can state the charge of each ion, the rest follows.

---

## 3. Teach (15 minutes): why do ionic compounds have high melting points?

**Hook (1 min).** "Table salt doesn't melt in a hot oven, yet butter melts in your hand. What's different about what's inside them?"

**Explain and model (8 min).** Draw as you speak.

1. Ionic compounds are **giant ionic lattices**: millions of positive and negative ions in a regular pattern, not separate molecules.
2. Every ion is held in place by the **strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions** in all directions.
3. To melt it, the ions must be free to move. That means overcoming those attractions.
4. There are very many strong attractions, so it takes **a lot of energy**. So the melting point is **high**.

**The model answer, built with her** (sentence-by-sentence, she writes it herself):

> Ionic compounds have a giant lattice structure with strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions in all directions. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces, so ionic compounds have high melting points.

**Common mistakes to rule out out loud:**
- "The ionic **bonds** break" is fine; "the **atoms** break" or "the molecules separate" is not. There are no molecules in NaCl.
- "Strong bonds" alone does not get the marks. She needs the idea: **strong forces + many + a lot of energy**.
- Do **not** say "ionic bonds are stronger than covalent bonds". The covalent bonds within a molecule are also strong. Save this for next week.

**Check (3 min).** "Close the page. Explain to me in your own words, as if I'm Year 7, why salt has a high melting point." Listen for the three ideas. If one is missing, ask a question that points to it, not the answer itself.

**Bridge to covalent bonding (2 min).** One sentence only: "Next week the school will teach covalent bonding. When you get a property question, you'll be asked to say *what* is held together and *how strongly*. Today's answer is the template."

---

## 4. Guided practice (10 minutes)

You and S work through these together. She leads; you prompt only if stuck.

**Formulae (5 min), say the charges first:**
1. Lithium oxide: Li+ and O2- gives **Li2O**
2. Aluminium chloride: Al3+ and Cl- gives **AlCl3**
3. Magnesium bromide: Mg2+ and Br- gives **MgBr2**
4. Aluminium oxide: Al3+ and O2- gives **Al2O3** (stretch: lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6)

**Explain (5 min).** Question: "Explain why magnesium oxide has a very high melting point (about 2,850 degrees Celsius)." Build the answer together using the template. Prompt with: "What is it made of? What holds it together? What has to happen to melt it?" (Then discuss: higher than NaCl because 2+ and 2- ions attract more strongly. Optional extension only.)

---

## 5. Independent practice (15 minutes)

S answers alone, you watch method, not just answers. Do not rescue; note where she hesitates.

1. Write the formulae of: (a) potassium oxide (b) calcium chloride (c) aluminium oxide (d) magnesium sulfide (4 marks)
2. Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point. (3 marks)
3. Explain why magnesium oxide has a higher melting point than sodium chloride. (3 marks, stretch)
4. **Six-mark practice:** "Describe the structure of sodium chloride and explain its properties." (6 marks)
5. Quick confidence check: S rates herself 1 to 5 on "I can work out a formula without guessing".

**Answers**
1. (a) K2O (b) CaCl2 (c) Al2O3 (d) MgS
2. Giant ionic lattice (1); strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions (1); a lot of energy needed to overcome them (1)
3. MgO ions have bigger charges (2+ and 2-) than NaCl (1+ and 1-) (1); stronger electrostatic attraction (1); more energy needed to overcome (1)
4. Credit: giant lattice of Na+ and Cl- (regular, alternating) (1); strong electrostatic forces in all directions (1); high melting and boiling points as lots of energy is needed (1); does not conduct when solid as ions are fixed in place (1); conducts when molten or dissolved as ions are free to move (1); soluble in water (optional). Level of response: 5 to 6 marks for a logical, linked answer using all of these.

---

## 6. Exit check (3 minutes)

No help, no notes.
1. Write the formula for calcium chloride and say how you worked it out. **CaCl2: Ca2+ needs two Cl-.**
2. Finish the sentence: "Ionic compounds have high melting points because..." **...there are strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice and a lot of energy is needed to overcome them.**

If she gets both right, say so clearly. If not, repeat the failed one first in the next session's starter.

---

## 7. Homework (set in the last 2 minutes; 20 minutes in total)

1. **Retrieval from memory (5 min):** write the charges of ions in Groups 1, 2, 3 (metals) and 5, 6, 7 (non-metals).
2. **Formulae (5 min):** sodium sulfide, calcium oxide, aluminium bromide, lithium chloride, magnesium oxide, potassium sulfide.
3. **Explain (5 min):** "Explain why potassium chloride has a high melting point." Write it as three linked sentences.
4. **Look ahead (5 min):** write down what you already know about how atoms of non-metals bond (a sentence is enough, no looking it up). Bring it next week; it is a pre-check, not marked.

**Answers:** 1. 1+, 2+, 3+; 3-, 2-, 1-. 2. Na2S, CaO, AlBr3, LiCl, MgO, K2S. 3. As the model answer, with potassium and chloride ions.
