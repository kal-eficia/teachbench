# Cutting marking time this term without hurting learning

*Assumptions: a secondary school with marking and feedback policies that probably ask for regular written comments in books; about half a term to run a pilot; no extra budget. The proposals below are in order, from easiest wins to bigger changes.*

## The principle

Marking exists to improve what students do next, not to prove that a teacher has looked. The DfE's 2016 independent review of marking said marking should be **meaningful, manageable and motivating**. Where the written comment doesn't change what a student does, it can go. Where it does, feedback still has to happen, but the format can be quicker.

## 1. Replace most written comments with whole-class feedback

After reading a set of books or papers, the teacher fills in **one page**: what went well, the 3 or 4 most common errors with the fix, one model answer, and a short improvement task students do at the start of the next lesson.

- Put codes (A, B, C) on the pieces of work that need a specific fix. Students look the code up and act on it.
- Takes about 10 to 15 minutes per class set instead of 1 to 2 hours.
- Students get something to *do*, which is where the learning is.

## 2. Mark less often, deeper, and plan it

- Agree a **marking calendar** per department: one substantial piece of work marked in depth every 3 to 4 weeks, per class.
- Stagger across the week so no teacher has several big sets landing together.
- Everything else gets checked in class (see below) or not at all.

## 3. Move feedback into the lesson

- **Live marking:** circulate while students write and give verbal feedback on the spot. Students fix it immediately.
- **Self-check with model answers:** students attempt first from memory, then check against a model or mark scheme in a different colour. This works for quizzes, practice questions and retrieval homework.
- **Multiple-choice and short-answer quizzes** that can be scored in seconds by students or with an online tool, with the teacher looking only at the most-missed questions.

Be clear about one rule: students may check and correct their own *factual* work, but extended writing and anything that feeds a grade is still marked by the teacher, or moderated.

## 4. Stop what has no effect

Ask departments to agree to drop:

- Grading or ticking every page
- Written comments students never read or act on
- Triple-marking, dialogue columns, and any "must use green pen" style rule
- Lengthy marking codes that need a key to read
- Re-marking after a student has responded to feedback, unless it matters

## 5. Be clear what leaders will and won't check

A big hidden cause of over-marking is fear of book scrutiny. Say in writing:

- We will look at **whether students are acting on feedback and improving**, not at how much ink is on the page.
- No one will be asked to show evidence of marking beyond what the department's policy requires.
- Whole-class feedback sheets *are* acceptable evidence.

## 6. Make it a pilot, then measure

| Step | When | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Staff survey: marking hours per week, and which tasks feel pointless | Week 1 | All staff |
| Agree the new policy with 2 or 3 departments, pilot 4 to 6 weeks | Weeks 1 to 2 | Heads of department |
| Short drop-in: do students act on feedback? | Week 4 | Deputy head and HODs |
| Repeat the survey and compare, with a student voice sample | Week 6 | Deputy head |
| Decide: extend to all departments, adapt, or stop | End of half term | SLT |

## How you'll know learning isn't suffering

- Compare assessment results on the pilot classes with comparable classes, and with previous years.
- Check students' work in books: are corrections and improvements happening?
- Ask students: "Do you know what to improve in this subject?"

If these hold up, you have saved time without a loss. If one slips, change that part, not the whole approach.

## What to say to staff

Present this as **taking things off their plates**, not as another initiative. Name what is *stopping* (items in section 4) before what is starting. Tell them clearly it's a trial and their feedback shapes the final version. Visible actions from leaders, such as dropping the book-scrutiny emphasis, will do more for morale than any sheet.
