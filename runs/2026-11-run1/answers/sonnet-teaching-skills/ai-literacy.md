# Year 8 lesson (60 minutes): How do AI chatbots work, and how do I use one to learn?

**Assumptions:** mixed-ability Year 8 (ages 12 to 13), a classroom with one projector. Most school AI tools have age or account restrictions, so the plan works with a **teacher-led demo on the projector**, with an optional pair-work version if your school has approved a tool for students. The school's own AI-use rules are slotted into Section 5; adjust the wording to match your policy.

## Objectives
Students can:
1. **Explain** in simple terms how a chatbot produces its answers (it predicts likely next words from patterns in huge amounts of text).
2. **Spot** when a chatbot is wrong and say why that can happen.
3. **Write** a prompt that makes the chatbot help them learn instead of doing the work for them.
4. **State** when AI is and isn't allowed for schoolwork.

## Resources
- Projector and a chatbot you are allowed to use (or screenshots, see backup plan)
- Printed or shared student sheet (below)
- Mini whiteboards or paper

## 1. Starter (5 min): Real or made up?

Show three short statements about a topic the class knows well (use your school, a famous football team, or last term's topic). Two are true, one is invented. Students decide on whiteboards which is invented. Reveal.

**Say:** "Today you're going to find out why a machine that sounds this confident can still be wrong, and how to use it so it makes you smarter, not lazier."

---

## 2. How it works (10 min): the predict-the-next-word game

**Activity.** Say the start of a sentence and students shout, write or hold up the next word:
- "The cat sat on the ..."
- "Fish and ..."
- "Happy birthday to ..."
- "The capital of France is ..."

**Explain (use the student sheet):**
- A chatbot is trained on a huge amount of text from books and websites.
- It learns **patterns**: which words tend to follow which.
- When you ask it something, it **predicts a likely next word, then the next, then the next**.
- It does not "look up" the answer or "know" things the way you do. It builds an answer that **sounds right**.
- Because it is built to sound right, it can sound **very sure even when it is wrong**.

**One analogy, and its limit.** "It is like your phone's autocomplete, but trained on a huge library and much more powerful." The limit: it is much more advanced than autocomplete, but the basic idea of predicting words is the same.

**Check:** hold up a whiteboard: true or false? "A chatbot always looks up the correct answer." (False.)

---

## 3. Catch it out (15 min)

**Teacher demo, with the class.** Ask the chatbot something about a topic the **students** know better than most people (your school, a local place, a YouTuber, a game, a team's last match). Ask it for something specific, such as a date, a name or a statistic.

**Students:** work in pairs. For each statement the chatbot gives, mark it **Right / Wrong / Can't tell**. Ask: "How would you check?"

**Discuss:**
- What did it get right? What did it get wrong?
- Did it sound less sure when it was wrong? (Usually not.)
- If you hadn't known the topic, would you have spotted it?

**Key idea to leave them with:** chatbots can "hallucinate": make things up that look real, including fake books, fake quotes and fake websites. So: **check anything that matters** using a trusted source (textbook, teacher, a reliable website).

**Bias, in two minutes.** Ask the chatbot to "describe a doctor" or "write a story about a scientist". Is the person always the same type? Why might that be? (It learned from text written by people, and text can be unfair or incomplete.)

**Backup plan:** if there is no access to a chatbot, use the pre-prepared example in the student sheet (a made-up answer about your school), and ask students to find the problems.

---

## 4. Prompts that help you learn (15 min)

**Model it.** Put two prompts on the board:

| Weak prompt | Strong prompt |
|---|---|
| "Write my essay on the Romans." | "Quiz me on the Roman army, one question at a time. Wait for my answer. Tell me if I'm right, and explain it if I'm not." |
| "Do my maths homework." | "Here is a question I got wrong. Don't tell me the answer. Give me a hint to help me work out the first step." |

**Ask:** "Which of these will make you a better learner? Which one is cheating yourself?"

**The four-part prompt** (put it on the board):
1. **Role:** "Act as my revision tutor..."
2. **Task:** "Quiz me on..."
3. **Rules:** "One question at a time. Don't give the answer until I've tried."
4. **Level:** "I'm in Year 8."

**Pair task (7 min).** Each pair writes a strong prompt for a subject they are learning right now. They swap with another pair, who say whether it will make them think. (If you have approved student access, they test it. If not, you try two or three on the projector.)

---

## 5. The rules (5 min), then a quick check (5 min)

Explain the school's levels. For example:
- **No AI:** tests and some homework. Using it counts as cheating.
- **AI to check:** you do the work first, then use AI to check or give feedback.
- **AI as a tool:** the teacher says it's allowed.

**Ask:** "If a teacher doesn't say, what do you do?" Answer: ask first.

**Quick check (whiteboards, true/false):**
1. "If a chatbot says it, it must be true." False.
2. "I can copy a chatbot's answer into my homework and say it is mine." False.
3. "I can ask it to quiz me." True (unless the teacher says no AI).

---

## 6. Exit question (5 min)

Each student answers on a slip of paper: **"A chatbot gave me an answer that sounds right. What are two things I should do before I trust it?"**

Good answers: check it against a trusted source (book, teacher, reliable site); ask myself if it makes sense; remember it predicts words and can make things up.

---

## Misconceptions to listen for

| Students say | Fix |
|---|---|
| "It knows everything." | It predicts words from patterns; it can be wrong. |
| "It's thinking like a person." | It doesn't understand like a person does; it finds patterns in text. |
| "It's cheating to use it at all." | Not if you use it to learn and the rules allow it. |
| "If it sounds confident, it's right." | Sounding sure and being right are different things. |

## Differentiation
- **Support:** sentence starters for prompts ("Act as a... Quiz me on... One question at a time...").
- **Stretch:** "Write a prompt that gets a chatbot to teach you something *without* giving you the answer. Test it. What went wrong? Improve it."

## Optional homework (10 minutes)
Pick one subject you're learning. Write a strong four-part prompt for it. Bring it next lesson. (Only test it with a tool if your school or a parent has agreed you can.)
