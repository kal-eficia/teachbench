# Year 4 science: Electricity, a 6-week unit (6 x 1-hour lessons)

## Assumptions

- England, national curriculum Year 4 "Electricity": identify common appliances that run on electricity; construct a simple series circuit and name its basic parts (cells, wires, bulbs, switches, buzzers); say whether a lamp will light depending on whether it is part of a complete loop with a cell; recognise that a switch opens and closes a circuit; recognise some common conductors and insulators, and that metals are good conductors.
- A class of up to 30 working in pairs. You have roughly 15 kits, each with a cell (or two) in a holder, 2 bulbs in holders, 4 to 6 wires with crocodile clips, a switch and a buzzer. Extra items for the conductor and insulator test can be found in the classroom (paper clip, coin, foil, wooden spoon, plastic ruler, rubber, pencil, key, scissors blades, fabric, glass).
- Circuits are drawn as **pictures** this year. Standard circuit symbols are a Year 6 expectation, so introduce them only as a stretch.
- Only low-voltage cells are used. Nothing mains-powered is used in any lesson.
- No retrieval is possible from Year 3 electricity, because there was none; starters draw on earlier Year 4 lessons and on earlier science (materials, magnets, forces).

## What students should know by the end

1. Electricity powers many things we use, and mains electricity is dangerous.
2. A circuit needs a complete loop with no gaps, including a cell.
3. The parts have names: cell, bulb, wire, switch, buzzer.
4. A switch opens (gap) or closes (no gap) the circuit.
5. Conductors let electricity pass; insulators don't. Metals are good conductors.

## Key vocabulary (introduced as needed, not all at once)

circuit, cell, battery, bulb, wire, switch, buzzer, complete, loop, gap, conductor, insulator, metal, appliance, mains, series (as a name for "one loop")

## Common misconceptions (listen for these)

- "A bulb only needs one wire from the cell." (They need a loop.)
- "Electricity is used up on the way round, so the bulb nearest the cell is brighter." (This is the most persistent one; just plant the doubt in week 2 and return to it in week 5.)
- "Electricity comes out of both ends of the cell and meets in the bulb."
- "A switch lets electricity in; it doesn't matter where it is in the loop."
- "All shiny things conduct" or "all metals are shiny so they conduct"; also "plastic-covered wire doesn't have metal in it".
- "A cell and a battery are the same thing." (The national curriculum uses both; say "cell" in this unit and add that several cells together are called a battery.)

## Starters in detail (retrieval, one per lesson)

- **Week 2:** (a) Name the parts in the kit (picture cards). (b) What is it dangerous to do with electricity? (c) Name two things that need electricity to work.
- **Week 3:** (a) Draw a complete circuit with a bulb from memory. (b) A bulb isn't lighting. Give two possible reasons.
- **Week 4:** (a) What is a switch for? (b) What does "complete circuit" mean? (c) Name one thing you've built that has a buzzer in it.
- **Week 5 (mid-unit quiz):** 8 questions drawn from weeks 1 to 4, mixing the old and recent. Include one on the safe use of electricity from week 1.
- **Week 6:** one question from each week, the oldest first.

## Hinge question for week 2 (ask at the turning point of the lesson)

*A pupil connects a wire from the positive end of a cell to a bulb, and leaves the other end of the bulb unconnected. The bulb doesn't light. Why?*

- A. The wire is too short
- B. The cell is flat
- C. There is no complete loop back to the cell
- D. The bulb needs two cells

**Answer: C.** A (wrong) shows pupils think length matters. B (wrong) tells you they don't understand the idea of the loop and are guessing about a fault. D (wrong) means they think more power is the fix. If more than a quarter of the class choose anything but C, spend ten more minutes on the fault-finding task before continuing.

## How you'll know what they've learned

**1. Before:** the week-1 draw-and-label ("what's inside a torch") shows what each pupil thinks and gives you the baseline to compare with at the end.

**2. During:** 
- **Hinge question** in week 2 (above) and an exit question in weeks 3 and 4.
- **Observation, not just questions:** in the practical lessons, look for who gets a circuit working without help, and who explains it in their own words. A circuit that works but is explained wrongly ("the electricity goes in at one end and is used up") is a sign of a misconception that needs revisiting.
- **Predict-test-explain:** in week 4, check how many predictions were right and whether pupils can say why.
- **Mid-unit retrieval quiz** in week 5, marked with a quick whole-class review.

**3. After (week 6):** 
- A written quiz of about 10 marks that includes: naming the five parts (5 labels on a diagram), spotting why a circuit doesn't work (2 marks), explaining what a switch does (1 mark), sorting materials into conductors or insulators (2 marks), and one "explain why" (2 marks).
- A practical fault-finding task done in pairs: find and fix the fault, and explain it.
- Repeat of the week-1 drawing, for a before and after comparison.

**4. What the levels look like (a simple three-point guide)**

| | Working towards | Expected | Greater depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parts and loop | Names some parts; can build a circuit with help | Names all five parts; builds a working circuit independently; says a gap stops it working | Explains why a circuit fails and predicts what will happen if a part is added or removed |
| Switches | Can use a switch | Explains that a switch opens and closes the circuit, and links it to the bulb | Designs a circuit with a switch for a real purpose and explains where the switch could go |
| Conductors and insulators | Sorts some materials | Sorts materials correctly and says metals are good conductors | Explains how the results support their predictions; links to everyday uses and safety |

Record each pupil's level for each row on a class sheet. This lets you see at a glance who needs reteaching in week 5.

## Safety and practical notes

- Use cells only (never mains, never a battery pack with a large voltage).
- Do not allow wires to connect the two ends of a cell directly without a bulb or buzzer in between (this makes a short circuit, which heats the wire and drains the cell). Teach this as a simple rule: "Always put something in the loop."
- Bulbs and wires can get warm but not dangerously so; remind pupils not to hold them.
- Some materials in the week 4 test may be sharp (scissors, keys): use blunt items.
- Wash hands after handling any foil or coins if appropriate.

## Home heads-up (tell parents a week ahead)

- **Week 1:** a short note that the class are learning about electricity safety. Ask children to tell you one electrical safety rule they know. No home activity needed.
- **Week 4:** if you want to add a home activity, ask pupils to find 5 things at home that use electricity (not to touch anything plugged in).
- **Week 6:** a quick note about what they learned and a photo of their circuit.
