# 60-minute staff session: using AI to plan lessons

**Assumptions**
- 15 teachers, mixed subjects, most have never used AI. Some sceptical.
- The school has an approved AI tool and staff can sign in on a laptop or phone. Whoever is facilitating tests access the day before. If access is not available, use the fallback in step 4.
- It is a staff meeting slot, so there is no pre-work. Teachers will have a real lesson coming up to work on.
- I have not quoted statistics. Keep any claims to what you can show in the room.

## Goal

By the end, every teacher has used AI to produce a draft of a real lesson resource, has seen where it goes wrong, and has committed to one specific thing they'll do in the next two weeks.

## Before the session

- Ask staff, by email, to bring **a lesson or topic they're planning in the next fortnight** and a laptop or phone.
- Send the school's data rules in advance. State them again at the start.
- Print the handout and commitment cards (below). Put people in **pairs of mixed confidence and mixed subject** (with one group of three), and tell them in advance.
- Prepare one live example in your own subject, plus one deliberately flawed output to show.

## Timings and facilitator notes

| Time | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:05 | **Why this matters** | The problem, and the data rules |
| 0:05 to 0:15 | **The idea** | Three key points |
| 0:15 to 0:25 | **See it** | Live model, including a failure |
| 0:25 to 0:50 | **Try it** | Pairs work on their own lesson |
| 0:50 to 0:55 | **Share** | Two or three pairs show |
| 0:55 to 1:00 | **Commit** | The card |

### 0:00 to 0:05: Why this matters

- Open with a problem everyone recognises: "How long did your last lesson take to plan? How much of that was thinking, and how much was typing and formatting?"
- Say plainly: "AI can't teach your class. It can give you a first draft in two minutes, which you then fix. Today is about trying it, and I want to hear where you think it's rubbish."
- **Data rules (read them out and put them on the slide):**
  - Only use the school-approved tool.
  - Never put in student names, personal details, or any identifying information.
  - You are accountable for everything you use. Check it.
  - Don't paste in marks or student work.
- **For sceptics:** welcome their doubts and invite them to find faults. Their job in the pair is to be the "quality checker".

### 0:05 to 0:15: The idea (three points)

1. **Give it context.** It knows nothing about your class. Tell it the year group, topic, how long, what students already know, and what they find difficult.
2. **Say what you want back.** Be specific about format: "a 50-minute lesson plan with timings, three questions that check understanding, and a worked example".
3. **Treat the first answer as a draft.** Follow up: "Make the starter shorter", "Add a misconception question", "Rewrite for a lower reading age". The second and third prompts are where it gets useful.

Give a simple prompt recipe on the handout:
> I teach [subject] to Year [x]. The topic is [topic]. They already know [prior knowledge] and find [difficulty] hard. Write [what you want] for [time]. Include [anything specific]. Keep it simple enough to read aloud.

Also say: **The tool does not know your curriculum, exam board, or what you did last week. You do.**

### 0:15 to 0:25: See it

- Do a live demo in a subject you teach. Type a poor prompt first ("plan a lesson on fractions"), then a good one with the recipe. Show the difference.
- **Show it going wrong on purpose.** For example: ask for a worksheet with answers and check them aloud. Find an error (a wrong answer, a made-up fact or quotation, or an activity that doesn't fit the time). Say: "This is why you read it."
- Ask the room: "What would you change?" Take two suggestions and rerun the prompt.

### 0:25 to 0:50: Try it (the most important part, protect it)

In pairs, with one person typing and one person checking. **Switch halfway.**

**Task (on the handout):**
1. Open your upcoming lesson. Write a prompt using the recipe.
2. Read the output. Tick or cross each part: *Accurate? Right level? Fits my timing? Something I'd actually use?*
3. Follow up at least twice to improve it.
4. Pick one thing you'd use and one thing you'd throw away.
5. If you finish early: ask it for three questions to check understanding mid-lesson, with the common wrong answers explained.

Circulate. Help the nervous. Praise the sceptics' catches ("good, you found an error, that's the job"). Note two or three good examples.

**Fallback if there's no access:** project your screen. Pairs tell you what to type. You run it live, and pairs check the output on the handout.

### 0:50 to 0:55: Share

Two or three pairs (choose in advance, including one sceptic) show one useful thing and one thing it got wrong. Thank them.

### 0:55 to 1:00: Commit

Each teacher fills in a **commitment card** (below) and keeps it. Collect a copy of each or photograph them for the follow-up. Close with: "You don't have to like it. You just have to try it twice."

## Handout outline (one side of A4)

1. Data rules (4 lines)
2. Prompt recipe (as above)
3. Three follow-ups to try: "make it shorter", "add a misconception question", "make it easier to read"
4. Checking list: Accurate? Right level? Fits my time? Would I use it?
5. Practice task (as above)

## Commitment card

> In the next two weeks I will use AI to ____ (one specific task, for one specific lesson).
> I'll check it by ____.
> I'll tell ____ how it went.

## Follow-up

- **Two weeks later:** a 10-minute slot at the next meeting, or a short email: "What did you try? What worked? What got in the way?"
- **Share a template:** a shared document where teachers paste prompts that worked, by subject.
- **Optional:** pairs observe each other using one AI-prepared resource.
- **For those who weren't convinced:** that's fine. Ask them what would need to change for it to be worth using.

## Things to watch

- Don't show an overwhelming list of tools. One tool, one task.
- Don't claim it saves hours. Say "it gives you a draft to fix".
- If someone raises concerns about students and AI, acknowledge them and put them in a "parking" list to return to, so they don't derail the practice time.
