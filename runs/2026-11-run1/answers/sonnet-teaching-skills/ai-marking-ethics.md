# Guidance: using AI tools when marking student essays

**Assumptions:** you are a UK-style school, you have a data protection officer (DPO), and you want something short enough to circulate as a one-page policy. Check the final version against your own data protection and safeguarding policies before issuing it.

## The short version for staff

AI can help you give feedback faster. It must not decide a grade, and it must not be given anything that identifies a child. If you cannot do both of those things, don't use it for that piece of work.

## 1. Protect student data first

This is the point most likely to cause real harm, so it comes first.

- **Use only tools the school has approved.** Free consumer accounts often use what you type to improve their models and store it on servers outside the UK. A school-approved account, with a data agreement that rules this out, is the only acceptable route. Ask your DPO which tools those are. If there are none yet, the answer for now is "don't paste in student work".
- **Remove names and anything identifying before pasting.** That means names, class, school, teacher, and personal details students sometimes write into essays (family, health, home address, safeguarding disclosures). Use a number or initials.
- **Never paste work that contains a disclosure or sensitive personal content.** Follow your normal safeguarding route instead.
- **Students' work belongs to them and to the school.** Tell students and parents how AI is used in marking. Don't let it be a surprise.

## 2. AI must not decide the mark

Current tools are inconsistent. The same essay can get a different mark on a second run. They tend to reward length and confident, polished prose, and they can miss what the mark scheme actually asks for. They can also be wrong about the text itself, for example by praising a quotation that isn't in the essay.

So:

- **The teacher awards every grade.** AI output is a draft comment, never a mark.
- **Never use AI marks for reports, tracking data, predicted grades, or exam board work.** Where marking contributes to a qualification (NEA, coursework), follow the awarding body's rules. Most do not allow this, and you remain responsible for authenticity and for the marks submitted.
- **Moderate as normal.** If a department trials AI-assisted feedback, mark a sample by hand and compare.

## 3. Where AI can genuinely help

These uses are low risk and save real time, because the teacher still reads the work:

- Turning your own rough notes about a class set into a **whole-class feedback sheet** (common errors, a model paragraph, a short improvement task). This needs no student text at all.
- Drafting **comment banks** or sentence stems you then adapt.
- Producing a **checklist from the mark scheme** to mark against.
- Suggesting **improvement tasks** once you've identified a student's target.
- Tidying the wording of feedback you've already written.

Pasting whole essays for line-by-line comment is the higher-risk use. If a department wants to try it, do it only with an approved tool, anonymised work, and a rule that the teacher reads every comment before a student sees it.

## 4. Check everything before it reaches a student

Staff should read each AI-drafted comment and ask:

1. Is it **accurate**? Does it describe what the student actually wrote, and is any quotation really in the essay?
2. Does it match **our mark scheme** and what we have taught?
3. Is it **specific and kind**, in language the student can act on, with one clear next step?
4. Would I be comfortable **signing my name** to it?

The teacher is accountable for feedback, not the tool.

## 5. Be honest with students

- Tell students when feedback was drafted with AI assistance and checked by a teacher.
- Be consistent with how you ask students to use AI in their own work. It is hard to ban students from AI while quietly using it for everything yourself.

## 6. Review

Pilot with one or two volunteer departments for a half-term. Ask them: did it save time, were the comments accurate, did students act on the feedback? Report back, then revise this guidance. Review it at least once a year, because the tools and the exam board rules change quickly.

## One-paragraph version to put in the staff handbook

> Staff may use school-approved AI tools to help draft feedback, but never to decide marks or grades. Student work must be anonymised before it is entered, and work containing personal or safeguarding information must never be entered. Staff must read and check every piece of AI-drafted feedback before it reaches a student, and remain accountable for it. For qualification work, follow the awarding body's rules. Where AI has been used, say so.
