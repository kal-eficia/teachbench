# Year 10 electricity quiz: what went wrong and what to do

**Assumptions:** the class is 26 students, and you've given me counts and confidence for Q3, Q5, Q6, Q8 and Q9, not individual names. I haven't named students. I've treated the pattern across the class as the information.

## Summary: fix this first

**Current is used up in the bulb.** 14 of 26 said the current gets smaller after the bulb, and 9 of them were very confident. That is more than half the class, confidently wrong, and it underlies several other mistakes. It won't fix itself. It also probably explains Q5.

## The misconceptions, most urgent first

### 1. "Current is used up" (Q3)
- **The wrong idea:** "The bulb uses up some of the current, so less comes out."
- **Evidence:** 14 of 26 (54%), 9 confident.
- **Why it feels right:** In everyday language, "using electricity" is the same as using something up. A bulb gives out light and heat, so something must be lost. Students are mixing up current and energy.
- **The fix:**
  - *Contrast:* current is the flow of charge, and the charge keeps going round the loop. What goes down is **energy per unit charge** (potential difference). Energy is transferred to the surroundings as light and heat.
  - *Counter-example:* if current were used up, the same amount of charge couldn't be flowing all around a loop. Give the analogy of a **bicycle chain or a loop of rope**: the rope keeps moving at the same speed all the way round, while people along the way take energy out of it. (State the limits: it's only a model for steady current.)
  - *Demo:* put ammeters before and after a bulb in a series circuit. Ask students to **predict first**, write their prediction down, then reveal that both read the same. Predict, observe, explain.
  - *Reteach plan (5 min):* predict, demonstrate, explain "charge isn't used up, energy is transferred", then re-ask the question.
  - *Hinge question to check it's gone:* see below.
- **Revisit:** the next two starters.

### 2. Parallel bulbs are dimmer (Q5)
- **The wrong idea:** "Adding more bulbs means each gets less, so each is dimmer." (This is the right answer for series, so students are over-generalising a rule.)
- **Evidence:** 11 of 26 (42%), mostly not confident. So it's an unsure guess, a rule borrowed from series, more than a firm belief.
- **Why it feels right:** In series, extra bulbs *do* make each dimmer. Students apply "more bulbs, dimmer" everywhere.
- **Link to the first misconception:** some of these students may be thinking of a single supply of current being shared out or used up.
- **The fix:** Contrast series and parallel side by side. Each branch in parallel is connected directly across the battery, so each bulb gets the full potential difference and is as bright as a single bulb. The battery supplies more total current. Build both circuits and compare brightness, with students predicting first. Use a water-pipe or "each branch connects to the same pump" analogy with its limits stated.
- **Revisit:** a starter within a week, with a series/parallel pair.

### 3. "A battery stores charge/electricity" (Q8)
- **The wrong idea:** "A battery is a store of electricity or charge."
- **Evidence:** 12 of 26 (46%), 8 confident.
- **Why it feels right:** Everyday phrases such as "it ran out of charge", "recharge", "electricity". The charge is already in the wires, and it isn't what the battery supplies.
- **The fix:** A battery stores **chemical energy** and transfers it to the charges as they go through. The charges are already in the circuit. Link to energy stores and transfers in the earlier unit. Have students complete: "The battery ______ energy to the charge. The bulb ______ energy to the surroundings." Contrast the battery with a pump moving water that's already in the pipes.
- **Revisit:** a starter next week.

### 4. Voltmeter measures current (Q6)
- **The wrong idea:** a mix of confusion between meters, and blank answers (3), which may mean no idea.
- **Evidence:** 4 gave "current", 3 blank, so 7 of 26 (27%).
- **Why it's likely:** the two meters are confused, or "volts" is not tied to anything meaningful. This could be a gap more than a misconception, which is why the blanks matter.
- **The fix:** A quick lesson on what each meter measures and how it connects: ammeter in series (measures current, A), voltmeter in parallel across a component (measures potential difference, V). Practise "Which meter? How connected? What unit?" with a three-column table.
- **Revisit:** within the week; use a short retrieval question.

### 5. Unit of resistance (Q9)
- **Evidence:** 3 of 26 (12%).
- **Likely a slip or a gap**, not a shared misconception, because it is a small number and the answers differ (amps, volts). Fix with a quick unit-sort retrieval (current, A; potential difference, V; resistance, ohm). I wouldn't spend whole-class lesson time here.

## Pattern across questions
Questions 3, 5 and 8 all trace back to the same confusion: **current, charge and energy are being treated as the same thing.** If students think current gets used up and that a battery stores "electricity", they will struggle with potential difference, power and parallel circuits. Fixing the idea of energy transfer should help more than reteaching each question.

## Hinge questions to check the fix (one per main misconception)

Key positions: 1 = C, 2 = A, 3 = D, 4 = B. No repeats, no staircase, no position correct more than once. (Checked by hand: neighbours C-A, A-D, D-B are all different; no run of three; no staircase; each letter appears once.)

**Q1. A bulb is in a series circuit with a battery. The current before the bulb is 0.5 A. What is the current after the bulb?**
- A. 0.25 A, because the bulb uses up half the current
- B. 0 A, because the bulb uses it all up
- C. 0.5 A, because the current is the same all the way round
- D. It depends how bright the bulb is

*(Correct: C. A is the used-up idea; B is the extreme version; D is a guess about the bulb's effect.)*

**Q2. Two identical bulbs are connected in parallel to a battery. Compared with one bulb on its own, each bulb is...**
- A. just as bright, because each has the same potential difference across it
- B. half as bright, because the battery's current is shared
- C. twice as bright, because there are two paths
- D. dimmer, because there are more bulbs

*(Correct: A. B and D are the series rule; C is over-correcting.)*

**Q3. What does a battery do in a circuit?**
- A. It stores electricity until the circuit is switched on
- B. It makes new charge as the circuit runs
- C. It is a store of charge that runs out
- D. It transfers energy from a chemical store to the charges in the circuit

*(Correct: D. A and C are the "stores electricity or charge" ideas; B is another everyday idea.)*

**Q4. A voltmeter is connected...**
- A. in series with a component, to measure current in amps
- B. across a component (in parallel), to measure potential difference in volts
- C. in series with a component, to measure potential difference in volts
- D. across a component, to measure current in amps

*(Correct: B. Mixes meter, connection and unit.)*

## Plan for the next two lessons

**Lesson 1 (current and energy), about 15 to 20 minutes on this:**
1. Re-ask Q3 on mini-whiteboards with a confidence rating, without telling them the answer.
2. Run the ammeter prediction demo.
3. Explain "charge goes round, energy is transferred". Give a worked sentence for students to complete.
4. Ask hinge Q1 and Q3 on whiteboards. If more than about 80% are right, move on; if not, reteach with the rope-loop analogy.

**Lesson 2 (series vs parallel, meters):**
1. Starter: Q1 hinge question again (retrieval).
2. Build series and parallel circuits, predict brightness first.
3. Then meters: ammeter and voltmeter table.
4. Hinge Q2 and Q4.

**Starters to include over the next fortnight (spaced):**
- Next lesson: current is the same all the way round a series circuit.
- A few days later: battery transfers chemical energy.
- A week later: unit sort (A, V, ohm) and meter placement.

**Re-test:** ask Q3 and Q5 again in two to three weeks, with confidence, and compare with this quiz. Aim for a clear drop in confident-and-wrong answers.

## Caution
These counts come from one quiz. Look at which students gave wrong answers to more than one of Q3, Q5 and Q8; they probably have the same underlying idea and could be the first people to ask in the demo.
