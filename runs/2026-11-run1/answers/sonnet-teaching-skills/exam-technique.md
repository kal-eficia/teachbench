# Year 11 Geography: Fixing the 9-mark "assess" question (AQA GCSE)

## The brief and my assumptions

- 60-minute lesson, Year 11, AQA GCSE Geography. The students already hold the content, so **no new content is taught**. The lesson teaches one skill: turning knowledge into a judgement.
- AQA marks 9-mark questions by levels (Level 1: 1 to 3, Level 2: 4 to 6, Level 3: 7 to 9). Level 3 needs a detailed, well-developed answer that is balanced and reaches a supported conclusion. Where your paper's 9-marker also carries SPaG marks, tell students to write in full sentences with key terms spelled correctly; the plan below doesn't change.
- Where I give a case study, I use generic detail in square brackets. **Swap in your own case study facts**: [place + one number].
- Typical timing: about one minute per mark, so a 9-marker is about 10 to 11 minutes including planning.

## What is going wrong (diagnose before teaching)

Students who "know the content but lose marks" usually do one or more of these:

1. **They describe or explain instead of assessing.** Every sentence is true, none says *how effective* or *how important*.
2. **No judgement.** The answer just stops. No conclusion, or one that repeats the question.
3. **One-sided.** They write about what worked and never what limited it, or only one factor.
4. **Generic.** No place, no name, no figure. Level 3 needs specifics.
5. **No plan,** so the answer wanders and runs out of time.

**Before the lesson:** pull three or four anonymised 9-mark answers from the last mock. Give them to students at the start and ask them to find which of these five problems each has. Their own work makes the problem real.

## Objective (for students)

*By the end, I can write a 9-mark "assess" answer that weighs things up and finishes with a judgement.*

## Success criteria (give to students, to keep visible all lesson)

For a 9-mark "assess" answer, I have:
1. Spent one minute planning, and **decided my judgement before I started writing**.
2. A first sentence that gives my overall answer to the question.
3. At least **three** strategies, factors or impacts, each in its own paragraph.
4. **Specific evidence** in each paragraph (named place, name of strategy, a figure).
5. In each paragraph, said **how far / how effective / how important**, not only what happened. I've used "however".
6. **Compared** at least twice ("more effective than...", "whereas...").
7. A final sentence that gives a **judgement with a reason** ("Overall... because...").

**Paragraph frame: P-E-E-A.** *Point. Evidence. Explain. Assess (how far, and what limits it).*

## Phrases for assessing (put on the board)

*was effective because ... / however, this was limited by ... / to a large extent / only partly / was the most important because ... / was less important than ... / whereas / overall / it depends on ...*

## Lesson plan (60 minutes), I do, we do, you do

| Time | Phase | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 8 | Starter | Same fact, three levels of answer (below) |
| 8 to 20 | I do | Teacher models a full answer, thinking aloud |
| 20 to 32 | We do | Class builds a second answer together |
| 32 to 47 | You do 1 | Students write with the scaffold |
| 47 to 52 | Check | Students check their work against the criteria and improve it |
| 52 to 60 | You do 2 (starts) / exit | Judgement sentence, then homework set |

### Starter (8 min): what does "assess" add?

Show these three answers to the same question: *"Assess the effectiveness of sea walls."*

- **A:** "Sea walls are made of concrete and are built at the bottom of cliffs."
- **B:** "Sea walls reflect wave energy back out to sea, which stops waves reaching the cliff and so reduces erosion."
- **C:** "Sea walls are very effective at stopping erosion directly behind them because they reflect wave energy, however they are expensive at around [figure] per metre, so they are only worth it where there are homes or roads to protect."

Ask: *Which one is describing, which one is explaining, which one is assessing, and how do you know?* Take answers by cold call. Land the point: **C is the only one that says how effective, and why that's limited.** Then students spend 3 minutes marking the three real answers from their last mock for the same problems.

### I do (12 min): teacher model

**Task:** *Assess the effectiveness of hard engineering strategies in managing coastal erosion. [9]*

Write on the board as you go. Say the underlined parts out loud.

**Think-aloud script:**

"First, the command word. It says **assess**. I underline it. Not *describe*, not *explain*. Assess means I have to weigh it up: how effective, compared to what, and then decide. Marks: nine. That's about ten minutes. Plan for one.

My plan. Three strategies: sea wall, groynes, rock armour. For each I need: what it does, a place or figure, how effective it is, what limits it. **And now I decide my judgement before I write:** hard engineering protects the exact spot it's built, so it is effective there, but it's expensive and can make erosion worse further along. So my judgement is *partly effective: effective locally, limited overall.* I'll write that in my first sentence.

Introduction: *'Hard engineering can be very effective at protecting the area it is built to protect, but it is expensive and can cause problems elsewhere, so it is only partly effective.'* That answers the question straight away.

Paragraph one, sea walls. *'Sea walls are concrete walls built at the base of cliffs. They have a curved face which reflects waves.'* ... Hold on. I've just described a sea wall. Where's the assess? Nothing there tells the examiner how effective it is. **I'll fix it.**

*'Sea walls are very effective at stopping erosion directly behind them because the curved face reflects wave energy back out to sea, so the cliff is protected, for example at [your case study]. However, they are expensive, costing around [£ per metre for your case study], and need repairs, so they are only worth building where there is something valuable to protect.'*

There. What it does, evidence, how effective, what limits it.

Paragraph two, groynes. *'Groynes are wooden or rock barriers built out into the sea. They are effective at trapping sediment, building a wider beach that absorbs wave energy, as at [your case study]. However, they starve beaches further along the coast of sediment, so erosion there can get worse. This makes them less effective for the coastline as a whole than a sea wall, although they are cheaper.'* I've compared two strategies there. That's one of my criteria ticked.

Paragraph three, rock armour. *'Rock armour is large boulders piled at the base of the cliff. It absorbs wave energy and is cheaper than a sea wall, so it is more cost-effective. However, storms can move the rocks, and it can look unattractive, which may reduce tourism.'*

Now the conclusion. This is where marks are won. I don't just repeat my introduction. I weigh: *'Overall, hard engineering is effective where the land is worth protecting, such as homes and roads. Sea walls were the most effective at stopping erosion directly but the least cost-effective, whereas groynes and rock armour are cheaper but protect less. The overall effectiveness depends on whether the benefits are worth the cost, which is why it is only partly effective along a long coastline.'*

Finally I check: three strategies, specifics, 'however' in each paragraph, two comparisons, a judgement with a reason. Done."

**Model answer (full marks):** the script above, read in order, forms the model answer. Give students a copy to keep with the paragraphs labelled P / E / E / A.

### We do (12 min): class builds an answer

**Task:** *Assess the importance of human factors in causing river flooding. [9]*

Don't tell them what to write. Use these prompts:

1. "What's the command word, and what is it asking me to do?" *(weigh up; judge)*
2. "Which human factors? Give me three." *(urbanisation, deforestation, ...)* Write them up.
3. "Which one is most important in your view, and why? Decide now, before we write." Take two or three views and push for reasons. *(That's the judgement.)*
4. "For urbanisation: what's the point, what's the evidence, what's the explanation?" Then: "**Now assess it. How important, and what limits it?**" Prompt: "Is it the only cause? What else matters?" *(physical factors such as heavy rain and steep slopes also matter; urbanisation matters most in small, fast-responding catchments)*
5. Repeat for the second factor. Students write their own sentences on mini-whiteboards first, then share.
6. "Write our conclusion. Which is most important, and *why*? What does it depend on?"

**Check understanding before releasing them (hinge):** Show a sentence and ask the class to vote with fingers: *"Deforestation means trees are removed, so less water is intercepted and more reaches the river."* Is this **describe, explain or assess?** *(explain: it has a reason but no judgement of how important)* Ask what they'd add. If more than a quarter get this wrong, do another round before moving on.

**Model answer (we do):**
"Human factors are important in causing river flooding, but they are not the only cause, and in some places physical factors matter more.

Urbanisation is one of the most important human factors. Tarmac and concrete are impermeable, so rainwater cannot infiltrate and runs straight into drains and rivers. This increases surface runoff and shortens the lag time, so the river reaches peak discharge more quickly, as in [your UK case study]. However, urbanisation matters most in small catchments with a lot of built-up land; in a large catchment with little building its effect is smaller.

Deforestation is also important. Trees intercept rainfall and take up water through their roots, so removing them means more water reaches the river faster. However, this is less important than urbanisation where only small areas are cleared, because the effect on discharge is smaller.

Physical factors such as heavy rain, steep slopes and impermeable rock are also important. Heavy rainfall on already saturated ground will cause flooding wherever it falls, even with no human influence, so the physical cause is the trigger, while human factors make the flood worse.

Overall, human factors are most important in urban areas, where they make flooding quicker and more severe, but heavy rainfall is the main trigger. Human activity makes flooding more likely; physical conditions decide when it happens."

### You do 1 (15 min): with scaffold

Hand out the scaffold. Students have 12 minutes to write, 3 minutes to check.

**Task:** *Assess the importance of migration in the growth of cities in an LIC or NEE. [9]*

**Scaffold (on the sheet):**
> **Plan (1 min):** Command word: ___ . Three things I'll weigh: 1 ___ 2 ___ 3 ___ . My judgement: ___ is the most important because ___ .
> **Intro:** *"[Factor] is ... but ..."*
> **Paragraph 1:** Point / Evidence (place + figure) / Explain / Assess *(how far, however...)*
> **Paragraph 2:** Same, and compare with paragraph 1.
> **Paragraph 3:** Same.
> **Conclusion:** *"Overall, ... was most important because ... however, it depends on ..."*

**Model answer (you do 1):**
"Migration is a very important cause of city growth in LICs and NEEs, but natural increase also matters, and the relative importance depends on the city.

Rural-urban migration is the main driver in cities such as [your case study city]. Push factors such as poor farmland, lack of jobs and drought drive people out of the countryside, while pull factors such as jobs, schools and healthcare draw them to the city. For example, [one figure from your case study]. However, migration has slowed in some cities as the cities become more crowded and housing is harder to find.

Natural increase is also important. Migrants are often young adults, so they have children in the city, and the birth rate is high while the death rate falls thanks to better healthcare. This means the population keeps growing even after migration slows. This is more important in cities where migration has fallen.

Government policy is a third factor. Investment in industry, such as [your case study], can create jobs that attract more people, but this only matters if the city has the infrastructure to support them.

Overall, migration is the most important cause of the initial rapid growth, because it brings large numbers of people at once. However, natural increase becomes more important over time, because migrants have children and the growth continues. So the importance of migration depends on how long ago the city began to grow."

### Check (5 min)

Students swap books with a partner and **tick criteria 1 to 7** in pencil, pointing to the sentence for each tick. Partners write: *one thing that's assessed well* and *one place the answer only describes*. Then each student rewrites **one** sentence that only describes so it assesses.

### You do 2 (set as the exit task and homework): no scaffold

**Exit (5 min):** *"Write the final judgement sentence for: 'Assess the effectiveness of strategies to manage the effects of climate change.' Start with 'Overall...' and include a reason and a condition ('it depends on...')."*

**Homework (11 minutes, timed):** *Assess the effectiveness of strategies to manage the effects of climate change. [9]* No scaffold. Plan visible. Use the success criteria checklist.

**Model answer (you do 2):**
"Strategies to manage climate change can be effective, but their success depends on how many countries take part and how quickly they work, so overall they are only partly effective.

Mitigation, such as switching to renewable energy like wind and solar, tackles the cause by reducing greenhouse gas emissions. For example, [your case study: a country's % of electricity from renewables]. It is effective in the long run because it cuts carbon dioxide, but it is expensive to start, and the effects take decades to show because carbon already in the atmosphere stays there.

Carbon capture and planting trees remove or store carbon. Planting trees is cheap and also helps wildlife. However, forests take decades to mature, and the amount of carbon they store is small compared with global emissions, so they are less effective than switching energy sources.

International agreements, such as the Paris Agreement, are effective because they get many countries to set targets. However, they depend on countries keeping their promises, and since they are not always legally enforced, countries may miss targets, which makes them less reliable than local action.

Adaptation, such as building flood defences or growing drought-resistant crops, helps people cope now, and is effective in the short term. However, it doesn't stop climate change, so it is only a short-term fix.

Overall, mitigation is the most effective long-term strategy because it tackles the cause, but adaptation is more effective in the short term. The best approach combines both, but it depends on whether governments act together quickly enough."

## How to mark these in class

Mark by levels, quickly, with three questions:
1. **Does it reach a judgement?** If no, it is capped at Level 2.
2. **Is it balanced** (each point has an *however* or a limit)? If no, probably Level 2.
3. **Is there specific evidence** in each paragraph? If no, it's lower in the level.

A Level 3 answer should have all three. Whole-class feedback next lesson: read out two anonymous sentences, one that assesses and one that doesn't, and ask students to explain the difference.

## Follow-up (to make it stick)

- **Next lesson starter:** a timed 5-minute planning task on a new 9-marker. Plan only: three things, a judgement, no writing.
- **Within a fortnight:** another timed 9-marker, no scaffold, marked with the same 7 criteria.
- **Spacing:** use "assess" questions from different units (physical, human, environmental) so students learn the skill transfers, not that it belongs to one topic.
