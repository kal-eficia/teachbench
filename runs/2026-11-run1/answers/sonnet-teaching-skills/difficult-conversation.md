# Meeting Prep: Parent Concern, "Picking On" a Year 9 Student

*(I've used "S" for the student and "Mum" for the parent. Add nothing identifying to emails or notes you share.)*

## Before the meeting (today)

1. **Talk to your head of year first.** Agree who leads, who takes notes, and what each of you will say. Ask whether this has been logged as a formal complaint. If it has, school procedure applies, and that changes how the meeting runs.
2. **Gather the evidence:** the dates of the three detentions, what was said or recorded each time, the warnings given beforehand, and where S sits. Check you can show you apply the same rules to other students: if others also had no equipment or talked, were they treated the same?
3. **Check the other two detentions** with the head of year: which teachers, and what for. The pattern across lessons matters.
4. **Ask yourself honestly:** was each warning given clearly before the detention? Has anything been said in a tone you would handle differently? If you find something, own it in the meeting. It builds trust.

## Facts (keep these separate from interpretations)

- Three detentions from you this half-term. Reasons: no equipment, and talking over you.
- Two further detentions from other teachers.
- Mum reports S comes home crying.
- **Mum's interpretations:** you are "picking on" S; you "clearly don't like her". These are views and feelings. You don't need to argue with them, but you do need to listen to them.

## What Mum is likely worried about underneath

- S is upset, and Mum feels helpless.
- She fears S is being treated unfairly or singled out.
- Possibly: equipment costs, something else going on for S (friendship, anxiety, home), or S not feeling safe to talk to you.

## Your goal

Mum leaves believing you care about S and that you have a plan. S leaves with a fresh start. You agree what happens next, and when you'll review.

**Not negotiable:** the expectations (equipment, listening when the teacher talks) and your right to use the school's sanctions.
**Negotiable:** how you support S in meeting them (equipment solutions, seating, a quiet word instead of a public warning, a check-in).

## Opening (30 seconds)

> "Thank you for coming in, and for telling me how S is feeling. I'm sorry she's been upset. We both want her to do well and to feel good about my lessons, so I'd like to hear what S has told you, share what I've seen, and then agree what we can do together."

## Listen first

Let Mum finish. Then summarise: "So what I'm hearing is that S is coming home upset, and you're worried I'm treating her differently from others. Is that right?" Don't defend yet.

## Share the facts calmly

"These are the three occasions: [dates]. On each, S didn't have her equipment or was talking while I was teaching. I gave a warning first, then a detention, as I do with any student." Use facts, not labels: not "she's always chatty", but "three times in this half-term."

## Likely moments and calm responses

| What Mum says | A calm reply |
|---|---|
| "You clearly don't like her." | "I can see why it feels that way, and I'm sorry it does. I do want S to do well. What would help her see that?" |
| "She comes home crying." | "That matters to me. Thank you for telling me. I'd like to talk to S, and I'd like us to work out what's upsetting her, whether that's my lessons or something else." |
| "Other kids talk and you don't give them detentions." | "I use the same steps for everyone. If S feels others are treated differently, I'd like to look at that, and I'll keep a closer eye on it." (Only say this if true; check your records.) |
| "She can't afford the equipment." | "Thank you for telling me. That's easy to fix. I can give S [pen/ruler/etc.] each lesson, or the school can help." |
| "The other teachers never have a problem." | "Two other teachers have also given S detentions, so I think we should all look at what's going on. [Head of year] can help us work that out." |
| "I want you to stop giving her detentions." | "I can't promise that if expectations aren't met, but I can promise warnings are clear and I'll help S meet them." |
| Anger or tears | Pause. "Take your time." Lower your voice; don't match the volume. |

## Agree next steps

- **You:** a quiet word and a fresh start with S; kit available if needed; a warm, private reminder before the lesson, not a public telling off.
- **S:** brings equipment; listens when you talk. Agree something small she can do.
- **Head of year:** checks in with S, looks at the five detentions together.
- **Mum:** tells you if S is upset again or if anything changes.
- **Review:** in two to three weeks.

**Close on the shared aim:** "Thank you. We both want S to enjoy school and do well."

If S's distress sounds like it comes from something bigger (bullying, worries, something at home), pass that to the designated safeguarding lead through your school's usual route.

## Follow-up email (send same day)

> Dear [Mum's name],
>
> Thank you for coming in today. I know it wasn't an easy conversation, and I'm glad you told me how S has been feeling.
>
> We agreed that:
> - I will give S a fresh start, and I'll remind her about equipment privately before lessons.
> - [Equipment support agreed.]
> - [Head of year] will check in with S.
> - We'll review in two to three weeks, and you'll let us know if anything changes at home.
>
> We both want S to do well and to feel positive about school.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Your name]

*Assumptions: your school has a behaviour policy with warnings before detentions, and you are the class teacher. If Mum has made a formal complaint, follow the school's complaints procedure.*
