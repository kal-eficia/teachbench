# Running a 45-minute department meeting on AI-written differentiated worksheets

## Assumptions

- Secondary maths department of about 5 to 8 teachers, with a school-approved AI tool (or at least one the school permits for teacher use).
- The two sceptics' worry about laziness is about **students** relying on AI. Teachers will be using the tool, and students won't touch it in this trial. You'll want to check that is what they meant, early in the meeting.
- The aim of the meeting is not to convince everyone. It is to get every teacher to run one small, safe trial and bring back evidence.

## Before the meeting (about 30 minutes of your prep)

1. **Check your school's data and AI rules** and have them on one slide. The rule for the trial: no student names, marks or personal data go into the tool.
2. **Make one "seeded errors" worksheet.** Ask the AI tool for a differentiated worksheet on a topic the department is about to teach (for example, solving linear equations, in three versions: support, core, stretch) with an answer key. Check it carefully yourself and note every error you find. The tool will very likely give at least one slip (a wrong answer, a question that doesn't match its difficulty tier, an unrealistic context, or a question with no clean answer). If it is flawless, edit in two errors yourself and say so honestly afterwards. Print one copy per pair.
3. **Choose the topic for everyone's trial** (next two weeks' content) so the practice is real and every teacher produces something they will use.
4. **Send a two-line pre-read:** "Come to see a worksheet the AI produced. Be ready to find what's wrong with it."

## The 45-minute agenda

| Time | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | **Why we're here** | State the problem everyone recognises: writing three versions of a worksheet takes hours, and the weaker and stronger groups often get the leftovers. Say plainly what this meeting is **not**: no one is being told to use AI, and no student will use it. The decision today is only whether to run a small trial. Ask the two sceptics to say their worries in one sentence each and write them on the board. |
| 5 to 15 | **The idea and the risks** | Three points, no more: (1) The AI is a first-draft tool; the teacher is the quality control. (2) The biggest risk is errors, so there is a mandatory checking routine (below). (3) Good differentiation changes the **support**, not the thinking: weaker groups get scaffolding and worked steps, stronger groups get reasoning and problem-solving, and everyone has to do the maths. |
| 15 to 25 | **Error hunt** | Pairs get the seeded-errors worksheet and answer key. Five minutes to find every error. Then share: what kinds of mistakes were found, and how did you find them? Expected result: the group discovers the tool can be wrong, and also that they are able to spot errors quickly. This answers the worried colleague with evidence from their own hands, rather than your reassurance. |
| 25 to 37 | **Try it** | Each pair writes a prompt for the agreed topic, runs it, and **checks** the result using the checking routine. They should aim for one tiered worksheet each. (Show a model prompt on screen first, see below.) |
| 37 to 42 | **Share** | Two pairs show what they made and one thing the AI got wrong or did well. |
| 42 to 45 | **Commit** | Each teacher fills in the commitment card (below). Agree the review date. |

## Facilitator notes: handling the two concerns

**"It will make students lazy."**
- First, clarify: "Are you worried about students using AI, or about the worksheets being easier?" If it's the first, say the trial has no student use of AI. If it's the second, you have a good shared principle: *the worksheet must not lower the challenge*.
- Agree a rule: differentiation by scaffold, not by dilution. Support versions have worked examples and sentence starters; stretch versions have reasoning prompts ("explain why", "find the error", "what if the numbers change?").
- Invite the sceptic to be the **challenge checker**: when the worksheets come back from the trial, their job is to look for versions that make the thinking too easy.
- If the worry is about later student use of AI, acknowledge it as a separate, important conversation for another meeting, not one to settle today.

**"It will make errors."**
- Agree: it will. That is why the error hunt is in the meeting.
- Introduce the checking routine and put it on the handout:
  1. **Solve every question yourself first**, or at least every question in the stretch version, and compare with the key. Never trust the key without doing this.
  2. **Check difficulty tiers**: is the "support" sheet really easier, and is the "stretch" sheet really harder?
  3. **Check the context**: realistic numbers, sensible units, a clean answer where one is expected.
  4. **Check the notation and wording** against the department's conventions.
  5. **Spot-check the answers by a second method** (estimate, substitute back, or use a calculator).
  6. **Sign off**: the teacher's initials go on the master copy once checked.
- Make the point honestly: checking costs time, but it is quicker than writing three versions from scratch, and the worry about errors does not go away if we ignore the tool. We stay in control by checking.
- If the worried colleague wants to go further, ask them to keep a tally of errors found during the trial. That makes the risk measurable.

**General stance:** don't argue with scepticism; give it a job. Both sceptics are most useful as the people who check the quality, and the trial should be designed so that their concerns are tested rather than dismissed.

## A model prompt to show on screen

> I teach Year 9 maths. Write three versions of a worksheet on solving two-step linear equations. Version A (support): 8 questions, with a fully worked example at the top and the first step shown on the first 4 questions. Version B (core): 8 questions with no support. Version C (stretch): 6 questions including 2 that ask students to find and explain an error, and 1 that asks them to write their own equation with a given solution. UK GCSE style. Use whole-number answers except where stated. Give a separate answer key. Do not use student names.

The point to make: the better the prompt (year, topic, number of questions, type of scaffold, answer style), the less correcting afterwards.

## The practice task (25 to 37 minutes)

For the agreed topic:
1. Write a prompt (start from the model).
2. Generate the worksheet and key.
3. Run the six-step checking routine and record every error found and fixed.
4. Rate it: would I use this, with edits, next week? Yes / Maybe / No, and why.

## Commitment card (each teacher fills in at 42 to 45 min)

- In the next two weeks I will use an AI-drafted worksheet with: **class** ________ **topic** ________
- I will check it using the six-step routine before it goes to students.
- I will record: time taken (compared with my usual), number of errors found, and what the students did with it.
- I will bring it to the review on: ________ (date)

## Follow-up plan

- **Day 3:** one-line message from you: "How's the first attempt going? Any errors worth sharing?"
- **Shared folder** for the prompts, the checked worksheets and the error log, so the department builds a bank of good prompts and known problems.
- **Two-week review (15 minutes at the next department meeting):** each teacher says (1) time saved or not, (2) errors found, (3) whether the sheets worked in class, (4) whether students were challenged enough.
- **Decision at the review:** continue, change the approach, or stop. Agree this in advance so the trial has a real end point. If the error count or the quality concerns are too high, stopping is a valid and respectable outcome.
- **Optional:** one colleague observes another giving the worksheet to see how students respond.

## How you'll know the meeting worked

- Everyone leaves with a completed commitment card.
- The two sceptics have a defined role in the trial.
- The department has an agreed checking routine, and the date for the review is in the diary.
