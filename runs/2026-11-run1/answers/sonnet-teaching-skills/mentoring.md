# Mentoring Session Plan: First Session (20 minutes)

**Student:** S (Year 11) | **Predicted:** grade 4s | **Capable of:** grade 6s | **Her words:** "I'm just not motivated." | **Current revision method:** reading her notes

**Assumptions:** This is the first meeting, so there is no previous target to review. Your 5-minute "review" slot is used instead to find out what she already does and what she's already good at. I've assumed you meet again in a week, and that she has at least one subject she can use as the working example. If she's predicted 4s across the board, let her pick the subject. I'm using "S" for the student. Keep any notes in line with your school's data policy.

## The big idea for this session

"Not motivated" is usually a conclusion students draw from a different experience: *I revise, it doesn't seem to work, so why bother?* Reading notes feels like revision but gives a weak sense of progress, because it feels easy and familiar without testing what she can actually remember. So the plan is:

1. **Listen first.** Don't argue with "not motivated". Find out what it means for her.
2. **Show, don't tell.** Let her experience, in five minutes, that remembering from a blank page feels harder but finds the gaps, which gives her something concrete to work on.
3. **Leave with one small action** that she chooses. Motivation often follows a first small win rather than coming before it.

You are not fixing her revision in 20 minutes. You are making the second session easy to say yes to.

## Step by step

### 1. Check-in (3 minutes)
**Say:** "Thanks for coming. This isn't a telling-off or a grade conversation. I'm here to help you get where you want to be, and you'll do most of the talking."

**Ask (pick one or two):**
- "How are things going at the moment, in school and outside it?"
- "What's one thing that's gone well this week, however small?"

**Listen for:** workload, tiredness, worries about home or friends, anything outside revision. *If anything raises a wellbeing or safeguarding concern, follow your school's procedure rather than carrying on with the plan.*

### 2. What's already working (5 minutes)
**Ask:**
- "You said you're not feeling motivated. What does that look like for you? What happens when you sit down to revise?"
- "When was the last time you *did* feel motivated about something at school? What was different?"
- "Which subject, or which piece of work, are you most proud of this year?"
- "What grade would you *like* to get in a subject that matters to you?" *(Let her answer before you mention your view that she's capable of 6s.)*

**Then:**
- Say what you've noticed that's positive, in specifics: "You told me you do revise, and you've kept going. Many students don't. I also noticed that you [something real she said]."
- Scale question: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how confident are you that you could get a grade 6 in [subject] if you found the right way to revise? What stops it being one point lower?" *(Asking why it isn't lower gets her to name her own strengths.)*

**Notes on what you're likely to hear (and how to respond):**

| She might say | You could respond |
|---|---|
| "I don't see the point, I'll just get a 4 anyway." | "What makes you think it'll be a 4?" Then: "Which bits of the course feel hardest, and which feel easy?" |
| "I do revise, I read my notes for hours." | "That sounds like real effort. Can I show you something quick, to see how much of it is staying in your head?" |
| "I'm tired / too busy." | "What does a normal week look like?" Then in step 4, make the action very small. |
| "I don't know" or silence | Wait. Then: "Take a guess. Or what would a friend say about you?" |

### 3. Focus: one subject, one method (9 minutes)

Pick **one subject** (her choice) and **one topic** she has revised by reading. The aim is for her to see the difference between *reading* and *remembering*.

**a) The blank-page test (live demo, about four minutes)**
1. "Think of the last topic you read your notes on in [subject]. Pick a small one."
2. Give her a blank sheet. "Close your notes. For two minutes, write down everything you can remember about it. Don't worry if it's messy."
3. "Now open your notes. In a different colour, add what you missed."

**b) Ask, don't tell (about four minutes)**
- "What did you notice?"
- "How did that feel compared with reading your notes?" *(It will probably feel harder. Say: "Harder is actually a good sign. That's the part that makes it stick.")*
- "What did you remember well? What was missing?"
- "If you did that for ten minutes after each lesson, what difference do you think it would make?"
- "What might get in the way of trying this at home, and what could you do then?"

**c) Offer one idea, after hers (about one minute)**
Only if she hasn't got there herself, offer: "Many students find that the quickest way to improve is to swap *reading* for *testing yourself*: blank-page recall, flashcards, or past-paper questions from memory. The bits you miss show you what to revise next, so you spend your time on the right things."

**Aim for her to say:** "I can see which bits I don't know." That's the first small win, and it's *hers*.

### 4. Action: one small step she chooses (2 minutes)
**Ask:** "What is *one* thing you could try this week? Make it small enough that you're nearly certain you'll do it."

Help her make it specific (when, where, how long, what, and what might get in the way):

> "I'll do a 10-minute blank-page recall on [topic] after school on **Tuesday** and **Thursday**, at the kitchen table, before I check my phone. If I'm too tired, I'll do 5 minutes."

**Check:**
- "On a scale of 1 to 10, how sure are you that you'll do it?" *(If it's under 7, make it smaller.)*
- "What could get in the way, and what will you do then?"

### 5. Close (1 minute)
- "What's one thing you're taking away from today?"
- "Next time we'll look at how it went and pick the next small step. Is [day/time] good?"
- End on something real and positive that she did in the session.

---

## What to avoid
- **Don't** tell her she's "capable of more" in a way that adds pressure; ask what *she* wants.
- **Don't** offer a list of five strategies. One method, one action.
- **Don't** compare her to other students; compare her to where she is now.
- **Don't** do more than half the talking.

## If you have extra time
- Ask her to show you how she normally revises, in a notebook, so you can see it.
- Ask what *kind* of help would be useful to her: a revision partner, a quiz sheet, a check-in message midweek.
