# Modelling an analytical paragraph: 'The Eagle' (Year 7)

**Assumptions:** the class has read the poem once and knows the words "metaphor", "personification", "simile" and "alliteration" at a basic level. The structure is **Point, Evidence, Explain, Zoom out**. The same question runs through all tasks so the skill, not the content, is what changes: **How does Tennyson present the eagle as powerful?**

**The poem (Tennyson, 1851)**

> He clasps the crag with crooked hands;
> Close to the sun in lonely lands,
> Ring'd with the azure world, he stands.
>
> The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
> He watches from his mountain walls,
> And like a thunderbolt he falls.

**Success criteria (put these on the board, in student words)**
1. My first sentence answers the question.
2. I use a short quotation, in speech marks.
3. I name the technique correctly.
4. I explain the effect: what picture or feeling does it give the reader, and why?
5. I finish by linking to the big idea.

---

## I DO (teacher models, about 6 minutes)

**Line to analyse:** "He clasps the crag with crooked hands."

**Say aloud as you write:**

"The question asks how Tennyson presents the eagle as *powerful*. So my first sentence has to use that word, so I know I'm answering the question and not just describing the poem.

*(Write:)* **Tennyson presents the eagle as powerful from the very first line.**

Now I need evidence. I want something small, not a whole line. The word that jumps out at me is 'clasps'. *(Write:)* **The verb "clasps" suggests a strong, firm grip.**

Now I need to say what that does. I'll write: *(Write:)* **This is good because it makes you imagine the eagle.**

*Pause.* Hang on. 'Good' and 'imagine' are too vague. If a teacher read that, they would ask, 'Imagine what?' Let me fix it. What do I actually picture? Someone holding on so tightly that nothing could pull them off. So: *(Cross out and rewrite:)* **It makes the eagle seem as if it is holding on so tightly that nothing could move it, which shows its strength.**

Next I want to say something about 'crooked hands'. Eagles have claws, not hands, so this is a metaphor. The poet is calling the claws 'hands'. Why? I think it makes the eagle seem almost human, like a king or a ruler, but the word 'crooked' also makes me think of hooks, ready to grab prey. *(Write:)* **The metaphor "crooked hands" turns the eagle's claws into hands, which makes the bird seem almost human, but the curved shape also suggests hooks ready to seize prey.**

I can hear something too. 'Clasps', 'crag', 'crooked': lots of hard c sounds. That's alliteration. It sounds rough, like claws scraping on rock. *(Write:)* **The hard "c" sounds in "clasps the crag with crooked" sound rough, like claws scraping on stone.**

Last, I zoom out and link back to the question. *(Write:)* **Together, these details make the eagle seem like a ruler gripping his throne, completely in control of the high place.**

Now I check against my success criteria. First sentence answers the question? Yes. Quotations? Yes. Technique named? Yes: metaphor and alliteration. Effect explained? Yes. Link? Yes."

**The finished model paragraph (leave on the board):**

> Tennyson presents the eagle as powerful from the very first line. The verb "clasps" suggests a strong, firm grip, as if the eagle is holding on so tightly that nothing could move it. The metaphor "crooked hands" turns the eagle's claws into hands, which makes the bird seem almost human, but the curved shape also suggests hooks ready to seize prey. The hard "c" sounds in "clasps the crag with crooked" sound rough, like claws scraping on stone. Together, these details make the eagle seem like a ruler gripping his throne, completely in control of the high place.

## WE DO (together, about 8 minutes)

**Line:** "The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls."

**Prompts, one at a time. Take a few answers each, and ask "why?" every time.**

1. "What's our first sentence? Which word from the question must it include?" *(Accept: "Tennyson also shows the eagle's power through how small and slow the sea looks below him.")*
2. "Which word should we choose as evidence?" *(Accept "wrinkled" or "crawls".)*
3. "Can the sea really crawl or be wrinkled? So what technique is this?" *(Personification.)*
4. "What does a wrinkled, crawling thing make you think of? Old, slow, small? How does the sea look from very high up?"
5. "So what does that say about the eagle?" *(It is so far above that the huge sea looks tiny and weak.)*
6. "Which sentence will link back to our big idea?"

**Scaffold on the board:**
- *Tennyson also shows ... through ...*
- *The word "..." suggests ...*
- *This personification makes the sea seem ..., which shows the eagle is ...*
- *Overall, ...*

**Model answer:**
> Tennyson also shows the eagle's power through the way the sea looks from above. The personification "the wrinkled sea beneath him crawls" makes the huge ocean seem old, slow and tiny, like a creature crawling far below. This suggests that the eagle is so high up that even the sea looks weak, which makes the eagle seem like a ruler looking down on everything he controls.

**Hinge question before releasing the class (hands up for A, B, C or D):**
Which is the best explanation of "the wrinkled sea beneath him crawls"?
- A. It makes the poem good because it uses a lovely description.
- B. It tells us the sea is wrinkled and that it crawls.
- C. The personification makes the sea seem small and slow, so the eagle seems far above it and powerful.
- D. The poet uses rhyme to make the line sound nice.

**Correct answer: C.** A is vague, B retells, D names a technique that is not in the line. Move on if most pick C; if many pick B, re-teach "explain the effect, not what it says."

## YOU DO 1 (with the scaffold, about 8 minutes)

**Line:** "And like a thunderbolt he falls."

Students write one paragraph using the same four-step scaffold. Scaffold available but not forced.

**Model answer:**
> Tennyson shows the eagle's power at the end of the poem too. The simile "like a thunderbolt he falls" compares the eagle to lightning, which suggests he is incredibly fast, sudden and dangerous. This is especially powerful because the eagle has been still and watching, so the sudden dive feels shocking. Overall, the eagle seems like a force of nature that nothing can escape.

## YOU DO 2 (no scaffold, about 10 minutes)

Hide the scaffold. Students choose **one** of these lines and write the paragraph from scratch:
- "Close to the sun in lonely lands"
- "Ring'd with the azure world, he stands"

Before they submit, students tick each success criterion in their own words.

**Model answer (first option):**
> Tennyson presents the eagle as powerful by placing him in a high, remote setting. The phrase "close to the sun" suggests the eagle lives at a height that no other creature can reach, almost like a god. The word "lonely" also suggests he is separate from everything else, which could show that powerful beings stand alone. The alliteration of "lonely lands" gives the line a calm, drawn-out sound, as if he rules a huge, silent kingdom.
