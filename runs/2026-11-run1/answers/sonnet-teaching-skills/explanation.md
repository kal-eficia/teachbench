# Why we have seasons: an explanation for Year 7

**Assumptions:** Year 7 (ages 11 to 12) know that the Earth orbits the Sun once a year and spins once a day, and that the Sun gives us light and heat. You have a torch and a sheet of A4 (or a mini whiteboard), and ideally a globe or a ball. A phone torch works. The explanation takes about three minutes spoken, plus the checks.

**The misconception to beat:** "Summer is when the Earth is closer to the Sun." It sounds sensible, which is why it's stubborn. Don't just say it's wrong. Give them something that *can't* be explained by it, and then something that can.

---

## Teacher script

### 1. Hook: start from what they know (30 seconds)

"Put your hand up if you've been somewhere hot at Christmas. Some of you have family in Australia, or you've seen Christmas on a beach on TV. In Australia, December is summer. In Britain, December is winter. Same planet, same Sun, same day. Hold that thought."

**Check 1 (hands, then talk to your partner, 20 seconds):** "If the Earth being closer to the Sun caused summer, what would happen in Australia and Britain at the same time?" (Answer: they would both have summer at once. They don't, so closeness can't be the cause.)

### 2. A surprising fact: the "closer" idea fails (30 seconds)

"Here's a fact that surprises most adults. The Earth is actually *closest* to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the British winter. It's about 147 million kilometres away then, and about 152 million in July. So we are further away in our summer. The distance changes by only a few per cent, and that is far too small to make summer hot and winter cold."

**Check 2:** "In January, is the Earth closer to or further from the Sun than in July? Does that match the 'closer in summer' idea?" (Answer: closer in January, so no.)

### 3. Concrete example first: the torch (60 seconds)

Hold the torch about 20 cm above a sheet of paper, pointing straight down. Show the small bright circle.

"The torch is the Sun. Straight on, the light lands in a small, bright, strong patch. Now watch what happens when I tilt the paper but keep the torch exactly the same distance away." Tilt the paper. The patch stretches into a long, dim oval.

"Same torch, same distance, same amount of light. But now the light is spread over a bigger area, so each bit of paper gets less. That means it's less hot. The *angle* matters, not just the distance."

**Check 3:** "Which patch is hotter, the small one or the stretched one? Why?" (Small one, because the same light is squashed into a smaller area.)

### 4. Then the abstraction: the tilt (45 seconds)

"The Earth does the same thing. It is tilted, by about 23 and a half degrees, like a spinning top leaning over. And here's the key part: as the Earth travels around the Sun, the tilt always points the same way, towards the same spot in space. So for half the year, the northern half of the Earth leans *towards* the Sun. For the other half, it leans *away*."

Hold up a globe or a ball and walk it round a desk (or a torch on the table as the Sun), keeping the axis pointing at the same corner of the room. Show the north leaning towards the torch at one side, away at the opposite side.

"When the north leans towards the Sun, the Sun's rays hit us more directly, like the torch pointing straight down. The light is concentrated and the days are longer, so there is more time to warm up. That's summer in Britain. At the same time the southern half leans away, gets slanted, spread-out light and shorter days. That's winter in Australia. Six months later, it swaps."

**Key vocabulary (write on the board):** *axis* (the imaginary line the Earth spins around), *tilt* (23.5 degrees), *orbit*, *direct / slanted rays*, *hemisphere*.

**Check 4:** "In June, the northern hemisphere is tilted towards or away from the Sun? What season is it in the southern hemisphere?" (Towards; winter.)

### 5. A second, different example (20 seconds)

"You've seen this already in a different way. The Sun is low in the sky in a British winter, so shadows are long. In summer, the Sun is high overhead, so shadows are short. Low Sun means slanted light, which means weaker heating. High Sun means direct light, which means strong heating."

### 6. A non-example (20 seconds)

"What if the Earth wasn't tilted at all, but just moved closer and further away? Then both halves of the Earth would have summer and winter at the same time. And there would be no different day lengths. We don't see that, so tilt is the real cause."

### 7. Final check (question that only someone who understood can answer)

**"A friend says: 'It's hot in summer because we're nearer the Sun.' Using what you know, tell your friend why that's wrong and what really happens."**

- **Full-marks answer:** "The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January, which is our winter. Summer happens because the Earth is tilted, so in summer our half leans towards the Sun. The light hits us more directly and the days are longer, so we get more heat."
- **Most likely wrong answer:** "The tilt makes us closer to the Sun in summer." (Still a distance idea, just reworded.) Ask: "Is the Earth closer or further in July? How do we know?" and go back to Check 2.
- **Second likely wrong answer:** "The tilt changes as the Earth moves around the Sun." (No, the axis points the same way throughout; it's the Earth's *position* that changes.) Redo the table walk with a pencil stuck through the ball.

## The analogy and where it breaks

**Analogy:** The torch on a tilted sheet of paper is the Sun's rays hitting a tilted part of the Earth.

**Where it breaks (say this out loud so it doesn't plant a new misconception):**
- The paper is flat, but the Earth is a sphere, so the angle changes smoothly from the equator to the poles.
- When you tilt the paper, you change the *paper*. In real life, the Earth's tilt stays fixed; it's the Earth's *position* in its orbit that makes our half lean towards or away from the Sun.
- A real torch only lights one patch. The Sun lights half the Earth at once.

---

## Misconceptions to expect

| What they say | Why it feels right | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| "Summer = closer to the Sun" | Closer to a fire means hotter | Australia and Britain have opposite seasons at the same time; and we are closest in January |
| "The tilt moves to point at the Sun in summer" | They picture the Earth rocking | The axis keeps pointing at the same place in space; the Earth's position changes |
| "The Earth tilts a lot" | Globes look very tipped | 23.5 degrees is small; try it with a pencil |
| "The seasons are caused by the Moon" | The Moon is the other thing in the sky | The Moon orbits the Earth, and has nothing to do with the seasons |

## A paragraph pupils can read later

> We have seasons because the Earth is tilted. The Earth's axis, the imaginary line it spins around, is tilted by about 23.5 degrees and always points in the same direction in space as the Earth orbits the Sun. For half the year the northern half of the Earth leans towards the Sun. The Sun's rays hit it more directly and the days are longer, so it is summer there. At the same time the southern half leans away, so it gets weaker, more spread-out light and shorter days, and it is winter. Six months later, it swaps. The distance to the Sun is not the cause: the Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January, in the middle of the British winter.
