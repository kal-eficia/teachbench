# Coastal spit: a board diagram built in six stages

**Assumptions:** Year 8 have met waves and erosion but not longshore drift. You have a whiteboard and at least two colours (the plan below also works in one colour, because every element is distinguished by line style and a written label, never colour alone). The example is Spurn Head in East Yorkshire. Swap in a spit you know better if you like (Hurst Spit, Orford Ness).

Draw it as a **plan view** (a bird's eye view of the coast). Keep it on the left two thirds of the board so your words go on the right.

---

## The finished diagram (what the board looks like at the end)

```
                      LAND
   ________________________________________
  |  cliff / beach (coast runs left to right)|__
  |                                           \_____
  |                                                 \ <- coast turns away
  |                                                  |    (bend)   RIVER
  |  - - -> - - -> - - -> - - ->  - - - - - - ->  ~~~~~~~~~~  mouth
  |        longshore drift (zigzag)                         \
  |     ====================================================)  <- SPIT
  |                                               ,-----------'  recurved end
  |                                              (   calm water: SALT MARSH
   \__ SEA ___                                    `---------.
                       WIND ->  waves arrive at an angle
```

Labels to be on the picture itself: **prevailing wind**, **swash**, **backwash**, **longshore drift**, **bend in coastline**, **spit**, **recurved end**, **river mouth**, **sheltered water / salt marsh**, **sea**, **land**.

Do not draw this finished version first. Build it live, in the stages below.

---

## Build order and what to say

### Stage 1: The coast and the bend (2 minutes)

**Draw:** A long straight coastline running left to right, land above, sea below. At the right-hand end, bend the coastline sharply upwards and inland, like the corner of a room. Write **LAND** and **SEA**. Add a small river mouth at the bend (a gap with a wavy line).

**Say:** "This is a coastline from above. Land at the top, sea at the bottom. See how it runs straight for a long way, then suddenly turns inland here? That corner might be a river mouth or a bay. Remember this corner, because the whole story depends on it. By the end of the lesson I want you to be able to tell me why a long finger of sand ends up sticking out *across* that gap."

**Check:** "In one sentence, what is special about this stretch of coast?" (Answer: it changes direction.)

### Stage 2: The wind and the waves (3 minutes)

**Draw:** Along the bottom left, a large arrow labelled **prevailing wind**. From it, draw 3 wave lines (short curved lines) heading towards the beach at an **angle**, not straight on. Make the angle obvious, about 45 degrees.

**Say:** "In Britain the wind most often comes from the south west. We call that the prevailing wind. Waves follow the wind, so waves hit this beach at a slant, not head on. That slant is the secret of the whole topic. Everything we draw next comes from it."

**Check (mini whiteboards):** "Draw me which way a wave would travel if the wind blew straight at the beach. Now one if the wind blows at a slant." (Everyone shows boards. You are looking for one straight, one angled.)

### Stage 3: Swash and backwash (4 minutes)

**Draw:** Take one wave and zoom in. At the beach, draw a **solid arrow** running up the beach at the same slant as the wave. Label it **swash**. From where it stops, draw a **dashed arrow** running straight back down the beach at 90 degrees to the coastline. Label it **backwash**. Add a small dot on the swash arrow and write **pebble**.

**Say:** "A wave runs up the beach. This is the swash, and it goes up at the same slant as the wave, carrying sand and pebbles with it. Now the water has to come back down. What pulls it? Gravity. And gravity pulls straight down the slope, not at a slant. So the backwash drops the pebble straight down the beach, not back to where it started. The pebble has moved a little bit along the beach. One wave, one tiny step sideways."

**Check:** "Does the backwash go back along the same path as the swash? Why not?" (Answer: no, gravity pulls straight down the slope.)

### Stage 4: Longshore drift (4 minutes)

**Draw:** Repeat the pebble many times, joining the swash and backwash arrows into a **zigzag** along the beach, moving left to right. Add one big arrow beside the zigzag labelled **longshore drift**. Write underneath: *"Swash up at an angle, backwash straight down."*

**Say:** "Now do it again, and again, thousands of times a day. Up at an angle, down straight. Up at an angle, down straight. The pebble zigzags along the beach in the direction the wind is pushing it. Zigzag by zigzag, tonnes of sand and shingle travel along the coast. This movement has a name: longshore drift. Say it with me. Longshore: along the shore. Drift: gradual movement."

**Check:** "Which direction will this pebble be going in an hour? Where will it be when it reaches the corner?" (Pupils point or answer chorally: along the coast, towards the bend.)

### Stage 5: Deposition and the spit begins (5 minutes)

**Draw:** Extend the zigzag to the bend. Where the coast turns away, draw the line of sediment **carrying straight on** out into the water, as a thin sandbar going across the gap, a short finger. Label **deposition**. Shade the water behind it lightly and write **sheltered, calm water**.

**Say:** "The pebbles reach the corner. The coast turns away, but the waves and the wind keep pushing the sediment straight on. Sand and shingle are now moving into deeper water that has less energy. The waves can no longer carry the load. What do we call dropping material? Deposition. The sediment is dropped, and over many years a ridge builds out from the end of the land, into the sea, in the same direction as the drift. That ridge is a spit. Notice what keeps feeding it: the zigzag is still delivering new sediment behind. As long as the supply continues, the spit gets longer."

**Check (cold call, no hands up):** "Name the process that builds the spit. Name the process that supplies the sediment." (Deposition; longshore drift.) Ask a second pupil to say it again in their own words.

### Stage 6: The recurved end, salt marsh and why it stops (4 minutes)

**Draw:**
1. At the tip of the spit, add a **hook** curling back towards the land. Label **recurved end**. Draw a second, shorter wind arrow from a different direction pointing at the tip.
2. In the calm water behind the spit, draw tufts or little reeds. Label **salt marsh**.
3. Across the river mouth, draw current arrows pushing outwards. Label **river current stops the spit joining the other side**.

**Say:** "Two last things. First, the tip hooks. Why? Winds and waves sometimes change direction, and that bends the end of the spit round, like a hooked finger. This is called a recurved end. Second, look behind the spit. The water here is sheltered, so very fine mud settles and plants colonise. Over time this becomes salt marsh, a habitat full of wildlife. Finally, why doesn't the spit grow all the way across the gap? The river keeps pushing water out, which stops sediment settling across the mouth. So the spit stays attached to the land at one end only."

**Check:** "Give me one thing that makes a spit longer and one thing that stops it growing." (Longer: supply of sediment from longshore drift. Stops: river current, or the coast no longer changing direction.)

---

## What to say as you finish (30 seconds)

"Wind blows at a slant. Swash goes up at an angle, backwash comes straight down. That makes longshore drift. At the bend, the sediment is dropped. Deposition builds the spit. The end hooks when the wind changes. Calm water behind makes salt marsh. If I give you those six steps, can you re-tell the story to your partner without looking at the board?"

---

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- **"The sea picks up the sand and throws it somewhere."** Stress that it is lots of tiny zigzags, not one big movement.
- **"The backwash goes back the way it came."** Redraw the dashed arrow at 90 degrees and ask why.
- **"A spit is made by erosion."** It is made by *deposition*. Erosion (maybe elsewhere along the coast) only supplies the material.

---

## Blank version for pupils to complete from memory

Give each pupil a sheet with the coastline, the bend and the river mouth already drawn, and **numbered empty label boxes**. Under the diagram, add this word bank (in alphabetical order, not the order they'll be used):

*backwash, deposition, longshore drift, prevailing wind, recurved end, salt marsh, sheltered water, spit, swash*

Task, in two parts:
1. **Draw** the arrows for the wind, swash, backwash and longshore drift, and label the spit and its recurved end.
2. **Explain** in three sentences using at least four words from the bank: *How does a spit form?*

**Model answer (for you):** "The prevailing wind makes waves hit the beach at an angle, so the swash carries sediment up the beach at a slant while the backwash drops it straight down. This zigzag movement, longshore drift, moves sediment along the coast. Where the coastline bends away, the energy falls and the sediment is deposited, building a spit out into the sea. Winds from another direction can curve the end into a recurved tip, and salt marsh forms in the calm water behind."

**Retrieval for next lesson:** give the blank version again, cold, with no word bank.
