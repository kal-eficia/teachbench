# Supporting a Year 4 boy with ADHD: low-effort things that work

**Assumptions:** he's in a typical mainstream class, you have no extra adult for him, and you want a handful of habits rather than a new system. I've worked from what you described (slow to start, calling out, up a lot). Check anything specific in his support plan or with your SENCO, and remember what works for one child with ADHD may not for another, so treat these as things to try and keep the ones that help.

## The principle
Most of what you describe is difficulty with starting, waiting and holding a task in mind, not defiance. So the fix is mostly to change the task and the routine, not to repeat the instruction. And most of these help the whole class.

## 1. Starting tasks (the biggest win)

- **Make the first step tiny and visible.** "Write today's date and the first word." He's started, and starting is the barrier. Write a "First step" on the board for every task.
- **Chunk the work.** Give three or four small steps on a mini checklist at his desk (or on a sticky note), not one long task. He ticks each one. Fewer, important questions beat lots of questions. Ten minutes of focused work is better than a page he abandons.
- **Say it, show it, then check it.** Give the instruction once, to the whole class. Then pass his desk and quietly ask "What are you doing first?" Don't ask "Did you hear me?"
- **Start him off, then leave.** Sit by him for 30 seconds on the first line, then say "I'll be back in three minutes to see the second line." That gives a clear end to your absence.
- **Visual timer** (sand timer or a screen timer) for short bursts: "Beat the timer for 8 minutes." Avoid making it feel like a countdown threat; frame it as a challenge.

## 2. Getting up a lot

Treat movement as a need to plan for, not a behaviour to stop.

- **Give him legitimate reasons to move.** A job once or twice a lesson: hand out books, wipe the board, take the register to the office, collect the pencils. Schedule it at the points you've seen him get restless.
- **Allow standing or a movement option.** A standing spot at the back of the table, a wobble cushion or a resistance band round the chair legs, if the school has them. Fidget tools only if they don't distract others.
- **Build movement into the lesson.** Stand up to vote, hold up mini-whiteboards, "stand if you think the answer is...". Everyone benefits.
- **Agree a signal** (a card on his desk, "movement break") he can use once or twice in a lesson to walk to a set spot and back. Fewer random trips happen when the need is met.

## 3. Calling out

- **Teach and practise one alternative.** Agree privately: when he has an idea, he writes a word on a sticky note or his mini whiteboard, or puts a hand on his head, and you'll come to it. Then actually come to it, quickly and often at first.
- **Reduce the wait.** Use whole-class response (mini whiteboards, "talk to your partner for 20 seconds") so everyone answers at once and he isn't holding an answer in.
- **Catch him doing it right, specifically.** "You put your hand up and waited. Thank you." Aim for several of these a day, quietly, not in front of the class. Praise works better than reminders with children with ADHD.
- **Don't take calling out personally or publicly.** A private, calm reminder works better than naming him in front of the class.

## 4. Routines that take no extra time
- **Same structure every lesson**, on the board: Start, Learn, Task, Finish. A tick or a rub-out as you go.
- **Seat him** near you and the board, away from the door and busy areas, next to a calm, steady partner.
- **Warn before transitions**: "Two minutes, then we tidy up."
- **Short, specific instructions**, one at a time, with the key word first: "Pencil down. Eyes this way."
- **A quick daily check-in** (30 seconds at the door): "Today's goal is to start the first task quickly. Show me at the end." Then a quick thumbs-up at the end. Link it to a small reward, such as a sticker, a point towards a class reward, or a message to a parent.

## 5. Keep the effort small
Pick two things this week: a **first-step prompt** and a **movement job**. Do those for a fortnight. Then add one more. Keep a note of what happens: a quick tally of how long he takes to start, and how many times he gets up. If you can show that something helps, you'll also have evidence to share with the SENCO and parents.

## 6. Talk to him and his parents
- Ask him, privately and kindly, what helps and what makes it hard. Children with ADHD often know.
- A short, positive message to home ("He started quickly three times today") builds the relationship and usually gets useful information back about what works at home.
- If the strategies aren't enough, ask your SENCO about a support plan, extra adult time at the hardest parts of the day, or advice from outside agencies.

## What to avoid
- Long verbal instructions with several steps.
- Taking away break or movement time as a consequence. It makes the next lesson harder.
- Public reprimands.
- Treating each reminder as a failure. Reset and go again.
