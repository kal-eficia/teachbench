# Year 10 session: how to revise so it actually sticks (50 minutes)

**Assumptions:** a whole class of about 25 to 30, any subject, in a normal classroom with a board and paper. Students bring their own notes or a textbook page from a recent topic (ask them to bring any set of notes; have a spare page for those who forget). No special equipment. Phones are allowed out only when the plan says so.

**By the end, students can:**
1. Say why highlighting and rereading feel good but work badly.
2. Use retrieval practice on their own notes, and check their answers.
3. Plan one week of spaced revision with phones handled.

**Core message, repeated three times:** *Close the notes and try to remember. That is the revision.*

## Overview

| Min | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Hook: prove it to yourself | Quick memory test |
| 5-12 | Explain the why | Short teacher input, no more than 5 minutes |
| 12-27 | Practise: retrieval on own notes | Brain dump and question-making |
| 27-37 | Practise: spacing and mixing, plan a week | Planner |
| 37-45 | Phones | Where the phone goes while you revise |
| 45-50 | Commit and exit | One action, one question |

## 0-5: Hook (phones away, on desks face down for now)

Put 12 unrelated words on the board for 30 seconds (e.g. river, candle, bicycle, seven, Tuesday, helmet, orange, ladder, silence, button, ocean, wire). Cover them. Students write down as many as they can in 60 seconds. Then compare with a partner.

Ask: *How did that feel? Did you check the board again and again, or did you try to pull the words out of your head?* Take two or three answers.

Tell them: today is about the difference between **feeling** like you know something and **actually** knowing it.

## 5-12: The why (teacher input, 5 to 7 minutes, short and punchy)

Show three things only.

1. **Rereading and highlighting feel easy because the information is in front of you.** That ease is called fluency. It tricks you into thinking you know it. In the exam, the notes are gone.
2. **Pulling it out of your head is what builds memory.** Struggling to remember is not failing; it is the part that works.
3. **Forgetting is normal, and spacing fights it.** If you learn something and don't come back to it, most of it fades within days. Coming back at gaps, just before you forget, makes it last.

Quick poll (hands up, no names): *Who mostly highlights or rereads?* Count. Then tell them you are not telling them off; it's what most people do, and it is exactly why this session is useful.

Add one honest line: studies that compare methods find testing yourself beats rereading, even when students feel the opposite.

## 12-27: Try it on your own notes (15 minutes)

**Step 1: Brain dump (4 min).** Choose one topic from your notes. Close the notes. On blank paper, write everything you remember, in any order. No peeking. Stuck is fine.

**Step 2: Check (3 min).** Open the notes. With a different colour pen, add what you missed. Circle the biggest gaps. These are the topics to come back to.

**Step 3: Make questions (4 min).** Turn the three biggest gaps into questions on the front of an index card or a piece of paper, with the answer on the back. Example: not "Photosynthesis" but "What are the reactants and products of photosynthesis, and where does it happen?"

**Step 4: Swap and test (4 min).** Swap questions with a partner. Ask each other. Answers wrong? Mark them with a cross and keep the card in the "again" pile.

Circulate. Note one or two good questions to share.

## 27-37: Spacing and mixing, plan the week (10 minutes)

Show a simple pattern on the board:
- Day 1: learn it or fix it
- Day 2 (next day): test yourself
- Day 4 or 5: test yourself again
- A week later: test yourself again, mixed with another topic

**Task:** On a one-week grid (Mon to Sun), each student writes in **three** 20-minute slots in the next seven days. For each, they write the subject, the topic, and how: "Brain dump", "Question cards", "Past-paper question". Rules:
- No slot is "read through notes".
- At least one slot mixes two subjects or topics.
- Slots go where they actually have time (after prayer, after clubs, before a game, whenever suits them), not where they wish they had time.

Pairs check each other's plan with one question: *Would you really do it?*

## 37-45: Phones (8 minutes)

Do not lecture about phones. Run it as an experiment.

1. Ask: *Who thinks they can read a page properly with their phone buzzing next to them?* Hands up.
2. Tell the class a fact: research on this finds that even a silent phone on the desk, face down, uses up some of your attention. (Say "research suggests", not a percentage.)
3. **Agree one rule for the next revision slot:** phone out of the room, or at the other end of the house, on do not disturb. Not face down on the desk. Out.
4. **Put the phone to good use:** use it as a timer (25 minutes work, 5 minutes break) set before they put it away, or a voice recorder to say answers aloud, or a camera to photograph the brain dump. If using a revision app, use it for quizzing, not browsing. When they use AI, ask it to quiz them, not to give them the answers.

Ask for one honest barrier ("When I revise, my phone is...") and have two or three students share what they would try.

## 45-50: Commit and exit

- **One action:** each student writes on their plan one sentence: "Tonight I will ... for 20 minutes, phone in ..." and puts their initials on it.
- **Exit question (on paper, handed in or shown):** *Close your notes. Write two things you remember from today's session.* That is retrieval, and you can check it.
- Last line: **"Close the book and try. That is the revision."**

## Resources to prepare
- 12 words for the hook (above), a timer
- Blank paper, index cards or slips, a second colour of pen
- A one-week planner grid (printed, one per student)
- Optional: one-page summary handout of the three ideas

## Teacher notes
- **Likely objections:** "I don't have time to make cards" (use the brain dump instead; it needs no cards); "I just forget everything" (that is normal; each retrieval makes it slower); "Highlighting works for me" (try the brain dump on the same topic tomorrow and compare).
- **Follow-up:** next lesson, open with a five-minute brain dump of the previous topic, then ask who did their slots. After two weeks, re-run the planner for a new week.
- **Differentiation:** students who find writing hard can do the brain dump as a voice note or draw a mind map from memory. Stronger students can write questions that need explanation ("why", "compare"), not just recall.
