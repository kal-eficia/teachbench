# Adapted task: the Great Plague, two sources

**Assumptions:** the source texts weren't attached, so the adapted sheet below has slots for them. I've assumed the student is reading at or near age level but decodes slowly and tires on dense text, and that typed answers or voice recording are allowed. The thinking is unchanged: compare what two sources suggest about how Londoners reacted, then judge which is more useful for an enquiry into the plague's impact, using own knowledge. Nothing is simplified in the history. The route is what changes.

---

## Student sheet (print on cream or pale blue paper, sans-serif such as Arial or Verdana, 13 to 14 pt, 1.5 line spacing, left-aligned, not justified, lots of white space)

# The Great Plague of 1665: how did Londoners react?

**Your job:** read two sources, compare them, and decide which is more useful.

**Time:** about 35 minutes. Tick each step as you finish it.

- [ ] Step 1: Get ready
- [ ] Step 2: Read Source A
- [ ] Step 3: Read Source B
- [ ] Step 4: Compare
- [ ] Step 5: Decide which is more useful
- [ ] Step 6: Check

### Key words (look back here any time)

| Word | Means |
|---|---|
| **source** | something from the past or about the past that gives us evidence |
| **reaction** | what people did or felt when something happened |
| **useful** | helps us answer a question. It does not mean "true" or "easy to read" |
| **provenance** | who wrote it, when, and why |
| **enquiry** | a big question we are trying to answer. Ours is: *What was the impact of the plague?* |

### Step 1: Get ready
1. Read the enquiry question out loud or in your head: *What was the impact of the plague on London?*
2. Colour highlighter ready: **pink** for fear, **green** for action, **blue** for anything else interesting.

### Step 2: Read Source A (a diary from 1665)

*Reading help: your teacher or the audio file can read this to you. Listening counts as reading.*

> **[Teacher: paste Source A here in chunks of 2 to 3 lines, one chunk per paragraph, with a line of space between. Bold any tricky word and put its meaning in the box underneath. Keep the original wording; put the meaning of old words in brackets rather than rewriting the source.]**

**Word help for Source A:** [teacher adds 3 to 5 words, e.g. old spellings or terms like "shut up", "pest-house", "bill of mortality"]

**Quick questions (answer in a few words, typed or spoken):**
1. Who wrote this and when?
2. Highlight in **pink** any words showing fear.
3. Highlight in **green** any words showing what people did.

### Step 3: Read Source B (a modern historian)

> **[Teacher: paste Source B here, same layout as Source A. Break into short paragraphs. Bold the key claim in each paragraph.]**

**Word help for Source B:** [3 to 5 words]

**Quick questions:**
1. Who wrote this and when?
2. Highlight in **pink** words showing fear, in **green** words showing action.
3. Is this written at the time, or later by someone studying it?

### Step 4: Compare

Fill in the table. Short notes are fine. Use words, bullet points or a voice note.

| | Source A (diary, 1665) | Source B (historian) |
|---|---|---|
| What does it say Londoners **did**? | | |
| What does it say they **felt**? | | |
| One thing that is **the same** in both | | |
| One thing that is **different** | | |

**Sentence help (use these if you want to):**
- "Source A suggests that Londoners ... because it says ..."
- "Source B suggests that ..., which is similar to / different from Source A because ..."
- "The main difference is that ..."

### Step 5: Which is more useful?

Think about the enquiry question: *What was the impact of the plague?* Use the table. Two minutes thinking time before you write.

| Think about | Source A | Source B |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** wrote it? Were they there? | | |
| **When**? Close to the events or later? | | |
| **Why** might they have written it? | | |
| What does it **tell us**? | | |
| What does it **leave out**? | | |

Now use **your own knowledge**. Add one or two facts you know about 1665 that help you judge each source. For example, think about what you know about: the numbers who died, people leaving London, what happened to houses with plague, the Fire of 1666, or the king and the wealthy leaving.

**Build your answer in three boxes** (about 2 sentences each):

| Box | Starter |
|---|---|
| **1. My choice** | "I think Source ___ is more useful because ..." |
| **2. Evidence and my own knowledge** | "For example, it says ... This fits with what I know, which is that ..." |
| **3. A limit** | "However, Source ___ is not perfect because it only shows ... / it was written ... / it does not tell us about ..." |

**Challenge (optional):** Say what source you would want next to give a fuller picture, and why.

### Step 6: Check
- [ ] I used the words "suggests" or "shows"
- [ ] I mentioned **who** wrote each source or **when**
- [ ] I used at least one fact of my own
- [ ] I gave one limit
- [ ] I read my answer aloud quietly or used read-aloud on the device

## Teacher note: what changed and why

| Change | Why | Keeps the thinking? |
|---|---|---|
| Steps numbered, one job each, with tick boxes | Cuts the load of holding a multi-part instruction in working memory | Yes, same sequence: read, compare, judge |
| Sources chunked, original wording kept, hard words glossed in a box | Reduces decoding load without simplifying the evidence. Rewriting a source would change what it can tell us | Yes, the student still reads the real source |
| Audio or reader support offered for both sources | Listening gives access to the content; the history thinking is the assessed part | Yes |
| Table for comparing, and for utility (who, when, why, tells us, leaves out) | Gives structure for the thinking, so the student spends effort on judgement rather than on organising the page | Yes. It scaffolds the utility criteria rather than answering them |
| Sentence starters, three-box answer | Reduces the blank-page problem for extended writing | Yes. The student still supplies the claims and evidence |
| Voice recording or typing allowed | Removes handwriting and spelling as a barrier to showing historical understanding | Yes |
| Spelling not marked | Mark the history, not the spelling | Yes |

**When to fade:** next time, remove the sentence starters but keep the three boxes. After two tasks, remove the boxes and keep the checklist. Keep audio and the glossary for as long as the student wants them. Gradually move from glossed sources to word help for only the hardest words.

**Marking:** judge the comparison and the use of provenance and own knowledge, exactly as you would for any other student. Give feedback on the one thing to improve first, and keep it short and positive.
