# How the immune system responds to a pathogen (Year 9)

**Assumptions:** Year 9 (age 13 to 14), moving towards GCSE Biology. Students already know that bacteria and viruses are pathogens that make us ill, and that blood contains white blood cells. One lesson slot of about 10 minutes for the explanation, then checks. Wording follows what a GCSE mark scheme credits.

---

## 1. Teacher script (about 3 minutes spoken)

Each step ends with a check question. Ask it before moving on, using mini-whiteboards or cold-calling. Answers are in section 3.

**Step 1: Start with something they have lived through (concrete first)**

"Think about chickenpox, or any illness you've had once and never again. Imagine Amira catches chickenpox. For two or three days the virus multiplies inside her and she feels rotten. After about a week she's better. Next year she's around someone with chickenpox and she doesn't get ill at all. Something in her body changed. That something is what we're explaining today."

"Every pathogen has molecules on its surface called **antigens**. Your white blood cells check every cell they meet. If the antigens aren't yours, they know it's an invader."

> **Check 1:** What do white blood cells recognise on a pathogen?

**Step 2: White blood cells have three jobs**

"White blood cells fight in three ways. One: some **engulf** the pathogen and digest it. That's called phagocytosis. Two: some make **antibodies**. Three: some make **antitoxins**."

> **Check 2:** Name the three ways white blood cells defend the body.

**Step 3: Antibodies**

"An antibody is a protein with a shape that fits one antigen, and only that one. Think of a key that fits a single lock. The antibody sticks to the pathogen's antigens and marks it so white blood cells can destroy it. The first time you meet a pathogen, your body has to work out the right shape and build antibodies from scratch. That takes a few days, and that's the time you feel ill."

> **Check 3:** Amira has made antibodies against chickenpox. Why can't those same antibodies fight the flu virus?

**Step 4: Antitoxins**

"Some bacteria make **toxins**, which are poisons. A lot of the damage in some diseases comes from the toxin, not the bacterium. White blood cells make **antitoxins** that neutralise the toxins. So antibodies go after the pathogen, and antitoxins go after the poison."

> **Check 4:** A bacterium releases a poison into the blood. Which would neutralise the poison, antibodies or antitoxins?

**Step 5: Memory cells**

"After Amira recovers, some of the white blood cells stay in her blood as **memory cells**. They remember the shape of the chickenpox antigens. Next time, they recognise the pathogen straight away and make the right antibodies much faster and in much larger numbers. The virus is destroyed before she feels ill. That's **immunity**."

> **Check 5:** Why does Amira usually not get chickenpox a second time?

**Step 6: The thing everyone gets wrong**

"Now, antibiotics. Are they part of the immune system? No. They're medicines. Antibiotics kill **bacteria**. They don't work on viruses. A virus lives and copies itself inside your own cells, and antibiotics attack things bacteria have that viruses don't. So if you have a cold or flu, which are viruses, antibiotics do nothing."

> **Check 6:** Mo has flu. His friend says, "Just ask the doctor for antibiotics." Is she right? Explain.

## 2. The analogy, and where it breaks

**Analogy: a security firm with a file of wanted posters.**
- White blood cells are the guards.
- An antigen is the burglar's face on a wanted poster.
- The first time a new burglar turns up, the firm has to work out who they are and make a new poster. That takes days.
- Memory cells are the poster kept on file. Next time the burglar appears, the guards recognise them at once and act in minutes.

**Where it breaks (say this out loud):**
1. Antibodies aren't guards. They're **proteins** that stick to the antigen. They can't hunt anything. White blood cells do the destroying.
2. Memory cells are living cells that divide quickly when needed. They aren't a photograph in a drawer.
3. Nobody is "in charge". The body does this automatically, with no decision-maker.
4. A burglar has one face. A pathogen like flu changes its antigens over time, so memory cells for last year's flu may not recognise this year's. This is why you can catch flu or colds many times.

## 3. Check questions: answers and likely wrong answers

| # | Answer | Most likely wrong answer, and why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Antigens** (molecules on the surface of the pathogen) | "The pathogen's DNA" or "its toxins". They are confusing what's inside or released with what's on the surface. |
| 2 | Engulf and digest the pathogen (phagocytosis); make antibodies; make antitoxins | Only naming "they eat the germs". Many students think white blood cells only do the eating. |
| 3 | Each antibody is specific: its shape only fits the antigens of one pathogen. Flu antigens have a different shape, so it doesn't fit. | "Because flu is a virus and chickenpox is bacteria." Both are viruses, and that's not the reason. Keep returning to shape. |
| 4 | **Antitoxins** | "Antibodies". They're treating them as one thing. Say: antibodies attack the pathogen, antitoxins attack the poison. |
| 5 | Memory cells stayed in her blood. They recognise the antigens and make the right antibodies faster and in greater numbers. | "The antibodies stay in her blood forever." Antibodies fade; the memory cells are what last. |
| 6 | No. Flu is caused by a virus, and antibiotics only kill bacteria. | "Yes, antibiotics kill germs." "Germs" blurs bacteria and viruses. Insist on the word *bacteria*. |

**Exit question that only someone who understood can answer:**
"A new virus appears that nobody has ever caught. Explain why the first infection makes someone ill for several days, but a second infection with the same virus usually doesn't."
*Model answer:* The first time, the body has to make antibodies with the right shape for the virus's antigens, which takes days, so the virus multiplies and makes them ill. The second time, memory cells recognise the antigens, so the right antibodies are made quickly and in large numbers, and the virus is destroyed before it makes them ill.

## 4. Antibiotics: how to tackle the misconception head-on

Students believe it because medicine is often given when you're ill, and "antibiotic" sounds like "anti-everything". So do three things.

**1. Name the misconception before they do.** "A lot of people think antibiotics cure colds. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain why they don't."

**2. Sort task (3 minutes).** Give pairs these cards and ask them to sort into "Antibiotics will help" and "Antibiotics won't help": *a sore throat caused by streptococcus bacteria; the common cold; flu; a bacterial ear infection; chickenpox; a bacterial skin infection; COVID-19; tuberculosis.* Don't tell them which are viruses and bacteria until after they've sorted. Reveal, and discuss the ones they got wrong.

**3. Give them a wording trick.**
- **anti-BIO-tic**: *bio* means life. It attacks living bacterial cells.
- **anti-BODY**: made by your **body**.
- **anti-TOXIN**: against **toxins**.

**Why antibiotics don't work on viruses (the reason to give):** a virus isn't a cell. It's genetic material in a protein coat that gets inside your own cells and uses them to make copies. Antibiotics target features that bacteria have, and the virus doesn't have them. Killing the virus inside your cells would also harm your cells.

**What does help with viruses:** rest, fluids and painkillers to manage symptoms. Vaccines to prevent infection in the first place. A small number of antiviral drugs for specific infections.

**Why it matters:** using antibiotics when they aren't needed helps bacteria evolve **resistance**, so they stop working when we really need them. (This leads into the next lesson on antibiotic resistance.)

**Comparison table for the board or their books:**

| | Made by | Works against | Part of immune system? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antibodies | White blood cells | A specific pathogen | Yes |
| Antitoxins | White blood cells | Toxins (poisons) made by pathogens | Yes |
| Antibiotics | Drug companies (medicine) | Bacteria only | No |

## 5. One-paragraph version students can read later

When a pathogen enters your body, white blood cells spot it because its surface molecules, called antigens, are not yours. White blood cells then defend you in three ways. They engulf and digest the pathogen. They make antibodies, which have a shape that fits only that pathogen's antigens and stick to it so it can be destroyed. And they make antitoxins, which neutralise poisons (toxins) released by the pathogen. The first time you meet a pathogen it takes a few days to make the right antibodies, so you feel ill. Afterwards, memory cells stay in your blood. If the same pathogen returns, they make antibodies much faster and in greater numbers, so it is destroyed before it can make you ill. This is immunity. Antibiotics are different. They are medicines that kill bacteria, and they do not work on viruses such as colds and flu.
