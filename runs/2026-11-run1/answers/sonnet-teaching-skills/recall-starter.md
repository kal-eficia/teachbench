# Year 8 science starter: acids and alkalis lesson

## Assumptions

- Lessons are roughly one a day or every other day, so "yesterday" is word equations and the other three lessons were within the last week or two.
- Students work individually, on paper or mini whiteboards, from memory. About 7 minutes.
- Nothing about acids and alkalis appears, because it hasn't been taught. This is retrieval only.
- The two problem areas from last week's quiz (compounds vs mixtures, and melting) each get two questions, because they are being forgotten right now.

## Student starter (7 minutes, no notes, no searching, no AI)

Write your answers on your whiteboard or in your book. For the multiple-choice questions, circle your answer and then write **G** (guessing), **T** (think so) or **C** (certain) next to it.

1. A solid melts. Describe what happens to its particles. Give **two** things.

2. Name **one** part found in a plant cell that is **not** found in an animal cell.

3. Which of these is a **compound**?
   - A. Air
   - B. Salt dissolved in water
   - C. Carbon dioxide
   - D. Iron filings mixed with sulfur

   Confidence: G / T / C

4. Look at this word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide. Which substance is the **product**?

5. What name is given to the elements in Group 0 of the periodic table?

6. Which of these is a **chemical change**?
   - A. Wood burning
   - B. Ice melting
   - C. Sugar dissolving in tea
   - D. Water boiling

   Confidence: G / T / C

7. Ice is heated and melts into water. Which statement about the **particles** is correct?
   - A. They get bigger and spread out
   - B. They stay in fixed positions but vibrate more
   - C. They stop moving and stick together
   - D. They gain energy and are free to move past each other

   Confidence: G / T / C

8. A student says: "Air is a compound because it contains oxygen and nitrogen." Is the student right? Explain your answer.

## Teacher key

| Q | Answer | Topic | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Particles gain energy / vibrate or move more; they break out of the fixed arrangement (they can now move past each other). Accept "move faster" and "no longer in a regular pattern". Not accepted alone: "they melt", "they get smaller". | Particle model (3 weeks ago) | About half the class couldn't say this in last week's quiz. Asked as a short answer first, so no options to guess from. |
| 2 | Cell wall, chloroplasts, or a permanent vacuole (any one) | Cells (a month ago) | Oldest material in the starter, due for a spaced check. |
| 3 | **C**: Carbon dioxide. A and B are mixtures. D is a mixture because the iron and sulfur are not chemically joined. | Elements, compounds and mixtures | Most of the class confused compounds with mixtures. Distractors are the common wrong picks: "air" (several elements, so compound?), "salt in water" (dissolved, so joined?), and "iron and sulfur" (two elements mixed, so compound?). |
| 4 | Magnesium oxide | Word equations (yesterday) | Keeps yesterday's thread. One quick, low-risk question. |
| 5 | Noble gases | Periodic table groups (lesson 2) | Spaced retrieval of lesson 2. Deliberately not Group 1, so tomorrow's alkali content isn't previewed by accident. |
| 6 | **A**: Wood burning. B, C and D are physical changes. | Chemical vs physical change (lesson 3) | Spaced retrieval of lesson 3. Notice who picks "water boiling" or "sugar dissolving"; those students are treating any change as chemical. |
| 7 | **D**. B is the most tempting wrong answer: it is true of solids, not of a melting substance. | Particle model, melting | Second chance on the weakest area, in a different format. Compare with Q1: if a student gets Q7 right but Q1 wrong, they recognise the idea but can't yet explain it. |
| 8 | Not right. Air is a mixture. The oxygen and nitrogen are not chemically joined to each other, and they can be separated. Accept: "it's a mixture because the gases are just mixed together". | Compounds vs mixtures | Second question on the biggest misconception, as a written explanation. Placed last so Q3 and Q8 are not neighbours. |

**Answer positions for the multiple-choice questions:** Q3 C, Q6 A, Q7 D (checked by hand: no repeats, runs or staircases).

## How to use the results

- After 6 minutes, go through the answers quickly and ask for hands on each confidence rating for Q3, Q6 and Q7. Students who are **certain and wrong** are the ones to note down.
- If more than a third of the class miss Q1 or Q8 again, spend 3 extra minutes reteaching before starting acids and alkalis. A quick sketch of particles in a solid and a liquid next to a mixture and a compound is enough.
- Don't move to the new topic without a short bridge sentence, for example: "Yesterday you wrote equations for reactions. Today we meet a new family of substances that react in very specific ways."
