# Whole-class feedback: Priestley's presentation of Mr Birling

You've done the hard part by reading all 28 and noting the patterns. Below is a one-page sheet you can give out as it is, a code system so you write almost nothing on individual essays, and a 15-minute improvement task.

**Assumption:** the question is something like "How does Priestley present Mr Birling in *An Inspector Calls*?", and the class has time in the next lesson for a rewrite task. Check the quotations against your copy of the text before printing.

---

## What to do with the 28 essays (about 10 minutes)

1. Write a **code** on each essay (A to F below). Choose the one or two codes that matter most for that student, no more.
2. Write one **star** (a tick and the word "Strong") next to the best sentence or paragraph in each essay. That is your praise, and students need it.
3. Give out the sheet. Students find their code, do the matching task, and rewrite.

For the stronger students who linked Birling to capitalism, use code **S** (stretch).

## Student sheet (print or display)

### Year 10 feedback: Mr Birling

**What went well**
- Your **knowledge of the plot** is secure. You know what happens and who says what, and that is the foundation of a good essay.
- Many of you used **quotations**. That is exactly right.
- Some of you went further and linked Birling to **capitalism**, showing that Priestley uses him to criticise a whole way of thinking, not just one man. That is a top-level idea.

**What to fix: find your code**

| Code | The problem | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Retelling the story | Stop telling us *what happens*. Tell us *what Priestley wants us to think, and how he does it*. Start sentences with "Priestley presents Birling as...", "This shows...", "The audience sees...". |
| **B** | Quotation with no comment on language | A quotation is evidence, not the answer. Pick out one word or phrase and say what it suggests. Use: **quote, then zoom in on a word, then what it suggests about Birling, then why Priestley wants us to think that**. |
| **C** | No context | The play is set in **1912** but was written and first performed in **1945**. The audience knows what Birling doesn't: the Titanic sank, and two world wars followed. Add one sentence linking Birling to this. |
| **D** | Paragraphs too long | One paragraph means one idea. If it covers two ideas, start a new paragraph. Aim for 6 to 9 lines. |
| **E** | Spelling | See the spelling box below. |
| **S** | Stretch | Your idea about capitalism is strong. Now push it: how does Priestley use Birling's views to make the audience *reject* them? What does the Inspector's opposite view suggest? |

(Code **F**: you did this well, so no action needed. Just check the spelling box.)

**Spelling box**

- **Priestley**: *Priest* + *ley*. Start with "Priest".
- **capitalism**: *capital* + *ism*. If you can spell "capital", you can spell it.
- **responsibility**: it comes from "responsible", then swap the *e* for *i*. The word has "sponsib" in the middle: re-**spons**-ibility.

### Model paragraph (read this, then look at what it does)

> Priestley presents Mr Birling as a man who is arrogantly certain about things he cannot possibly know. In 1912, he declares the Titanic to be "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable", and the repetition of "unsinkable" makes him sound as though he thinks his opinion should settle the matter. Because Priestley wrote the play in 1945, his audience knows the Titanic sank in 1912, so they laugh at Birling and trust him less. This early mistake damages his credibility, so when he later insists that "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own", the audience is already prepared to reject his selfish view.

**Why this works** (annotate in a different colour):
- **Point:** the first sentence answers the question straight away.
- **Quotation:** short, built into the sentence.
- **Zoom in on language:** it picks out "unsinkable" and the repetition, and explains the effect.
- **Context:** it uses 1912 and 1945 to say why the audience reacts this way.
- **Link to writer's purpose:** the last sentence links to Priestley's message.

## Improvement task (15 minutes, next lesson)

Everyone rewrites **one paragraph** from their own essay, as follows:

- **Code A:** pick a paragraph that retells. Rewrite it so every sentence says what Priestley is *doing*, not what happens.
- **Code B:** take one quotation and write three sentences zooming in on a single word.
- **Code C:** add a sentence of context to the end of a paragraph, using the model as a guide.
- **Code D:** split your longest paragraph into two, and add a linking word to the second one.
- **Code E:** write each of the three words five times, then use all three in one sentence.
- **Code S:** rewrite your capitalism paragraph so it explains how Priestley makes us *reject* Birling's views.

**Check yourself:** tick off "I started with the point", "I zoomed in on a word", "I used 1912 or 1945", and "my paragraph covers one idea".

---

## For you: what to reteach to the whole class (10 minutes, before the task)

1. **Analysis versus retelling.** Model the move live. Take one sentence of a retell ("Birling says the ship is unsinkable") and, with the class, turn it into analysis. Put "Priestley presents..." on the board as a sentence starter.
2. **Quotation then zoom.** Give out three short quotations. In pairs, students choose one word each and say what it suggests. Cold-call two pairs.
3. **Context as a single sentence.** Many students avoid it because they think it needs a paragraph. Show that one sentence is enough: "In 1945, the audience knew..."
4. **Paragraph discipline.** One idea per paragraph. A quick practice: give them a long paragraph from the model and ask them where to split it.

Next time, give a tight **success checklist** with the question: "I made a point about Priestley's purpose, I used a quotation, I zoomed in on a word, I used one fact about 1912 or 1945."

## Assumptions and cautions

- I've only used quotations I'm certain of. If your edition's wording differs slightly, adjust the model.
- If some students don't know that the Titanic sank in 1912 and war followed in 1914, say it aloud before they start.
