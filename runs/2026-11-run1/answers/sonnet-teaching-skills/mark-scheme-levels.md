# GCSE English Literature: A Christmas Carol
## 20-mark extract-based question with levels-based mark scheme (AQA-style)

**Assumptions:** This follows the shape of the AQA Paper 1 Section B question (extract, then whole text), scaled from 30 to 20 marks. The assessment objectives are weighted in the same proportions as the real paper: AO1 8 marks, AO2 8 marks, AO3 4 marks. AO4 (spelling, punctuation and grammar) is not assessed here. The question is original. The extract is from Dickens's novella (1843), which is out of copyright.

---

## 1. The question

**Read the following extract from Stave Five of *A Christmas Carol* and then answer the question that follows.**

At this point in the story, Scrooge has woken on Christmas morning to find that he has been given a second chance.

> "I don't know what to do!" cried Scrooge, laughing and crying in the same breath; and making a perfect Laocoon of himself with his stockings. "I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel, I am as merry as a schoolboy. I am as giddy as a drunken man. A merry Christmas to everybody! A happy New Year to all the world. Hallo here! Whoop! Hallo!"

**Starting with this extract, explain how far you think Dickens presents Scrooge as a changed man.**

**Write about:**
- **how Dickens presents Scrooge in this extract**
- **how Dickens presents Scrooge in the novel as a whole.**

**[20 marks]**

*Suggested time: 30 minutes. Students do not have a copy of the text in the exam.*

### Placing an answer within a level
- **Top of the level:** the AO that is weakest is still secure at that level, and the response is sustained throughout.
- **Middle:** the AOs are broadly balanced at the level, with some inconsistency.
- **Bottom:** the level is reached in places only, or one AO is clearly weaker.

### AO-specific guidance for examiners
- **AO3 is worth 4 of the 20 marks.** An answer with no context cannot normally rise above Level 4, however strong the AO1 and AO2. Context must be relevant to the question. Bolt-on biography does not earn credit.
- **Extract only or whole text only:** if a response deals with only one half of the task, cap at Level 3 (top of the level at most), unless the extract treatment is explicitly used to anchor a genuinely novel-wide argument.
- **Retelling:** plot summary is not analysis. Credit only where it supports an explained point.
- **Quotations:** accept short, accurate quotations and fair paraphrase of memorised moments. Do not penalise small slips in a quotation if the sense and the reference are clear.
- **"How far":** a response that simply says "he changes completely" can reach Level 4. Levels 5 and 6 need a judgement that is tested (for example, weighing the change against what the novel shows of his earlier self).

## 4. Indicative content

*This content is not prescriptive. Credit any valid, well-supported interpretation.*

### A. The extract (Stave Five): AO1 and AO2

- **Series of similes.** "As light as a feather... as happy as an angel... as merry as a schoolboy... as giddy as a drunken man." The accumulation and the repeated "I am" create breathless, overflowing joy. The images move from physical lightness (feather) to spiritual (angel) to youth (schoolboy), suggesting release and rebirth.
- **Contrast with Stave One.** Scrooge was "hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire" and "secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster". The earlier similes are cold, hard, closed and mineral. These are light, warm, open and human.
- **Innocence regained.** "Schoolboy" echoes the lonely boy at school in Stave Two. His joy returns him to the childhood self he lost.
- **Paradox and emotional excess.** "Laughing and crying in the same breath" shows long-suppressed feeling released. The man who "froze" others now cannot control his emotion.
- **Allusion.** "A perfect Laocoon of himself with his stockings." The classical reference (a priest wrestling with serpents) is comic and undercuts the grandeur, so the change feels warm and human rather than solemn. It might also suggest a man still wrestling with old habits.
- **Exclamatory sentences, interjections ("Hallo here! Whoop! Hallo!"), and repetition.** A rhythm of joy that contrasts with the clipped, dismissive voice of "Bah! Humbug!".
- **Inclusive language.** "Everybody", "all the world". His concern has widened from a single counting-house to everyone, reversing his isolation.
- **"A merry Christmas".** Echoes Fred's greeting, which Scrooge rejected in Stave One. The phrase now belongs to him.
- **"Giddy as a drunken man."** Can be read as comic excess or loss of control. A strong answer may question whether the joy is stable.

### B. The novel as a whole: AO1 and AO2

**Stave One: the starting point**
- Scrooge is a "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner": a list of harsh verbs that rhythmically hammers home his greed.
- "The cold within him froze his old features": the cold motif shows emotional and moral distance from others.
- He refuses charity ("Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses?") and dismisses the poor ("decrease the surplus population").
- Marley's warning: "I wear the chain I forged in life." "Mankind was my business." Marley models the consequence and sets up the Spirits' task.

**Staves Two to Four: how the change is built (structure)**
- **Past (Stave Two):** the school-boy alone, Fan, Fezziwig's ball, Belle. Scrooge weeps and says "I wish... but it's too late now." These show he once had warmth, so the change is a recovery as well as a transformation.
- **Present (Stave Three):** the Cratchits' meagre but loving Christmas; Tiny Tim; the Ghost throws Scrooge's own words back at him; the children Ignorance and Want ("This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both"). Scrooge is shown that he is part of a social problem.
- **Yet to Come (Stave Four):** the silent, cloaked Spirit, the stolen belongings, the unmourned death and the grave. "Am I that man who lay upon the bed?" Fear and shame push him from reluctant observer to desperate participant ("Why show me this, if I am past all hope?").
- **Structure:** five staves, a carol-like pattern of verse and refrain; a linear structure of crisis, vision and resolution that is like a moral fable or parable.

**Stave Five: evidence of lasting change**
- Promises: "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year."
- Actions rather than words: the prize turkey sent to the Cratchits, the charitable donation, the raise for Bob, becoming "a second father" to Tiny Tim, who "did NOT die".
- The narrator's verdict: "He knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge." The closing "God bless Us, Every One!" passes the moral on to the reader.

### C. "How far" (AO1 evaluation)

Creditable lines of argument include:
- **Complete transformation.** From "solitary as an oyster" to giving to charity, joining Fred's party and being a "second father". The final stave stresses permanence.
- **Recovery, not invention.** The Spirits restore a warmth that already existed (Fan, Fezziwig, Belle), which suggests he was always capable of change and that society's attitudes helped to harden him.
- **Change as a moral duty.** Dickens presents Scrooge as a model: the reader is shown that change is possible and necessary.
- **A tested argument.** Some readers may note that the change relies on supernatural intervention and fear (the grave) and that the story is told as a fable, so Dickens may be less interested in psychological realism than in persuading readers. The sudden, extreme joy invites the question of whether this is the first stage of a lasting change.

### D. Context (AO3)

- **Poverty and welfare.** The Poor Law Amendment Act (1834) and workhouses, and the attitudes behind "Are there no prisons?" and "surplus population", which echo contemporary arguments associated with Malthus. Dickens satirises those who see poverty as the fault of the poor.
- **Child labour and the 1840s social conscience.** Reports on children's employment (early 1840s) and Dickens's anger at them. Ignorance and Want embody the dangers of leaving children uneducated and hungry. Dickens's own boyhood in the blacking factory may also be linked, if relevant to the point.
- **Christmas.** The Victorians revived Christmas as a festival of family, generosity and goodwill. The novella helped to shape that image.
- **Christian redemption and the morality tale.** Scrooge's journey (sin, vision, repentance, new life) follows a Christian pattern of redemption, and Dickens uses it to argue that individuals and society can change.
- **Publication.** Written in 1843 as a short, affordable book with a message for a wide readership. Dickens uses a popular form to reach readers who might not read a political pamphlet.
- **Social responsibility.** The idea that wealthy individuals have a duty to the poor, and that "Mankind" should be the "business" of every person.

## 5. Model answer extract: Level 6 (annotated)

*An extract of a full-mark response (approximately the first 70 per cent of an answer). Annotations in square brackets are for teachers and are not part of the answer.*

> In Stave Five, Dickens presents Scrooge as transformed so completely that his language itself has been rebuilt. In the extract, the accumulating similes, "as light as a feather... as happy as an angel... as merry as a schoolboy", reverse the imagery that defined him in Stave One, where he was "hard and sharp as flint". Where the earlier similes locked him into images that were cold, hard and solitary, these are all lightness and lift: the climb from "feather" to "angel" suggests a soul literally rising, while "schoolboy" returns him to the lonely boy the Ghost of Christmas Past forced him to watch. **[AO2: analysis of simile, progression and contrast with earlier imagery. AO1: a developing interpretation, with quotations integrated into the argument.]** Dickens is subtle, though. Scrooge is "laughing and crying in the same breath", which hints that this joy is also a release of feeling held back for decades, and the grand allusion to "a perfect Laocoon of himself with his stockings" is gently comic, as if the man still wrestles with a lifetime of habit. The change feels human rather than magical. The exclamations, "Hallo here! Whoop! Hallo!", abandon the clipped, dismissive voice of "Bah! Humbug!", and the shift to "everybody" and "all the world" widens his concern from his own counting-house to everyone, reversing the isolation of the man "solitary as an oyster". **[AO2: sentence form, interjection and inclusive language, with the effect of each explained.]**
>
> This is the climax of a transformation Dickens has built carefully through the structure of the novella. In Stave One, Scrooge's "Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses?" voices the attitude behind the Poor Law of 1834 that Dickens attacked in 1843, a belief that poverty was a private failing rather than a social responsibility. The Spirits dismantle that belief through shame and vision rather than argument. The Ghost of Christmas Present throws Scrooge's own phrase, "decrease the surplus population", back at him as he watches the Cratchits, and the revelation of Ignorance and Want, with the warning "Beware them both", shows him that the poverty he dismissed is a danger he has helped to create. **[AO3: context integrated into the argument about Dickens's purpose and the Spirits' methods. AO2: structural choice of the Spirits' sequence.]** By Stave Four, the stolen belongings and the unmourned grave turn his question into a plea, "Why show me this, if I am past all hope?", and Dickens makes the change visible through actions rather than promises: the turkey sent to the Cratchits, the raised salary, and becoming "a second father" to Tiny Tim, who "did NOT die".
>
> So how far is he changed? Largely, but Dickens implies that Scrooge is restored rather than remade. The Scrooge who wept for Fan and danced at Fezziwig's ball was always there beneath the "covetous old sinner", which suggests that the coldness was learnt, and that a society that teaches such coldness can unlearn it. The narrator's confident verdict that "he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge" presents the change as permanent, and the closing blessing, "God bless Us, Every One!", hands the lesson to the reader. **[AO1: a critical, conceptualised judgement on "how far"; the answer would continue to a brief conclusion.]**

**Why this is Level 6:** a sustained and tested judgement on "how far"; precise, short quotations woven into the argument; analysis of individual choices (simile progression, allusion, interjection, structure) with their effects explained; and context used to explain Dickens's purpose rather than added as a separate paragraph.

## 6. Common weaknesses (for feedback)

| What the student does | Level it tends to sit at | Feedback prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Retells the ghosts' visits in order | 2-3 | "What does each vision make Scrooge feel, and how does Dickens show it?" |
| Names techniques ("simile", "exclamation") without effect | 3 | "So what? What does the reader feel or understand because of this choice?" |
| Context as a separate paragraph ("In Victorian times...") | 3-4 | "Link this to a specific moment: why does Dickens make Scrooge say this?" |
| Deals only with the extract, or only with the novel | cap at 3 | "Zoom out or zoom in: the question asks for both." |
| States "he changes completely" without testing it | 4 | "Does the novel suggest he was ever different? What does that do to 'how far'?" |
