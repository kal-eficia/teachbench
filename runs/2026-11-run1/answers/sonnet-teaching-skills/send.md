# Adapting the "Charles I and the Civil War" extended answer

**Same destination for everyone:** all three students still write a source-supported, judged answer to the same question. What changes is the route (layout, chunking, scaffolds, predictability), not the challenge. Most of these changes also help the rest of the class, so I'd make the whole-class changes for everyone and add the individual ones quietly.

**Assumptions:** I haven't seen pages 42 to 44, so the adaptations are about structure and access, not the content of the sources. Each student has a support plan or SENCO advice I haven't seen; check these first, and swap anything below that conflicts.

---

## 1. Changes for the whole class (do these first)

The worksheet task is one long block with three hidden demands (read three sources, plan, write for 30 minutes), a figure of speech ("how far do you agree"), and an open end. Fix those and you've removed most barriers for all three students.

**Rewrite the task as a numbered sequence, with times:**

> **Question:** "Charles I was mainly to blame for the outbreak of the Civil War." How far do you agree?
>
> 1. **Read** Sources A, B and C (pages 42 to 44). (6 minutes)
> 2. **Plan** using the grid. (5 minutes)
> 3. **Write** three paragraphs, then a conclusion. (15 minutes)
> 4. **Check** against the checklist. (4 minutes)
>
> **You are finished when you have:** a paragraph about Charles, a paragraph about another cause, a paragraph that uses a source to argue against the statement, and a conclusion that answers the question.

**Explain "how far do you agree" in plain words** in a box: *"Decide how much of the blame was Charles's, and how much was other causes. Give reasons. 'Mainly' means 'more than half'."*

**Give a planning grid** (a small table, with boxes):

| | What I think | Evidence (source or my own knowledge) |
|---|---|---|
| Charles was to blame because... | | |
| Other causes (Parliament, religion, money, Scotland, Ireland...) | | |
| My judgement: how far do I agree? (circle) | Fully / Mostly / Partly / Not much | |

**Say what a good answer looks like** by showing one short model PEEL paragraph on a different, related question (for example, on Charles dissolving Parliament) before they start. Keep it visible while they write.

**Provide a PEEL prompt strip:** Point; Evidence; Explain; Link back to the question. Add one sentence starter for each.

**Use plain language.** Check that instructions are literal and not idiomatic. "Reach a judgement" becomes: "Finish by deciding how far you agree and say why."

## 2. The student with dyslexia

**Barrier:** reading three sources and writing a long answer in a limited time.

| Adaptation | How |
|---|---|
| **Reading** | Give the sources in advance (the lesson before, or at the start of the day) so they can be read twice. Print on cream or pastel paper in a sans-serif font, 12 to 14 pt, 1.5 line spacing, left-aligned. Offer the sources as audio (reader pen, text-to-speech, or a classmate or adult reading aloud). |
| **Highlighting** | Pre-highlight the key lines in each source in three colours: one for Charles, one for Parliament, one for other causes. Or let the student do this, which is also a useful thinking step. |
| **Vocabulary** | Provide a mini glossary with 6 to 8 key terms (Parliament, tax, Ship Money, Puritan, divine right, Covenanters...) and a spelling bank. Don't penalise spelling in this task unless it's the objective. |
| **Writing** | Allow typing, a scribe, or speech-to-text. A planning grid with boxes helps avoid long blank pages. Sentence starters: "One reason Charles was to blame is... This is shown by Source A, which says... This matters because..." |
| **Time** | Extra time (up to 25 percent, or as per the support plan), or fewer paragraphs. Do not cut the judgement; shorten elsewhere. |
| **Marking** | Assess historical thinking: argument, evidence and judgement. |

## 3. The student with ADHD

**Barrier:** sustained attention across a 30-minute open task, starting and finishing.

| Adaptation | How |
|---|---|
| **Chunk the time** | Use the four timed steps above on a visible timer on the board. Give the student a personal checklist to tick off. |
| **Clear start** | Tell them exactly what the first action is: "Read Source A. Underline one sentence about Charles." Check in 1 minute after starting. |
| **Active reading** | A task for each source ("circle the word that shows who the writer blames") keeps them engaged. |
| **Movement** | Allow a legitimate break between steps (hand out materials, bring work to the front for a check). A seat near the front, away from distractions. |
| **Fewer words, more important questions** | Keep the plan grid short. Lower the word count target but keep the three paragraphs and conclusion. |
| **Rewards and feedback** | Quick, specific praise at each checkpoint: "You've finished the plan. Now start paragraph 1." |
| **Finish** | A short checklist at the end ("Does my conclusion answer the question?") to avoid stopping early. |

## 4. The autistic student who is anxious about open-ended tasks

**Barrier:** "write an extended answer" with no end point, and "how far do you agree" with no single right answer.

The key is **predictability and a clear finish line.** Take away the unknowns and the task is still demanding.

| Adaptation | How |
|---|---|
| **Warn in advance** | Tell the student the day before (and show the worksheet) what will happen: "Tomorrow you'll read three sources, then plan, then write." Offer a preview of the worksheet. |
| **Show the structure** | Provide the four-step timed plan on a card at the desk. The student can see what is coming next and when it ends. |
| **Make the end concrete** | "You are finished when you have these four parts." Say how many paragraphs, roughly how many lines, and which parts are needed. |
| **Define "mainly to blame" and "how far"** | Provide a **scale** to place the judgement on (Fully / Mostly / Partly / Not much), and explain that there is **no single right answer**: the mark is for giving reasons, not for choosing the "correct" side. |
| **Offer a worked answer** | Show a complete short model answer (one paragraph) first, so they can see what a finished one looks like. |
| **Reduce choice** | Give a fixed line of argument as a starting point ("Start with Charles. Then write about another cause."). If they want to argue a different line, they can ask. |
| **Literal language** | Avoid idioms and figures of speech. Say exactly what you want. |
| **Anxiety plan** | Agree a signal or card for "I'm stuck" so they don't have to speak in front of the class. Tell them who they can go to. A quiet place to work if needed. |
| **Consistent adult** | Check in at the end of step 1 and step 2, quietly, saying "you're on track". |

---

## 5. Keep the challenge, then fade the support

- **Same question, same mark scheme.** No student is given an easier question.
- **Fade the scaffolds over a few weeks:** first sentence starters go, then the plan grid becomes optional, then the checklist is theirs to make.
- **Share what worked.** Ask each student afterwards, privately, what helped, and record it for the next task and for their support plan.
